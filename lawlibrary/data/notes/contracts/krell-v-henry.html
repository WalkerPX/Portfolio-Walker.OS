This is the origin of frustration of purpose.

Coverage

Summarized in Knapp's introductory text (not reprinted). Details marked [Outside knowledge] are not in your materials.

Overview

The source of the frustration of purpose doctrine. Performance was still possible on both sides, but the value of the exchange to one party was destroyed by a supervening event.

Parties

  • Plaintiff: Krell, the owner of the flat on Pall Mall [Outside knowledge: location]. He wanted the balance of the rent.
  • Defendant: Henry, who agreed to pay for use of the rooms to watch the coronation procession. He refused to pay the balance. [Outside knowledge: he had paid a £25 deposit on a £75 price.]

Facts

  • From Knapp: the defendant agreed to pay for the use of a room overlooking the route of the coronation procession of King Edward VII.
  • The king's sudden illness forced cancellation (postponement) of the coronation, making the room useless to the defendant for that purpose.
  • [Outside knowledge]: the written agreement did not mention the coronation. It was for the use of the rooms on two days.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The trial judge ruled for the defendant. The Court of Appeal affirmed.

Issue

Is a party excused from paying when a supervening event destroys the purpose of the contract, though performance remains literally possible?

Holding

Yes. The defendant was excused from his duty to pay.

Reasoning

  • From Knapp: the court cited and relied on Taylor v. Caldwell, but the case is generally not viewed as true impossibility, since both promises could literally be performed. It is explained as frustration of purpose: the exchange "had lost all value to the defendant, because of a supervening change in extrinsic circumstances."
  • [Outside knowledge]: the court found the coronation procession was the foundation of the contract, recognized by both parties (shown by the surrounding circumstances, including the high price for a short use).
  • Doctrinal overlap (Knapp): if the illness had already occurred, unknown to both, at signing, the case could be analyzed as mutual mistake.

Rule

A party's remaining duties are discharged where, after contracting, its principal purpose is substantially frustrated without its fault by an event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption of the contract (Restatement (Second) § 265; First Restatement § 288).

Why It Matters

  • It is the origin of frustration. Note the distinction: frustration concerns the value received, while impracticability concerns the cost or possibility of performance (slides).
  • Knapp: case law actually applying frustration remains sparse. American courts rarely grant it, and an empirical study found few successful cases. Compare Mel Frank Tool v. Di-Chem, Hemlock Semiconductor v. Solarworld and Lloyd v. Murphy, where frustration failed.
