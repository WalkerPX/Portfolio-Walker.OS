Quick reference

Citation: 867 F.3d 692 (6th Cir. 2017) · Casebook: Knapp p. 771 · Topic: Impracticability and Frustration One line: A German buyer under long-term, fixed-price take-or-pay polysilicon contracts was not excused when Chinese subsidies crashed market prices. Market stability was not a basic assumption, and the buyer assumed that risk.

Overview

The course's principal case on impracticability and frustration in a commercial setting. A drastic market price change does not excuse performance of a fixed-price contract, because allocating market risk is the very point of fixing the price.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Hemlock Semiconductor Operations, LLC (Michigan). The seller of polysilicon. It wanted to enforce the contracts (including the liquidated damages, take-or-pay amounts).
  • Defendant / appellant: SolarWorld Industries Sachsen GmbH (Germany). The buyer, which refused to buy and argued impracticability and frustration.

Facts

  • The parties signed a series of long-term supply agreements (LTAs): Hemlock would supply set quantities of polysilicon at fixed prices from 2006 to 2019 (slides: 14-year contracts).
  • Take or pay: Sachsen had to pay for the stated quantities whether or not it took delivery.
  • At first the market price was well above the contract price. Several years in, China heavily subsidized its domestic polysilicon production, and the market price plummeted far below the contract price.
  • Sachsen refused to buy. Hemlock sued.

Procedural History

  • The district court entered judgment for Hemlock (rejecting Sachsen's defenses). [Outside knowledge: on summary judgment.]
  • Sixth Circuit: affirmed.

Issue

Was Sachsen excused from its fixed-price take-or-pay obligations by impracticability (UCC § 2-615 / Restatement § 261) or frustration of purpose (§ 265), because government-subsidized market changes made the contracts unprofitable?

Holding

No. Neither impracticability nor frustration excuses Sachsen.

Reasoning

  • Impracticability:
    • Continuation of market conditions is not a basic assumption of a long-term fixed-price contract. "The very reason parties fix prices is to allocate the risk of market fluctuations" (slides).
    • Sachsen could have negotiated for terms excusing it in a drastic market change. It did not, and so accepted the risk.
    • Increased cost or decreased profitability, even severe, is not impracticability.
  • Frustration: the primary purpose of the contract was to supply polysilicon at a fixed price. That purpose was not frustrated, since Hemlock stood ready to deliver. Sachsen's loss of profitability is a reduction in value, not destruction of the principal purpose.

Rule

A party is not excused from a fixed-price contract by a market price shift, even a drastic one caused by foreign government action, because market risk is the risk a fixed price allocates. Impracticability requires a contingency whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption, and frustration requires the principal purpose to be substantially frustrated.

Why It Matters

  • Impracticability elements (slides; § 261 / UCC § 2-615): (1) performance became impracticable; (2) because of a contingency; (3) whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption; (4) without fault of the party seeking excuse; (5) no assumption of the risk.
  • Frustration elements (§ 265): (1) principal purpose substantially frustrated; (2) by a contingency; (3) whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption; (4) no fault; (5) no assumption of risk.
  • Note 1: the elements overlap heavily, and it is hard to win either, because the basic assumption is difficult to establish and the threshold of impracticability or frustration is high.
  • Note 3, foreseeability: foreseeability does not automatically bar relief (§ 261 cmt. c: the "mere possibility" that a party might have contracted against a contingency is not enough). But the more foreseeable the event, the harder it is to show its non-occurrence was a basic assumption.
  • Note 6: courts have generally not excused performance even for COVID-19.

Other Key Points

  • Hypo 1, "The Solar Contract" (slides): GreenPower must supply at $80/kg, a new US process drops industry costs to $15/kg, and GreenPower still produces at $75/kg. Analyze the elements, whether the cause matters (technology vs. government), the lack of a force majeure clause, and a variation where costs rise to $200/kg from a regulation banning the input chemical. That last variation looks more like impracticability by governmental action (§ 264).
