Quick reference

Citation: 824 S.W.2d 545 (Tenn. 1992) · Casebook: Knapp p. 576 · Topic: Capacity › Minors One line: A 16-year-old who disaffirmed his purchase of a used truck could recover the price, but the seller may set off the truck's depreciation and use, absent overreaching or fraud by the seller.

Overview

The course case on minority (infancy). A minor's contracts are voidable. On disaffirmance, the traditional rule gives the minor a full refund on returning whatever remains. Dodson adopts a "benefit" or "use and depreciation" rule to protect fair-dealing sellers.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Joseph Eugene Dodson, age 16 at purchase. He wanted to disaffirm and get back his full $4,900.
  • Defendants / appellants: Burns and Mary Shrader, owners of Shrader's Auto Sales, Columbia, Tennessee. They wanted to offset the truck's lost value.

Facts

  • April 1987: Dodson paid $4,900 cash (borrowed from his girlfriend's grandmother) for a used 1984 pickup. The Shraders didn't ask his age, and he didn't misrepresent it. Mr. Shrader thought he was 18 or 19.
  • December 1987: the truck developed mechanical problems (likely a burnt valve). Dodson kept driving it without repairs, and the engine eventually "blew up."
  • He parked it at his parents' home, tendered it back and demanded a refund. The Shraders refused. A hit-and-run driver then damaged the parked truck. At the time of suit its value was minimal ([Outside knowledge: about $500]).

Procedural History

  • The trial court ordered rescission and a full refund of $4,900. The Court of Appeals affirmed. [Outside knowledge on the intermediate result.]
  • Tennessee Supreme Court: reversed and remanded to determine the fair market value at tender and the allocation of the hit-and-run damage. (One justice dissented.)

Issue

When a minor disaffirms a contract to buy goods, must the seller refund the full price, or may the seller recover compensation for the goods' depreciation and use while in the minor's possession?

Holding

Where the minor has not been overreached (no fraud or unfair advantage by the seller) and the contract was fair, the minor's recovery of the purchase price is subject to a deduction for the minor's use of the goods and their depreciation while in the minor's possession. Remanded to determine the amount.

Reasoning

  • The traditional rule (full refund on return of whatever is left) protects minors from improvident bargains, but can be abused by older minors in modern commerce, at the expense of honest merchants.
  • Adopts the "benefit" rule (following other jurisdictions): the minor gets back the price minus the value of the use and depreciation, if the seller dealt fairly.
  • The protection of minority remains a shield, not a sword.
  • Open fact questions: the truck's fair market value when tendered, and responsibility for the hit-and-run damage.

Rule

A minor may disaffirm a contract (it is voidable). On disaffirmance the minor must return any consideration still in hand. Under Dodson, if the seller dealt fairly, without overreaching or fraud, the seller may recover reasonable compensation for depreciation and use of the goods.

Why It Matters

  • The tension in the rule (slides): minority incapacity exists because minors are presumed to have poor judgment. Requiring compensation for depreciation "deprives minors of much of the protection" disaffirmance is supposed to give.
  • Note 1: avoidance is often used simply to resist enforcement of an executory obligation (paying).
  • Note 3, two qualifications: (a) minors are liable for the reasonable value of necessaries (in restitution, not on the contract); (b) misrepresentation of age or willful destruction of goods may limit disaffirmance (courts split).
  • Note 4, prevailing rule: the minor is bound unless he disaffirms within a reasonable time after reaching majority (voidable, not void).

Other Key Points

  • Hypo 1, "Used Truck" (slides): a 17-year-old buys a $5,000 truck now worth $2,000. Apply the traditional rule vs. Dodson.
  • Compare mental incapacity: Sparrow v. Demonico (Restatement § 15(2) reliance protection, which traditionally doesn't apply to minors).
