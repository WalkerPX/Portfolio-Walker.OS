The short version

A contract is a promise (or set of promises) that the law will back up. Contract law decides which promises count, what the words mean, and what happens when someone doesn't keep their word.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Foundations of Contract Law.

What it's about

Everyone makes promises. Most of them ("I'll call you back") are not things a court will ever enforce. Contract law is the set of rules that sorts the promises the legal system will stand behind from the ones it won't, and then decides what to do when a backed-up promise is broken.

The course follows a contract through its life:

  1. Was a deal made? Did both sides really agree? (Making the deal)
  2. Is it the kind of promise the law enforces? Usually that means each side gave something. (Consideration)
  3. Is there a reason to get out of it? For example lies, threats, serious mistakes or very unfair terms.
  4. Did someone fail to do what they promised? (Breach)
  5. What does the court give the other side? Usually money. (Damages)

Where the rules come from

  • Court decisions ("common law"): most contract rules were built up by judges, case by case.
  • The UCC (Uniform Commercial Code), Article 2: a statute nearly every state adopted for sales of goods (things you can move, like cars, grain or machines). See UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation (Plain English).
  • The Restatement (Second) of Contracts: a respected summary of the common law written by legal experts. It isn't law by itself, but courts cite it constantly. When you see "§ 90" or "§ 351," that's usually the Restatement.

An everyday example

You agree to pay a painter $2,000 to paint your house next week. That is a contract: a promise exchanged for a promise. If the painter never shows up, contract law doesn't punish the painter. It tries to put you where you would have been if the painter had kept the promise, usually by making them pay the extra cost of hiring someone else.

Big ideas that come back all semester

  • What you said and did counts, not what you secretly meant (the "objective theory").
  • Freedom of contract: adults can generally make whatever deals they want, and courts don't ask whether a deal was a good one.
  • Compensation, not punishment: contract remedies aim to make the injured side whole.

The cases, in plain words

  • Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute: fine print on a cruise ticket said lawsuits must be filed in Florida, and the Supreme Court enforced it. Fine print can bind you.
  • Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon: even when a contract doesn't spell out a duty, a court may read in a sensible one (here, a duty to make reasonable efforts).
  • Leibel v. Raynor: a business relationship with no end date can be ended, but with reasonable notice.

Words you'll hear

  • Breach: failing to do what the contract requires.
  • Enforceable: a court will back it up.
  • Remedy: what the court gives the injured side.
