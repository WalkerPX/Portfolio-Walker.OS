Quick reference

Citation: 227 N.Y. 200, 125 N.E. 94 (1919) (Cardozo, J.) · Casebook: Knapp p. 102 · Topic: Consideration One line: An aunt's $3,000 note to her eight-year-old nephew, given out of affection and marked "value received," was an unenforceable promise to make a gift.

Overview

The standard case on gratuitous promises. Love, affection and a desire to reward good behavior are motive, not consideration. A printed recital of "value received" does not create consideration where the facts show none.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / respondent: Charley Dougherty, age 8, suing through his guardian. He wanted payment of the $3,000 note.
  • Defendant / appellant: Salt, executor of the estate of the aunt ("Tillie"). He refused to pay, arguing no consideration.

Facts

  • While visiting, the aunt praised Charley ("Isn't he a nice boy?"). She said she loved him and wanted to "take care of him now" rather than "take it out in talk."
  • The guardian filled out a printed promissory note form containing the words "value received," for $3,000 payable at her death or before. She signed it.
  • She handed it to Charley: "You have always done for me, and I have signed this note for you. Now, do not lose it. Some day it will be valuable."
  • She died without paying. The executor refused.

Procedural History

  • The trial judge submitted consideration to the jury, which found for the plaintiff. The judge then set aside the verdict and dismissed the complaint.
  • The Appellate Division, divided, reversed and reinstated the verdict (treating the note as given for consideration).
  • The Court of Appeals reversed the Appellate Division. [Outside knowledge: the dismissal was reinstated. Knapp's excerpt ends "Judgment accordingly."]

Issue

Is a promissory note given by an aunt to her nephew out of affection, reciting "value received," supported by consideration?

Holding

No. The note was a promise to make a future gift, unsupported by consideration, and unenforceable.

Reasoning

  • The guardian's own testimony showed how the note came about. The aunt was not paying a debt, and the boy was not a creditor or dealt with as one. "The plaintiff, through his own witness, has explained the genesis of the promise, and consideration has been disproved."
  • Recital: a note "so given is not made for 'value received,' however its maker may have labeled it." The printed formula is "a mere erroneous conclusion" that cannot overcome the facts.
  • "You have always done for me" describes past kindness and affection, which is motive or past consideration, not a bargained-for exchange.
  • A gift requires delivery. A promise to give in the future is unenforceable without consideration.

Rule

A promise motivated by affection or gratitude, without a bargained-for exchange, is an unenforceable gratuitous promise. A recital of consideration ("value received") does not establish consideration when the facts show none.

Why It Matters

  • It establishes the line between gift promises and bargains.
  • Restatement links (slides): § 218 (a recital doesn't by itself establish consideration); § 81 (motive is distinct from consideration; love and affection are motive). Also, "being a good boy" would likely be too indefinite to be consideration on its own.
  • Note 2: should the law enforce purely donative promises? Commentators disagree. That debate leads into Promissory Estoppel and promissory restitution.

Other Key Points

  • Contrast Hamer v. Sidway: the nephew gave up specific legal rights at the uncle's request, which is consideration.
  • Hypo (slides): "Michael tells Erin, 'You've been the best receptionist I've ever had. I'm giving you $500 next month.'" Under Dougherty, that is a gift promise (past performance and motive).
  • Compare Berryman v. Kmoch (a recital of $10 paid for an option, but never actually paid).
