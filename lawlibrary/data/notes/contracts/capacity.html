In one paragraph

Defenses let a party avoid a contract that was otherwise properly formed. They work as a shield against enforcement or a sword (rescission plus restitution). Two groups have limited capacity. Minors' contracts are voidable at the minor's option. A minor may disaffirm before majority or within a reasonable time after, but ratifies by keeping the benefits. Courts increasingly require the minor to account for depreciation (Dodson v. Shrader). Mentally incapacitated persons: a contract made after a court has declared someone incompetent is void. Otherwise it is voidable under a cognitive test (could not understand the transaction) or a volitional test (could not act reasonably, and the other side had reason to know) (Sparrow v. Demonico, Ortelere v. Teachers Retirement Board).

Where capacity fits

Defenses rest on three grounds (existing note):

  1. Status / incapacity: this note.
  2. Defects in bargaining: Duress and Undue Influence, Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure.
  3. Substance of the bargain: Unconscionability and public policy.

Minors

  • Voidable, not void. Until the age of majority (generally 18), the minor may disaffirm, before majority or within a reasonable time after.
  • Ratification: doing nothing for a reasonable time after turning 18, or keeping and using the benefits, ratifies the contract.
  • Restitution on disaffirmance:
    • Traditional rule: the minor returns whatever is left of the goods and recovers the full price, even if the goods were damaged or used up.
    • Dodson v. Shrader: a 16-year-old bought a used truck for $4,900 cash. The engine failed, and he disaffirmed. Rule: the seller is entitled to reasonable compensation for depreciation and loss of value while the goods were in the minor's hands, if the seller did not overreach, defraud or take unfair advantage. The tension: the rule takes away much of the protection disaffirmance is meant to give.
  • Necessaries: a minor is liable in restitution for the reasonable value of necessaries (food, shelter, medical care), not the contract price (Dodson Note 3).
  • Misrepresentation of age or willful destruction: may make the minor liable. Courts split on whether lying about age forfeits the right to disaffirm.
  • Reliance: traditionally disregarded for minors (Note 2).

Mental incapacity

  • Adjudicated incompetent → contract void.
  • Not adjudicated → voidable (Restatement (Second) § 15(1)) if, because of mental illness or defect, the person:
    • (a) Cognitive test: cannot reasonably understand the nature and consequences of the transaction; or
    • (b) Volitional test: cannot act reasonably in relation to the transaction, and the other party has reason to know of the condition.
  • Protection for the other party (§ 15(2)): if the contract was on fair terms and the other party did not know of the incapacity, avoidance may be limited where it would be unjust, for example where there was reliance or performance.
  • Sparrow v. Demonico: Susan Demonico sought to avoid a mediated settlement, claiming incapacity at the time. The court required expert medical evidence; lay testimony alone was not enough.
  • Ortelere v. Teachers Retirement Board (note case): a teacher with depression chose a maximum-allowance retirement option, with no survivor benefit, shortly before she died. The court adopted the volitional test, and the board had reason to know of her condition. [Outside knowledge] the facts are supplied from general knowledge; see the brief.
  • Intoxication [Outside knowledge]: treated like mental incapacity where the other party has reason to know (Restatement § 16).
  • Compare restitution where capacity is contested: Credit Bureau Enterprises v. Pelo.

Exam framework

  1. Who lacks capacity, and why? Age, or mental condition (adjudicated or not).
  2. Void or voidable?
  3. Minor: timely disaffirmance? Ratification? Necessaries? Misrepresentation of age?
  4. Restitution: the traditional rule vs. Dodson depreciation. Was there overreaching by the seller?
  5. Mental: cognitive test, then volitional test (did the other party have reason to know?). What evidence is there (Sparrow: medical)? § 15(2) protection for fair, good-faith deals.

Hypos (slides)

  • Hypo 1, "The Used Truck": a 17-year-old pays $5,000 cash for a pickup, and the dealer believed the buyer was an adult. Six months later the engine fails, possibly from a pre-existing defect. The minor disaffirms; the truck is now worth $2,000. Under the traditional rule, a full refund on returning the truck. Under Dodson, $5,000 minus depreciation, unless the dealer overreached. A pre-existing defect makes overreaching and fault arguments relevant.
