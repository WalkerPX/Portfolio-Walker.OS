Quick reference

Citation: 15 U.S. (2 Wheat.) 178 (1817) · Casebook: Knapp pp. 650-651 (Hill v. Jones Note 1) · Topic: Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure › Nondisclosure One line: A tobacco buyer who knew, before the seller, that the War of 1812 had ended (which would raise prices) had no duty to disclose it. This is the classic statement of the classical no-duty rule.

Coverage

Summarized in Knapp's Hill Note 1. The opinion is not reprinted. Details marked [Outside knowledge] are not in your materials.

Overview

The leading nineteenth-century example of the classical rule: in an arm's-length transaction, a party has no duty to disclose information, even information that dramatically affects price, so long as it says or does nothing to deceive.

Parties

  • Buyer (Organ), who learned privately of the Treaty of Ghent ending the War of 1812.
  • Seller (Laidlaw & Co.), a tobacco merchant who did not know. [Outside knowledge: party roles. Organ sued for the tobacco after Laidlaw took it back.]

Facts

  • From Knapp: the buyer knew the war was over, while the seller did not (slides).
  • [Outside knowledge]: Organ learned early on the morning of February 19, 1815 that the treaty had been signed. When the seller's agent asked whether there was any news calculated to enhance the price, Organ said nothing about it and bought 111 hogsheads of tobacco. When the news became public, tobacco prices jumped 30-50%. The seller repossessed the tobacco. Organ sued.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The trial court instructed the jury to find for Organ. The Supreme Court (Marshall, C.J.) reversed and ordered a new trial, because the question whether Organ had been asked and responded deceptively should have gone to the jury.

Issue

Is a buyer obliged to disclose extrinsic information, equally accessible in principle to both parties, that would affect the price?

Holding

No duty to disclose such information. But a party may not say or do anything to impose upon the other (active deception), which was a jury question.

Reasoning

[Outside knowledge] Chief Justice Marshall reasoned that it would be difficult to circumscribe a duty to disclose when the means of intelligence are equally accessible to both parties. Each party must take care not to say or do anything tending to deceive.

Rule

(Classical) In an arm's-length transaction, there is no general duty to disclose material information, absent a fiduciary relationship, active concealment or misrepresentation.

Why It Matters

  • It is the classical baseline that Hill v. Jones and Restatement (Second) § 161 depart from.
  • It is a policy debate: rewarding the acquisition of information (efficiency) vs. good faith and fair dealing.
