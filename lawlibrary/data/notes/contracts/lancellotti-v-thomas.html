Quick reference

Citation: 341 Pa. Super. 1, 491 A.2d 117 (1985) · Casebook: Knapp p. 1044 · Topic: Reliance and Restitution Damages › Restitution for the breaching party One line: A buyer who defaulted on the purchase of a hoagie business may recover his payments to the extent they exceed the harm his breach caused. Pennsylvania adopts Restatement § 374 and rejects the old forfeiture rule.

Overview

The course case on restitution for a breaching party. The traditional rule barred a breaching party from recovering anything for part performance. The modern rule (Restatement (Second) § 374) allows recovery of the benefit conferred minus the harm caused by the breach.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Lancellotti. He bought the defendants' luncheonette (hoagie) business, then defaulted. He wanted his $25,000 purchase price back.
  • Defendants / appellees: the Thomases, the sellers and landlords. They counterclaimed for damages.

Facts

  • Lancellotti bought the Thomases' business for $25,000 and agreed to rent the building for 5 years and to build a 16' × 16' addition costing at least $15,000.
  • He didn't build the addition. The Thomases built a 20' × 40' addition themselves for $11,000.
  • After about a year, Lancellotti no longer wanted to run the business. He stopped paying rent, vacated, and opened his own competing restaurant.
  • The Thomases resumed operations and found some equipment missing.

Procedural History

  • Lancellotti sued for the $25,000. The Thomases counterclaimed for $52,000 ($6,635 unpaid rent plus $45,335 for damage to the business and Mrs. Thomas's emotional distress). [Outside knowledge: the trial court denied restitution under the traditional rule.]
  • Superior Court: rejected the common-law rule, adopted § 374, and remanded for the trial court to apply it. A dissent would have denied restitution for bad faith.

Issue

Can a defaulting buyer of a business recover any part of the payments he made before defaulting?

Holding

Yes, potentially. Pennsylvania adopts Restatement (Second) § 374. A breaching party may recover in restitution the benefit conferred in excess of the loss caused by its breach. The case is remanded to apply this rule.

Reasoning

  • The common-law rule caused a forfeiture of the breaching buyer's payments and unjustly enriched the non-breaching seller. The penalty grew with the amount of part performance, which is perverse.
  • § 374 balances both interests. The breaching party avoids forfeiture, and the non-breaching party is fully compensated first (its damages are deducted).
  • UCC § 2-718(2)-(3) gives a similar right to defaulting buyers of goods.

Rule

A party who has breached may recover restitution for any benefit conferred by its part performance in excess of the loss its breach caused the other party (Restatement (Second) § 374(1)), unless a valid liquidated damages clause controls (§ 374(2)).

Why It Matters

  • Note 2: the modern rule is fairer to both sides. It avoids forfeiture without ignoring the non-breaching party, who reduces the recovery by its damages.
  • Note 3, dissent: suggests bad faith (he learned the hoagie business from the defendants, then competed). Like an intentional deviation, bad faith may be a reason to deny restitution.
  • It contrasts with US ex rel Coastal Steel v. Algernon Blair (restitution for the non-breaching party).
