Quick reference

Citation: 934 P.2d 1313 (Alaska 1997) · Casebook: Knapp p. 360 · Topics: Statute of Frauds › Reliance and promissory estoppel, Promissory Estoppel One line: A promised two-year job, which would otherwise be barred by the one-year provision, could be enforced through promissory estoppel (Restatement § 139) because the employee relied by moving to Alaska.

Overview

The course case on using promissory estoppel to overcome the statute of frauds. The court adopts Restatement (Second) § 139: detrimental reliance can make an oral promise enforceable despite the statute, without the fraud-like conduct the First Restatement era required.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Kathleen Rice. A former executive director of the Party (1987-1991, fired in 1991), then working for the Maryland Democratic Party. She wanted damages after the promised job did not materialize.
  • Defendants / appellants: Alaska Democratic Party and Greg Wakefield, the chair-elect (elected May 1992, term starting February 1993) who allegedly offered her the job.

Facts

  • While Rice was in Maryland, Wakefield contacted her about his candidacy and her serving as his executive director.
  • After his election (May 1992), he allegedly offered her a two-year position as executive director, starting with his term. [Outside knowledge: the salary was about $50,000/year plus benefits.]
  • In reliance, Rice quit her Maryland job and moved to Alaska. The Party's leadership resisted. Other party officers refused to approve, and the job never materialized.

Procedural History

  • Jury trial: verdict for Rice on contract and promissory estoppel. The superior court reduced the award to $28,864 (lost wages and benefits), removing a misrepresentation item.
  • Supreme Court of Alaska: affirmed.

Issue

  1. Can promissory estoppel make enforceable an oral promise that the statute of frauds would otherwise bar (a two-year employment contract)?
  2. Was Rice's reliance reasonable given questions about Wakefield's authority?

Holding

  1. Yes. Alaska follows Restatement (Second) § 139. Detrimental reliance can make an oral promise enforceable notwithstanding the statute of frauds, to the extent needed to avoid injustice.
  2. Reasonableness of reliance, including the promisor's apparent authority, was a jury question, resolved for Rice.

Reasoning

  • One-year provision: a two-year contract cannot be performed within a year, so it is within the statute.
  • First Restatement era approach: estoppel against the statute required something like fraud (e.g., a promise to put the agreement in writing, or misrepresentation that a writing wasn't needed).
  • Restatement (Second) § 139 requires only that the promisor reasonably expect to induce reliance, the promisee's action or forbearance is induced, and injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. The court adopts it.
  • § 139(2) factors: (a) availability and adequacy of other remedies, especially restitution; (b) the definite and substantial character of the reliance in relation to the remedy sought; (c) the extent to which the reliance corroborates the making and terms of the promise, or they are otherwise established by clear and convincing evidence; (d) the reasonableness of the reliance; (e) the extent to which it was foreseeable by the promisor.
  • Reliance must be reasonable, including as to the authority of the person promising (slides). The jury could find Rice reasonably believed Wakefield could hire her.

Rule

A promise that the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance, and which does induce it, is enforceable notwithstanding the statute of frauds if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. The remedy may be limited as justice requires (Restatement (Second) § 139).

Why It Matters

  • It shows promissory estoppel as a way around the statute of frauds, alongside part performance (Beaver v. Brumlow) and the UCC exceptions (Buffaloe v. Hart).
  • It shows authority and reasonableness issues in reliance claims.
  • It shows reliance-based recovery of lost wages, which approaches expectation.

Other Key Points

  • Hypo 2 (slides): Maria and a "West Coast Sales Manager" promise. Did he have authority? Was reliance reasonable? Apply the § 139(2) factors.
  • One-year rule reminders (slides): if performance is possible within a year, however unlikely, the contract is outside the statute. Termination by breach or excuse doesn't count. Lifetime employment is traditionally outside the statute, though some newer cases treat it as a term of years.
