Quick reference

Citation: 129 Cal. App. 2d 179, 276 P.2d 8 (1954) · Casebook: Knapp p. 35 · Topic: Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance One line: Letters answering a buyer's questions about land and urging him to "decide fast" were preliminary negotiations, not an offer, so there was nothing to accept.

Overview

The course's main case on what counts as an offer. A communication is not an offer if the recipient knows or has reason to know the sender does not intend to be bound until he gives some further expression of assent (Restatement (First) § 25; Restatement (Second) § 26).

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Lonergan. A prospective buyer who answered a newspaper ad. He wanted specific performance of a sale of 40 acres for $2,500, or damages of $3,581 (he claimed the land was worth $6,081).
  • Defendant / respondent: Scolnick. A New York landowner who advertised the land. He sold it to a third party and denied any contract with the plaintiff.

Facts

  • March 1952: the defendant advertised in a Los Angeles paper: "Joshua Tree vic. 40 acres, ... need cash, will sacrifice."
  • March 26 letter: described the property and how to get there, stated a "rock-bottom price" of $2,500 cash, and said "This is a form letter."
  • April 7: the plaintiff asked for the legal description, whether the land was level, and suggested a bank as escrow agent "should I desire to purchase."
  • April 8 letter: the defendant confirmed the plaintiff had found the property, said the bank was "O.K. for escrow agent," and gave the legal description. He added: "If you are really interested, you will have to decide fast, as I expect to have a buyer in the next week or so."
  • April 12: the defendant sold to a third party for $2,500.
  • April 14: the plaintiff received the April 8 letter. April 15: he wrote that he would open escrow "in conformity with your offer." April 17: he opened escrow with $100.

Procedural History

  • The issue of whether a contract existed was tried first, on an agreed statement of facts.
  • The trial court found the April 8 letter was an offer conditioned on prompt acceptance, and that the plaintiff's week-long delay was not prompt. Judgment for the defendant.
  • The plaintiff appealed, arguing he accepted within a reasonable time and that his mailed acceptance of April 15 formed a contract.

Issue

Did the defendant's ad and letters constitute an offer that the plaintiff could accept, or only preliminary negotiations?

Holding

No offer was made. Affirmed, though on a different ground than the trial court. The ad was a mere request for offers, and the letters were preliminary negotiations, so no contract was formed.

Reasoning

  • Restatement (First) § 25: if the recipient knows or has reason to know the sender does not intend the communication as "an expression of his fixed purpose until he has given a further expression of assent," it is not an offer.
  • The ad was "a mere request for an offer."
  • March 26 letter: no definite offer, and it expressly called itself a form letter. It only gave particulars.
  • April 8 letter: it answered questions and said "act fast." The statement that he expected a buyer "in the next week or so" showed he intended to sell to the first comer and reserved the right to do so. The plaintiff knew or should have known that further assent from the defendant was required.
  • So the plaintiff was not given a power to accept within a reasonable time.

Rule

A communication is an offer only if it would lead a reasonable person in the recipient's position to understand that assent alone will conclude the bargain. Statements that invite offers, give information, or reserve the right to deal with others are preliminary negotiations. Advertisements are generally invitations to receive offers.

Why It Matters

  • It establishes the offer / preliminary negotiation line, which is the first step of any formation analysis.
  • Slide policy point: the result protects sellers who deal with many prospects at once (first come, first served), and it avoids binding a seller to several buyers.
  • It sets up the mailbox rule and timing questions (Note 3) and the advertisement rule (Note 2).

Other Key Points

  • Note 1: the words used are relevant but not decisive. Even "offer" may not be an offer (Moulton v. Kershaw). See Restatement (Second) §§ 24, 26.
  • Note 2, ads: the general rule is that ads are not offers (§ 26 cmt. b). An ad can be an offer if it uses language of commitment and invites action without further communication (Lefkowitz v. Great Minneapolis Surplus Store, "first come, first served"). "Bait and switch" ads may be treated as offers or attacked under consumer statutes (Izadi v. Machado Ford).
  • Note 3, alternative ground, the mailbox rule: offers and revocations are effective on receipt, and acceptance on dispatch (Restatement (Second) §§ 30, 63, 65, 66). The rule does not apply if the offeror requires receipt, because the offeror is "master of the offer" (§ 29 cmt. a). Note that the April 12 sale, unknown to the plaintiff, would not itself revoke an offer. Indirect revocation requires the offeree to learn of it (compare Normile v. Miller).
  • Note 4, CISG: Art. 16(1) bars revocation after acceptance is dispatched, but under Art. 18(2) acceptance is effective only when it reaches the offeror. So the risk of loss falls on the offeree.
