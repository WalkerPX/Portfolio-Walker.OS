Quick reference

Citation: 420 Mass. 52, 647 N.E.2d 1196 (1995) · Casebook: Knapp p. 225 · Topic: Promissory Estoppel › Charitable subscriptions One line: A jury could find that Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr.'s 1964 letter promised his papers to Boston University as an enforceable charitable pledge, supported by consideration or reliance.

Overview

The course case on charitable subscriptions (pledges). Courts have long strained to enforce them, through consideration, reliance, or Restatement (Second) § 90(2), under which charitable subscriptions are binding without proof of reliance.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Coretta Scott King, as administratrix of Dr. King's estate and individually. She sued BU for conversion, claiming the estate, not BU, owned the papers.
  • Defendant / appellee: Trustees of Boston University (BU). BU had housed the papers in its library's special collection since 1964. It claimed title by charitable pledge.

Facts

  • 1963: BU began expanding its library's special collections and sought Dr. King's papers (he had earned his doctorate at BU). [Outside knowledge: the BU doctorate.]
  • July 1964: at Dr. King's request, some of his papers were delivered to BU.
  • July 16, 1964 letter, signed by Dr. King: it named BU the repository of his correspondence, manuscripts and other papers, and said that from time to time he would give additional papers, that title would vest in BU, and "in the event of my death, all such materials deposited with the University shall become from that date the absolute property of Boston University." The papers would stay in BU's custody. [Paraphrased; the excerpt quotes the letter in the opinion.]
  • BU relied by undertaking care, storage and indexing of the papers (slides).

Procedural History

  • Jury trial on contract, charitable pledge, statute of limitations and laches. By special verdict, the jury found the letter was not a contract, but that Dr. King promised absolute title to BU, enforceable as a charitable pledge supported by consideration or reliance.
  • The trial judge denied the plaintiff's motion for JNOV or a new trial. The SJC granted direct appellate review.
  • Affirmed.

Issue

Was there sufficient evidence for the jury to find that Dr. King's letter was an enforceable charitable pledge of his papers to BU?

Holding

Yes. The letter could be read to contain a promise supported by consideration or reliance. The issue was properly submitted to the jury, and its verdict stands.

Reasoning

  • Promise: the letter's language (title vests in BU, the papers become BU's absolute property at death) supports a finding of a promise to give, not merely a bailment or deposit.
  • Consideration: BU's undertaking to care for, index and make the papers available to researchers could be consideration. Massachusetts precedent treats a charitable institution's acts in reliance on a pledge as consideration (citing older subscription cases).
  • Reliance: BU indexed and cared for the papers for decades, which is substantial reliance.
  • § 90(2) (slides): charitable subscriptions may be binding without proof of reliance. Even under § 90(2), courts factor in reliance, but the burden may shift to the defendant to prove its absence.
  • Standard of review: the question is not whether the court agrees with the verdict but whether the case was properly submitted to the jury.

Rule

A charitable pledge is enforceable if supported by consideration (including the charity's undertaking of obligations) or reliance. Under Restatement (Second) § 90(2), a charitable subscription is binding under § 90 without proof that it induced action or forbearance.

Why It Matters

  • It shows the special treatment of charitable subscriptions (public policy favoring charitable giving).
  • Note 2 (slides): § 90(2) eliminates the need to prove reliance, but courts are unlikely to ignore reliance entirely.
  • It shows how promissory estoppel and consideration overlap: the same facts (BU's care of the papers) support both theories.

Other Key Points

  • The jury's split (not a contract, but an enforceable pledge) shows that charitable pledges can be enforced on a different footing than ordinary bargains.
  • Related: Kirksey v. Kirksey (reliance without consideration, classical denial); Promissory Estoppel.
