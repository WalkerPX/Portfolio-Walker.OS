The short version

When parties disagree about what contract words mean, courts look for the meaning a reasonable person would give them. They differ on how much outside evidence (emails, conversations, industry practice) to consider.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Interpretation.

What it's about

Contracts are written in words, and words can be read more than one way. Interpretation is how courts decide what the contract actually means.

Two approaches

  • Plain meaning: if the words are clear on their face, the court enforces them as written and doesn't look at outside evidence.
  • Context first (the "Corbin" approach): the judge first looks at the surrounding circumstances (negotiations, purpose, how the parties acted) to see whether the words could reasonably mean what a party claims. Only then does the court decide what they mean.

Helpful tie-breakers courts use

  • Read the contract as a whole, and give every part a meaning.
  • Specific terms beat general ones.
  • How the parties acted under this contract (course of performance) matters most, then how they dealt in past deals (course of dealing), then industry custom (usage of trade).
  • Construe against the drafter: if a term is truly unclear, read it against the side that wrote it.

An everyday example

A contract says you'll be paid "per ton." You think a short ton (2,000 lbs); the buyer thinks a metric ton (about 2,205 lbs). A court will look at the industry, past deals and what each side knew. If each side reasonably meant something different and neither knew the other's meaning, there may be no contract at all.

The cases, in plain words

  • Taylor v. State Farm: Arizona follows the context-first approach. The judge can consider outside evidence to decide whether a release could mean what one side says.
  • Shay v. Aldrich: a divided Michigan court allowed outside evidence to show a release wasn't meant to cover certain people, over a dissent that insisted on the plain words.
  • Thompson v. Libby: the classical, plain-meaning view. A writing that looks complete is treated as complete.
  • Lucy v. Zehmer and Ray v. Eurice Bros: meaning is judged objectively, from the outside.

Words you'll hear

  • Ambiguous: reasonably open to more than one meaning.
  • Extrinsic evidence: evidence from outside the written document.
  • Contra proferentem: Latin for "against the drafter."

Closely tied to the parol evidence rule, which decides what outside evidence a court may consider at all.
