The short version

Even without a trade, a promise can be enforced if the other person reasonably relied on it and would be treated unfairly if the promise were broken.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Promissory Estoppel.

What it's about

Sometimes a promise has no consideration, but someone reasonably changed their life because of it: moved, quit a job, gave up a lease. Promissory estoppel lets a court enforce that promise to avoid injustice. The main source is Restatement (Second) § 90.

How a court decides (plain checklist)

  1. A promise was made.
  2. The promisor should reasonably expect the promise to cause the other person to act (or hold off acting).
  3. The person actually and reasonably relied on it.
  4. Injustice can be avoided only by enforcing the promise.

The court may enforce it only as far as needed. Often that means paying back what the person lost by relying, not the full value of the promise.

Special case: charitable pledges

Under § 90(2), a promise to give to charity can be enforced without proof that the charity relied on it.

An everyday example

Your grandmother says, "Quit your job and go to nursing school. I'll pay your tuition." You quit and enroll. She then refuses to pay. You gave her nothing in exchange, but you relied on her promise in a way she expected. A court could make her cover the tuition.

The cases, in plain words

  • Kirksey v. Kirksey: the old view. A widow moved 60 miles on a promise of a home and got nothing. Modern courts would likely enforce it under § 90.
  • Drennan v. Star Paving: a contractor that relied on a subcontractor's bid could hold the sub to it.
  • Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice: a promised two-year job that normally needed a writing was enforced because the employee moved to Alaska relying on it (see Statute of Frauds (Plain English)).
  • Pop's Cones v. Resorts International: a tenant who gave up its old lease because a casino kept saying "the deal is done" could recover.
  • King v. Trustees of Boston University: Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr.'s letter about his papers could be an enforceable charitable pledge.
  • Walser v. Toyota Motor Sales: would-be car dealers won on reliance, but got only their out-of-pocket losses, not the profits they hoped to make.
  • James Baird v. Gimbel Bros and Plowman v. Indian Refining: reliance arguments that failed.

Words you'll hear

  • Estoppel: a legal bar that stops someone from going back on their word.
  • Reliance: acting, or not acting, because you trusted the promise.
  • Reliance damages: money to undo what you lost by relying (see Reliance and Restitution Damages (Plain English)).
