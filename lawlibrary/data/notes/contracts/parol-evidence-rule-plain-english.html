The short version

If the parties wrote down their final deal, side agreements made before or while signing generally can't be used to change it. How strictly that applies depends on whether the writing was meant to be complete.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Parol Evidence Rule.

What it's about

"Parol" means oral, though the rule also covers earlier written drafts and emails. The parol evidence rule protects final written contracts. If the parties put their deal in a writing they intended as final, a court won't let one side add or contradict it with things said or agreed before or at the time of signing.

It does not cover:

  • agreements made after signing (later changes to the deal are a separate topic), or
  • evidence used to show the contract is invalid, for example because it was induced by fraud, duress or mistake.

Two questions courts ask

  1. Is the writing final ("integrated")?
    • Fully integrated: meant to be the complete deal. You can't add or contradict.
    • Partially integrated: final on what it covers but not complete. You can add consistent extra terms but can't contradict.
    • A merger clause ("This is the entire agreement…") is strong evidence of full integration.
  2. What does the evidence do? Explain an unclear term (allowed), add a term, or contradict a term?

An everyday example

You buy a used car. The signed contract says "sold as is," with an "entire agreement" clause. The salesperson had told you, "If the transmission goes in the first month, we'll fix it." Under the parol evidence rule, that oral promise probably can't be enforced, because it contradicts "as is" and the writing claims to be complete.

The cases, in plain words

  • Thompson v. Libby: a short written log sale that looked complete shut out evidence of an oral promise about the logs' quality.
  • Taylor v. State Farm: the modern view. A judge may look at outside evidence first to decide whether the words could mean what a party claims.
  • Shay v. Aldrich: outside evidence was allowed to show who a release was meant to cover.
  • Sherrodd v. Morrison-Knudsen: a subcontractor said it was misled about how much dirt it would have to move, but the evidence directly contradicted the fixed-price writing, so the majority barred it. The dissent strongly disagreed.

Words you'll hear

  • Integration / integrated: the writing is the final deal.
  • Merger clause: a term saying the writing is the entire agreement.
  • Extrinsic evidence: evidence from outside the document.
