Big picture

  • "Common law" = rules built from cases. Rules alone are often not enough.
  • Reasonable lawyers and judges disagree. Skill target: "think like a lawyer."
  • Class method: apply rules to hypothetical fact situations (bar-exam style, exam style).
  • Contract = agreement between two or more persons as to something to be done in the future by one or more of them, which the law will enforce in some way.
  • Every contract has at least one promise — a commitment to future action.
  • Contracts may be written or oral. Some must be in writing (Statute of Frauds) — but not most.
  • The usual contract is enforceable. Some kinds of agreements are not fully enforceable.
  • Much of contract law is about which promises courts will recognize.

Doctrine outline

Sources of contract law

  • Judicial opinions.
  • Statutory law: Statute of Frauds; UCC Article 2 (goods).
  • Restatements: summaries of common-law rules. Persuasive only — not binding, no force of law. Includes Restatement of Employment Law and the newly adopted Restatement of the Law of Consumer Contracts.
  • Legal commentary: secondary authority, not binding.

Contract theory

  • Formalists: contract law is universal rules distilled from decided cases; no moral or political values used.
  • Realists: general standards ("good faith," "unconscionability") over mechanical rules.
  • Economic approach: "efficiency." Rules tend to efficient outcomes; inefficient rules should move toward efficiency. Efficiency = low "transaction costs" + resources allocated to most highly valued uses.
  • Relational contract theory: contracts embedded in ongoing relationships, not just discrete exchanges.
  • Critical perspectives: rules are never neutral; they distribute advantage between drafting and non-drafting parties.

Lawyering roles

  • Counselor, negotiator, drafter, advocate.

Tests and elements

  • Working definition checklist (apply to any fact pattern):
    • Agreement between two or more persons?
    • As to something to be done in the future?
    • By one or more of them?
    • Which the law will enforce in some way?
  • Minimum content: at least one promise (commitment to future action).
  • Form: written or oral — writing required only where Statute of Frauds applies (not most contracts).
  • NOTE: a contract may rest largely on conduct and implicit understandings, with only minimal express terms.

Examples and hypos

  • Problem 1-1(A): contracts in your last week before class. Answers: apartment lease; purchase agreements for books, furniture, electronics; airfare; moving truck; student loan agreement.
  • Q1 — Sale of stock: Amalgamated Rubber buys 35% (700,000 shares) of Fargo Petroleum common stock for $85 million. Commercial contract — stock is a property interest in the corporation. Ask: nature of obligations between stockholders and corporate officers — some sound in contract, others in statute or judicial precedent.
  • Q2 — Employment/union: Grant hired as driver; Teamsters is bargaining representative; Grant pays dues. Three relationships: (1) employment contract — services for pay and benefits; (2) union–employer contract governing their relations; (3) employee–union relationship (dues, services), heavily regulated, in part by the NLRA.
  • Q3 — Dry cleaner's ticket: overcoat left, ticket with serial number, "Friday." Likely a contract based largely on conduct and implicit understandings with minimal express terms. Open question: where do the rest of the terms come from?
  • Q4 — Online purchase: free Facebook account under Terms and Conditions allowing use of "likes" in marketing. Online transactions readily treated as binding contracts. Further: electronic/layered contracting, Ch. 2 § D.
  • Q5 — Family dinner promise: promise to take family to dinner is not itself an enforceable contract. But there is a contract between family and restaurant (food for money; implied warranty of merchantability, UCC § 2-314 — what if a bone in the burger?). Separate contract between Kris and credit card company for extension of credit.
  • Q6 — Promise in a will: will leaves 3/4 to Maria, 1/4 to John; father had said he'd leave everything equally. Execution and validity of a will is state probate law, generally outside contract law. Probate statutes/case law often require such promises to be in writing or otherwise clearly established — courts reluctant to override a testator's right to freely revoke.
  • Q7 — Ski trip: Aurooj drives, Jennifer pays gas and brings snacks, hotel split evenly. Close call — casual agreement between friends seems a doubtful basis for an enforceable contract, but detailed plans and financial commitments cut the other way; debatable. Compare: prom date canceled after financial/emotional commitments — quid pro quo exists, but doesn't feel like something courts should enforce.

Exam angles

  • Apply the four-part definition to every hypo: agreement, future action, parties, legal enforceability.
  • Separate the contracts inside one fact pattern (Q2: three relationships; Q5: dinner promise vs. restaurant contract vs. credit-card contract).
  • Ask whether the promise is the kind courts will enforce at all (family/social promises: Q5, Q7).
  • Check subject-matter boundaries: probate law for wills (Q6); statute/precedent for some stockholder–officer duties (Q1).
  • Spot when conduct and implicit terms suffice (Q3).
  • Know which source is binding vs. persuasive (Restatements and commentary are not binding).
  • Expect disagreement — "close call" and "debatable" are signals to argue both sides.
