Quick reference

Citation: 66 Mich. 568, 33 N.W. 919 (1887) · Casebook: discussed in Lenawee County Board of Health v. Messerly (Knapp pp. 747-749) · Topic: Mistake › Mutual mistake One line: The famous "barren cow" case. A cow sold as barren turned out to be pregnant, and the seller could rescind because the mistake went to "the very nature of the thing," not merely its quality.

Coverage

Knapp quotes and discusses Sherwood inside Lenawee and its Notes. The opinion is not reprinted separately. Details beyond Knapp are [Outside knowledge].

Overview

The classic "quality vs. nature" (substance) test for mutual mistake, which Lenawee rejects as unworkable in favor of the Restatement's risk-allocation approach.

Parties

  • Plaintiff: Sherwood, the buyer, who wanted the cow delivered at the agreed price. [Outside knowledge: a banker. He sued in replevin.]
  • Defendants: Walker (the Walker brothers, cattle breeders), the sellers, who refused delivery after discovering the cow was pregnant.

Facts

  • From Knapp: the parties agreed to the sale of a cow both believed to be barren, but she was with calf. When the seller discovered this, he refused to deliver her.
  • Quoted in Knapp: "If the cow was a breeder, she was worth at least $750; if barren, she was worth not over" a fraction of that. [Outside knowledge: about $80. The price was about $80, at five and a half cents per pound. The cow's name was "Rose 2d of Aberlone."]

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The trial court ruled for the buyer (Sherwood). The Michigan Supreme Court reversed and granted a new trial, permitting rescission. Justice Sherwood dissented (Knapp p. 747 cites "Sherwood, J., dissenting").

Issue

May a seller avoid a sale when both parties were mistaken about whether the cow could breed?

Holding

Yes. The mistake "went to the whole substance of the agreement," and rescission was permitted.

Reasoning

  • Quoted in Knapp: "the mistake or misapprehension of the parties went to the whole substance of the agreement." A barren cow is "substantially a different creature" from a breeding one.
  • A mistake about mere quality or value would not justify rescission. A mistake about the nature or substance of the thing would.
  • Dissent (Sherwood, J.) [Outside knowledge, partially reflected in Knapp p. 747 cite]: the buyer believed she might breed and was in effect taking a chance, so there was no mutual mistake.

Rule

(Classical, now largely rejected) Mutual mistake justifies rescission when it concerns the substance or nature of the subject matter, but not when it concerns only its quality or value.

Why It Matters

  • Lenawee Note 2 (slides): the "quality vs. nature" distinction is unworkable. The Restatement's focus on basic assumption, material effect and risk allocation (§§ 152, 154) is more precise.
  • Gartner v. Eikill relied on Sherwood in granting rescission.
  • Under § 154, one might ask: did the buyer consciously accept the risk of uncertainty about fertility?
