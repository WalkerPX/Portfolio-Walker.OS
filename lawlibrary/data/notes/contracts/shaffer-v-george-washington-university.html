Quick reference

Citation: 27 F.4th 754 (D.C. Cir. Mar. 8, 2022) · Casebook: Knapp p. 1053 · Topic: Reliance and Restitution Damages › Restitution when the contract doesn't cover the issue One line: Students who paid for in-person education before COVID-19 forced classes online stated claims for breach of an implied-in-fact contract and for restitution (unjust enrichment), but not for breach of an express contract.

Overview

A pandemic-era case that brings together express contract, implied-in-fact contract, restitution, and (on remand) impracticability. It shows when restitution is available alongside a contractual relationship.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs / appellants: students and parents at George Washington University (GW) and American University (two consolidated cases). They wanted partial refunds of tuition and fees after the switch to online learning.
  • Defendants / appellees: GW and American University. They argued no promise of in-person instruction, and relied on reservation-of-rights clauses.

Facts

  • March 2020: COVID-19 hit. Both universities moved from in-person to online instruction and largely shut down campus activities and services.
  • Students had paid tuition and fees expecting in-person instruction and on-campus services. The universities' communications touted on-campus instruction, and both charged higher tuition for on-campus programs than for virtual ones.
  • Catalogs contained "reservation of rights" provisions allowing changes.

Procedural History

  • District courts: dismissed the complaints.
  • D.C. Circuit: affirmed in part and reversed in part, and remanded.

Issue

Did the students state claims for (1) breach of an express contract, (2) breach of an implied-in-fact contract, and (3) restitution or unjust enrichment, based on the shift to online instruction?

Holding

  1. Express contract: No. Nothing in the universities' published materials made in-person instruction and on-campus services an explicit term.
  2. Implied-in-fact contract: Yes, the claim survives. The universities' communications and tuition differential plausibly showed an implied promise of in-person instruction, and the reservation-of-rights clauses did not, as a matter of law, allocate pandemic risk to students.
  3. Restitution: Yes, the claim survives. Whether the universities were unjustly enriched is for the district court.
  • Remand includes whether the universities' obligations were discharged by impracticability.

Reasoning

  • Implied-in-fact contracts arise from conduct and circumstances: the universities marketed on-campus life and charged a premium for it.
  • Restitution is unavailable when a contract specifically covers the issue, but available when the contract does not cover it, or is invalid or unenforceable. Here the contract did not address payment for services that could not be provided on campus because of the pandemic.

Rule

A plaintiff may plead implied-in-fact contract and restitution in the alternative. Restitution lies where the parties' contract does not address the issue (or is unenforceable) and the defendant would otherwise be unjustly enriched. A general reservation-of-rights clause does not necessarily allocate the risk of an extraordinary event.

Why It Matters

  • Note 1: restitutionary claims when a contract is enforceable vs. unjust enrichment claims when the contract is invalid. Knapp p. 1063 has a chart comparing the Restatement (Second) of Contracts and the Restatement (Third) of Restitution.
  • Note 2, damages on remand:
    • If the implied contract was not discharged: tuition damages are tuition for the rest of the semester minus the value of virtual instruction. For non-tuition services (gym, meals), there are no damages until the closure orders took effect, and lost value after.
    • If discharged by impracticability: the question becomes unjust enrichment. For tuition, tuition for the rest of the semester minus the cost of providing virtual instruction. For non-tuition fees, a pro rata share (days from closure to semester end ÷ semester days).
  • Note 3: a reliance or restitution recovery for items like students' apartment leases has some authority, but it is unclear whether justice requires it.
  • Note 4, drafting: service providers should include terms allowing flexibility in delivering services without refunds.
