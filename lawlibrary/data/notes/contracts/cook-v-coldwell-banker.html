Quick reference

Citation: 967 S.W.2d 654 (Mo. Ct. App. 1998) · Casebook: Knapp p. 50 · Topic: Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance › Unilateral contracts One line: Once an agent had substantially performed under her broker's bonus offer, the broker could not change the terms to require her to stay until a March banquet.

Overview

The course's main unilateral contract case. An offer that invites acceptance by performance cannot be revoked once the offeree has substantially performed (Restatement (First) § 45). Restatement (Second) § 45 creates an option contract once performance begins.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / respondent: Mary Ellen Cook. A licensed real estate agent working as an independent contractor for the firm. She wanted the year-end bonus she had earned.
  • Defendant / appellant: Coldwell Banker/Frank Laiben Realty Co. (co-owner Frank Laiben). A brokerage that used the bonus to retain agents. It refused to pay after Cook left.

Facts

  • March 1991: at a sales meeting Laiben orally announced a bonus program to stay competitive and retain agents: $500 at $15,000 in commissions (paid immediately), 22% between $15,000 and $25,000, and 30% above $25,000. Bonuses over $500 were payable at year-end. The program year was calendar 1991.
  • Cook passed $15,000 by April (got the $500 in September) and $32,400 by September, qualifying for the 30% tier.
  • September 1991: Laiben announced bonuses would instead be paid at a March 1992 banquet, and that an agent had to be there in March to collect. Cook stayed through 1991, she testified, in reliance on the bonus.
  • January 1992: she joined Remax. The firm refused the bonus: over $17,000, more than half of it earned after the September change (slides).

Procedural History

  • Jury verdict for Cook: $24,748.89 (bonus plus prejudgment interest, per slides).
  • The firm appealed, arguing Cook had not accepted before the offer was modified (revoked), and raising instruction, evidence and argument errors.
  • Affirmed.

Issue

Can an offeror modify (or revoke) an offer for a unilateral contract after the offeree has substantially performed the requested acts?

Holding

No. By September 1991 Cook had substantially performed. The offer could no longer be revoked or modified, and she made a submissible case for breach of a unilateral contract.

Reasoning

  • The bonus program was an offer for a unilateral contract: a promise in exchange for performance (selling and staying through the year), not a return promise.
  • Classical rule: a unilateral offer is revocable until performance is complete. The modern rule (Williston, Corbin, Restatement (First) § 45 and comment b, via Missouri's Coffman case) is that substantial performance makes the offer irrevocable. Merely acting in justifiable reliance may also make a promise binding.
  • The evidence: an offer of a year-end bonus for continued work, Cook stayed with the intent to accept, she qualified for all three levels, the firm knew and paid $500, but not the rest. That was enough.

Rule

When an offer invites acceptance by performance, once the offeree begins (Restatement (Second) § 45) or substantially completes (Restatement (First), as applied here) the requested performance, the offeror may not revoke or change the terms. The offeror's duty is conditional on completion of performance.

Why It Matters

  • It applies the § 45 "option contract" fix to the classic unilateral-contract problem: under pure offer-and-acceptance rules, an offeror could revoke even when the offeree was nearly done.
  • Restatement (Second) drops the label "unilateral contract" and speaks of an offer that invites acceptance only by performance. "Same idea, different label" (slides).
  • The § 45 implied-promise device reappears by analogy in Drennan v. Star Paving.

Other Key Points

  • Employment and bonus settings: watch whether the employer is changing the rules mid-performance.
  • Problem 2-2 (GOC gas rewards): customers who bought all 100 gallons have essentially completed performance, while a one-gallon buyer is a much harder case. "Substantial performance" is doing a lot of work (slides).
  • Compare Hamer v. Sidway variation (b): revocation during performance, classical vs. § 45.
