App. 2008) · Casebook: Knapp p. 899 · Topic: Expectation Damages › Sale of real estate One line: When buyers breached a contract to buy commercial real estate, the seller recovered the contract price minus market value at the time of breach. A resale about a year later by highly motivated sellers was substantial evidence of that market value.

Overview

The course illustration of the expectation damages formula for a seller of real estate when the buyer breaches: contract price minus market value at the time of breach (plus incidental and consequential damages, minus costs saved).

Parties

  • Plaintiff / respondent: Crabby's, Inc., the seller (owner of the Crabby's Seafood Bar & Grill property in Joplin, Missouri, per the photo caption). It wanted damages for the buyers' failure to close.
  • Defendants / appellants: Paragon Ventures, L.L.C. and Hamilton ("Buyers"). They refused to close, then argued they had not waived a financing contingency and that damages were miscalculated.

Facts

  • The buyers contracted to buy the seller's real estate and refused to close.
  • They claimed a financing contingency excused them. The trial court found the contingency had been waived.
  • The seller later resold the property, just under a year after the breach, at a lower price. As "highly motivated sellers," they sold for what they could get.

Procedural History

  • Bench trial: judgment for the seller on breach, awarding damages based on the contract price minus the resale price.
  • Court of Appeals: affirmed.

Issue

Did the trial court properly measure the seller's damages by the difference between the contract price and the later resale price, used as evidence of market value at the time of breach?

Holding

Yes. The resale price, from a sale made by highly motivated sellers just under a year after the breach, was substantial evidence of fair market value at the time of breach. Damages were properly calculated. (The financing contingency had been waived.)

Reasoning

  • Measure for the seller: contract price minus fair market value at the time of breach.
  • Market value is usually proved by expert appraisal testimony (Note 2). But a later resale in an arm's-length transaction, reasonably close in time, may also show market value.
  • The debatable point (slides) is whether a resale nearly a year later reflects value at the time of breach. The court had precedent supporting its conclusion.

Rule

A seller whose buyer breaches a contract to buy land may recover the difference between the contract price and the fair market value at the time of breach, plus incidental and consequential damages (limited by foreseeability, certainty and mitigation). A reasonably timely resale may be evidence of market value.

Why It Matters

  • Restatement § 347 mapping (slides): contract price minus market value = loss in value. Consequential or incidental damages = other loss. If the seller had rented the building before the resale, that rent would offset damages (cost or loss avoided).
  • Book formula: ED = loss in value + other loss − cost avoided − loss avoided.
  • Note 1: a non-breaching seller has direct damages only if the contract price > market value. A non-breaching buyer has direct damages only if the contract price < market value.
  • Note 5: consequential and incidental damages are limited by foreseeability, certainty and mitigation (Limits on Damages).

Other Key Points

  • Formula drills (slides, p. 898): builder/owner: 130,000 + 0 − 85,000 − 10,000 = 35,000. employee/employer: 75,000 + 1,000 − 45,000 = 31,000. (The slides do not explain why the employee example subtracts a full year's salary as loss avoided, so check the facts on p. 898.)
