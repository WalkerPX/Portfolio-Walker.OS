This case does not appear in the casebook PDF in your Reading folder. The slides mention it only in the Mel Frank Note 1 comparison ("Compare Lloyd v. Murphy..."). Everything below other than the slide's one-sentence summary is [Outside knowledge] and should be verified.

Overview

A classic Traynor opinion rejecting frustration of purpose where government regulation reduced, but did not destroy, the value of a lease.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs: Lloyd et al., the lessors. They wanted the lease honored and rent paid. [Outside knowledge]
  • Defendant: Murphy, the lessee, a car dealer. He repudiated the lease, arguing frustration. [Outside knowledge]

Facts

  • Slides: wartime prohibitions on new car sales frustrated a car dealer's lease, but the tenant could still use the property for other commercial purposes.
  • [Outside knowledge]: the 1941 lease of a Los Angeles lot restricted use to displaying and selling new cars (with limited exceptions). After Pearl Harbor, the federal government restricted new car sales. The lessors offered to waive the use restriction and reduce rent. Murphy vacated anyway.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The trial court ruled for the lessors. The California Supreme Court affirmed.

Issue

Was the lessee's performance excused by frustration of purpose when wartime regulation restricted the sale of new cars?

Holding

No. The claim failed (slides).

Reasoning

  • Slides: the tenant could still use the property for other commercial purposes.
  • [Outside knowledge]: Justice Traynor reasoned that the risk of war-related restrictions was foreseeable in 1941 and not guarded against. The value of the lease was not totally destroyed: the lessee could still sell used cars and new cars under quotas, and the lessors offered to relax the use restriction.

Rule

Frustration of purpose requires that the frustrating event be unforeseeable (or not assumed) and that the value of the counter-performance be totally or nearly totally destroyed. Partial loss of value or a foreseeable risk is not enough.

Why It Matters

  • It is the comparison case for Mel Frank Tool v. Di-Chem (a serviceable use remains, so there is no frustration).
  • It shows the high threshold for frustration, even for governmental action.
