Overview

The course case on limits on contractual discretion. Even an express right to reject (a "satisfaction"-type discretion) must be exercised in good faith. A party may not use discretion to deny the other the benefit of the bargain.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Sondra Locke. An actor and director. She wanted damages for breach of contract (the implied covenant) and fraud.
  • Defendant / respondent: Warner Bros., Inc. The film studio, which had the right to accept or reject her proposals. It wanted summary judgment based on that express discretion.
  • Background figure: Clint Eastwood, Locke's former partner, who arranged the deal.

Facts

  • Locke sued Eastwood after their relationship ended. As part of the settlement, Eastwood arranged a development deal for Locke with Warner.
  • The deal: a pay or play fee to direct, plus a development deal under which Warner would consider her film proposals, with the express right to accept or reject them. [Outside knowledge: $1.5 million over three years for directing, plus $250,000 per year for development.]
  • Secretly, Eastwood agreed to reimburse Warner for the costs if Locke's projects weren't made ([Outside knowledge]). There was evidence Warner never intended to accept any proposal: it rejected everything she submitted.

Procedural History

  • The trial court granted summary judgment for Warner.
  • Court of Appeal: reversed as to the second (breach of the implied covenant) and fourth (fraud) causes of action, and remanded.

Issue

Is a studio's express contractual right to reject a director's proposals limited by the implied covenant of good faith, so that a refusal to consider them in good faith is a breach?

Holding

Yes. The express right to accept or reject is subject to the implied duty of good faith. Because there was evidence Warner categorically refused to work with Locke regardless of her proposals' merits, there was a triable issue on breach of contract and on fraud.

Reasoning

  • Where a contract gives one party discretion over the other's interests, the covenant requires that the discretion be exercised in good faith, not arbitrarily or as a pretext.
  • Warner's position, that it could reject everything for any reason, would make its promise to consider her proposals illusory and deny her the benefit of the bargain.
  • Warner did not have to like her proposals. It had to evaluate them honestly. Evidence suggested it did not (the Eastwood reimbursement arrangement and the blanket rejection).

Rule

An express grant of discretion is limited by the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. The discretion must be exercised honestly (subjective good faith) and not to frustrate the other party's reasonable expectations under the contract.

Why It Matters

  • It is the leading example of Seidenberg category 3: limiting discretion.
  • Note 1: two kinds of good faith. Subjective means honesty in fact. Objective means reasonable commercial standards. Locke seems to require only subjective honesty: Warner must have genuinely considered the proposals, not rubber-stamped rejections.
  • Note 2: if trade practice gives studios absolute creative discretion, the bad-faith claim may fail, but a fraud claim based on the Eastwood agreement may survive independently.
  • Note 3: would discrimination (gender, race, disability) breach good faith? Burton and Williams disagree.
  • Key limit: good faith constrains how discretionary rights are exercised. It does not create obligations the contract doesn't impose.
