Overview

Commercial restitution in a three-party setting. A subcontractor who has no contract with the owner seeks quantum meruit from the owner after the general contractor fails to pay. The owner is "unjustly" enriched only if it got the work without paying for it.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Equity Contracting Co. The stucco and surfacing subcontractor, owed $17,100. It wanted recovery directly from the owner.
  • Defendant / appellant: Commerce Partnership 8098 LP. The office-building owner. It said it had paid the general contractor in full.
  • Non-party: World Properties, Inc., the general contractor that did not pay Equity.

Facts

  • Commerce hired World Properties to improve its building. Equity subcontracted with World for stucco work worth $17,100 and fully performed. Commerce inspected weekly and knew of Equity's work.
  • World did not pay Equity. Equity's president testified he expected payment only from the general contractor.
  • Equity alleged Commerce had not paid the GC in full. Commerce answered that it had. Equity presented its direct case in under 30 minutes.

Procedural History

  • Non-jury trial: judgment for Equity on quantum meruit.
  • District Court of Appeal (en banc): reversed and remanded (for judgment for Commerce).

Issue

When may a subcontractor recover from a property owner in unjust enrichment (quantum meruit) for work done under a contract with the general contractor?

Holding

Only if the owner has not given consideration to anyone for the improvements. Equity failed to prove Commerce had not paid the general contractor, so it cannot recover.

Reasoning

  • "Quantum meruit" here is really a contract implied in law (unjust enrichment), not implied in fact. Equity never expected Commerce to pay, so there was no implied-in-fact contract.
  • Elements of unjust enrichment: (1) benefit conferred; (2) the defendant's knowledge and appreciation of it; (3) acceptance and retention under circumstances making it inequitable to keep without paying its value.
  • Two-element test for subcontractors (slides): (1) the sub must have exhausted all remedies against the GC and remain unpaid; (2) the owner must not have given consideration to any person for the improvements.
  • If the owner paid the GC, it is not unjustly enriched. Making it pay twice would be unjust. The burden was on Equity, and it did not carry it.

Rule

A subcontractor may recover from an owner in unjust enrichment only if it has exhausted remedies against the general contractor and the owner has not paid anyone for the improvements. The measure is the reasonable value of the services (market-based).

Why It Matters

  • It shows unjust enrichment requires more than a benefit. Retaining it must be unjust, and paying for it once is not unjust.
  • Note 1: the owner that hasn't paid is enriched, but may lose defenses against the GC. A solution is to allow recovery subject to the owner's claims and defenses against the GC. Restatement (Third) § 25(1) endorses broad liability when a benefit is conferred under a contract with someone else.
  • Note 2: claims against lessors (when a lessee fails to pay) are even harder, since the lessor never contracted for the improvements.
  • Measure: reasonable value (market-based, as here) vs. increase in the defendant's wealth (better for intimate relationships, as in Watts v. Watts).

Other Key Points

  • Hypo 7, "The GC Disappeared" (slides): ElectroCo is owed $80,000, the owner paid the GC $75,000, and the GC went bankrupt. Recovery is limited, perhaps to the unpaid portion. Practical protections include a mechanic's lien, a payment bond and joint checks ([Outside knowledge: common protective devices]).
