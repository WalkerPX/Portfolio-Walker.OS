Quick reference

Citation: 417 Mich. 17, 331 N.W.2d 203 (1982) · Casebook: Knapp p. 744 · Topic: Mistake › Mutual mistake One line: Both sides were mistaken about a hidden, illegal septic system, but the buyers could not rescind because the "in its present condition" clause allocated that risk to them.

Overview

The course case on mutual mistake under the Restatement (Second) approach (§§ 152, 154). The court rejects the old "quality vs. nature" distinction of Sherwood v. Walker and focuses on who bears the risk of the mistake.

Parties

  • Plaintiff: Lenawee County Board of Health, which condemned the property and obtained an injunction against habitation.
  • Buyers (appellees): Carl and Nancy Pickles. They bought the property on land contract and wanted to rescind for mutual mistake and failure of consideration.
  • Sellers (appellants): William and Martha Messerly. They wanted the land contract enforced (payments continued). (Intermediate owners: James Barnes and his wife.)

Facts

  • 1971: the Messerlys acquired land including a 600-square-foot tract with a three-unit apartment building. Their predecessor, Mr. Bloom, had installed a septic tank without a permit, in violation of the health code. It was too small.
  • The Messerlys sold to the Barneses on land contract (1973). The Barneses, with permission, sold to the Pickleses (March 1977).
  • The contract said the buyers had examined the property and accepted it "in its present condition."
  • Shortly after closing, sewage overflowed. The county condemned the property and enjoined human habitation until the system was fixed. Neither sellers nor buyers had known of the defect.

Procedural History

  • Trial court: no rescission. The buyers' remedy, if any, was against Bloom's estate. [Outside knowledge: trial court details.]
  • Court of Appeals: reversed (2-1) as to the Messerlys, granting rescission for mutual mistake.
  • Michigan Supreme Court: reversed the Court of Appeals. No rescission.

Issue

May purchasers rescind a land contract for mutual mistake when both parties were unaware the sewage system was defective and illegal, and the contract had an "as is"-type clause?

Holding

There was a mutual mistake of fact about a basic assumption. But rescission is denied because the purchasers bore the risk of the mistake under the "in its present condition" clause.

Reasoning

  • Rejects the Sherwood test: the distinction between mistakes about the "nature" or "substance" of the thing (rescission) and its "quality" or "value" (no rescission) is unworkable and largely a matter of characterization (Note 2).
  • Restatement (Second) § 152: a mutual mistake as to a basic assumption that has a material effect on the agreed exchange makes the contract voidable by the adversely affected party, unless that party bears the risk under § 154.
  • § 154: a party bears the risk when (a) the agreement allocates it, (b) the party is aware of limited knowledge but treats it as sufficient (conscious ignorance), or (c) the court allocates it as reasonable.
  • The "present condition" clause allocated the risk of such defects to the buyers. Equity does not require rescission. The real wrongdoer was Bloom, neither buyer nor seller.

Rule

A contract is voidable for mutual mistake if (1) both parties were mistaken (2) about a basic assumption on which the contract was made, (3) the mistake has a material effect on the agreed exchange, and (4) the party seeking relief does not bear the risk of the mistake (Restatement (Second) §§ 152, 154).

Why It Matters

  • It is the modern framework for mutual mistake (slides).
  • Note 3, "as is" clauses: classical: decisive, so the buyer assumed the risk. Modern: a boilerplate "as is" clause is one factor in the risk analysis, not conclusive, especially if it was not negotiated.
  • Note 8, reconciling with Gartner v. Eikill: Gartner allowed rescission for an unknown zoning restriction. One reconciliation is that zoning is a matter of public record (Gartner's buyer could have checked), while a hidden septic defect is not easily discoverable. Yet Lenawee denied relief, so the cases are apparently inconsistent.

Other Key Points

  • Hypo 1, "The Flooded Lot" (slides): a $100,000 parcel floods every other year, the seller disclosed one flood in ten years, there is no "as is" clause, and the buyer's contractor approved the land.
