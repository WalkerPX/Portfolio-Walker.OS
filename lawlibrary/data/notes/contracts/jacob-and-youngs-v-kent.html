Quick reference

Citation: 230 N.Y. 239, 129 N.E. 889 (1921) (Cardozo, J.) · Casebook: Knapp p. 851 · Topics: Material Breach and Substantial Performance, Expectation Damages › Cost of completion vs. diminution in value One line: A builder that installed non-Reading pipe of equal quality substantially performed, so it can recover the balance of the price, minus damages measured by the (nominal) difference in value, not the cost of tearing out and replacing the pipe.

Overview

Cardozo's classic statement of the substantial performance doctrine for constructive conditions, and of the diminution-in-value measure of damages where the cost of completion would be wasteful.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / respondent: Jacob & Youngs, Inc. The contractor that built a country residence. It wanted the unpaid balance of $3,483.46.
  • Defendant / appellant: George E. Kent [Outside knowledge: first name]. The owner, who refused to pay until the pipe was replaced.

Facts

  • The contract required "wrought iron pipe ... of a grade known as 'standard pipe' of Reading manufacture."
  • The plaintiff inspected the first 1,000 feet (Reading), but not later deliveries. 1,000 to 1,500 feet of non-Reading pipe (Cohoes and other brands) was installed, much of it inside the walls.
  • Kent moved in in June 1914 and learned of the substitution in March 1915. The architect refused a final certificate. Kent refused to pay the $3,483.46 balance unless the pipe was replaced, which would mean demolishing large parts of the structure.
  • The contractor sued, offering to prove the substitute pipe was of the same quality, appearance, market value and cost as Reading pipe. The omission was neither fraudulent nor willful, but the result of oversight.

Procedural History

  • The trial court excluded the evidence of equivalence and directed a verdict for Kent.
  • The Appellate Division reversed and granted a new trial.
  • Court of Appeals: affirmed the Appellate Division, and on the parties' stipulation directed judgment for the plaintiff. (A dissent by McLaughlin, J., joined by others, would have enforced the contract as written. [Outside knowledge: details of the dissent.])

Issue

  1. Does a builder's unintentional, trivial deviation from specifications bar recovery of the contract price?
  2. What is the measure of the owner's damages for that deviation?

Holding

  1. No. The contractor substantially performed, so its deviation was not a breach of a condition that bars recovery. It may recover the contract price minus damages.
  2. Damages are measured by the difference in value between the house as specified and as built, which is nominal or nothing here, not the cost of replacement, which would be grossly disproportionate.

Reasoning

  • Courts classify promises as dependent (conditions) or independent with "partly ... justice and partly ... presumable intention." Absent clear language making literal compliance a condition, the law will not impose a forfeiture for "venial faults."
  • "The transition is gradual" from trivial to material deviations. Courts weigh the purpose to be served, the desire to be gratified, the excuse for deviation, and the cruelty of enforced adherence.
  • The builder was not willful. "The willful transgressor must accept the penalty of his transgression."
  • Parties may make literal compliance an express condition, but must say so clearly.
  • Damages: where the cost of completion is "grossly and unfairly out of proportion to the good to be attained," the measure is diminution in value, which here is nominal.

Rule

A party who has substantially performed in good faith may enforce the contract, subject to the other party's claim for damages for the deviation. Substantial performance satisfies constructive conditions, not express ones. Where repair would be economically wasteful, damages are measured by diminution in value, not cost of completion.

Why It Matters

  • Note 2: constructive conditions are default rules that apply absent agreement.
  • Note 3: parties can make any obligation an express condition, and then substantial performance does not apply (Oppenheimer). A court might still excuse the condition by interpretation, waiver or excuse to avoid forfeiture.
  • Note 4: substantial performance is an aspect of the more general doctrine of constructive conditions.
  • Note 5, factors: the purpose of the provision; the reason for non-performance (good faith?); the cost of remedying the defect; the adequacy of damages.
  • Substantial performance = partial breach (Sackett Note 2): the other side gets damages but is not discharged.

Other Key Points

  • Comment on constructive conditions (p. 860): parties can change the default rules by agreement, and most modern commercial contracts do.
  • See also Expectation Damages (cost vs. value). [Outside knowledge: compare Peevyhouse v. Garland Coal, not on the slides.]
