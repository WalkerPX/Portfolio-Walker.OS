In one paragraph

A condition is an event, not certain to occur, that must occur before a performance becomes due, unless its non-occurrence is excused (Restatement § 224). Express conditions are created by the parties and must be strictly satisfied. Substantial performance doesn't count (Oppenheimer v. Oppenheim Appel Dixon, Maxton Builders v. Lo Galbo). Because strict enforcement can be harsh, courts (1) require clear language and read doubtful terms as promises rather than conditions where that avoids forfeiture (§ 227), and (2) excuse conditions through waiver, prevention (a good-faith idea), immateriality or excuse to avoid forfeiture (§ 229; JNA Realty v. Cross Bay Chelsea). Constructive conditions are supplied by courts and tempered by substantial performance. See Material Breach and Substantial Performance.

Conditions vs. promises

  • Condition: if it fails, the other party's duty never becomes due. But failure is not itself a breach, so there are no damages.
  • Promise: if it is broken, there is breach and damages.
  • A term can be both (a promissory condition). Which it is turns on interpretation.
  • Oppenheimer's three readings of "obtain the landlord's consent": (1) a promise to obtain it; (2) a promise to try; (3) no promise, only a condition.
  • Language: "on condition that," "provided that," "if," "unless and until" signal a condition. "Agrees to" or "shall" signal a promise.
  • § 227: a doubtful term should not be read as a condition if that would increase the risk of forfeiture.
  • Pay-if-paid vs. pay-when-paid: a pay-if-paid clause makes the owner's payment a condition of the GC's duty to pay the sub. A pay-when-paid clause only sets the timing of a promise. Courts require clear language for pay-if-paid.

Strict compliance

  • An express condition must be strictly satisfied. Substantial performance is irrelevant to an express condition.
  • Oppenheimer v. Oppenheim Appel Dixon: a sublease was conditioned on the defendant's receipt of written notice of the landlord's consent to telephone-link work by a deadline (extended to Feb. 25). The plaintiff got consent and phoned the defendant's lawyer on Feb. 25, but sent no written notice. The jury found substantial performance and awarded $1.2 million. The Court of Appeals reversed: substantial performance has no place with express conditions. The jury had found no waiver or estoppel.
  • Maxton Builders v. Lo Galbo (note case): a written cancellation notice that arrived a few days late did not satisfy the express condition, even though the other party knew of the intent to cancel. The losing lawyer was later held liable for malpractice.
  • Drafting lesson: use "condition," say whose duty is conditioned, and specify how notice is to be given.

Excuse of conditions

  1. Waiver: a party may waive a condition that exists for its benefit. Some courts require that the waiver be clearly established. Waiver can eliminate only a non-material condition; removing a material one requires a modification (with consideration). A party can waive all or part of a condition. A waiver may be retracted before the condition's date unless the other side has relied (see the retraction hypo).
  2. Prevention: a party that wrongfully prevents a condition from occurring can't rely on its failure. This is an aspect of good faith (Good Faith and Fair Dealing).
  3. Immateriality: Professor Childres would excuse any immaterial condition. The Restatement gives courts some flexibility but doesn't go that far.
  4. Impossibility or impracticability of a non-material condition.
  5. Repudiation by the party benefited [Outside knowledge]; going-out-of-business conditions (for example, in output contracts).
  6. Excuse to avoid forfeiture (below).

Excuse to avoid forfeiture

  • Restatement § 229: a court may excuse the non-occurrence of a condition if it would cause disproportionate forfeiture, unless the condition was a material part of the agreed exchange. [Outside knowledge] the section number; the slides describe the doctrine without citing it.
  • JNA Realty v. Cross Bay Chelsea: a restaurant tenant's lease option to renew required written notice six months before expiration. The tenant, through inadvertence, gave notice over four months late, after investing heavily in improvements. Test (equitable relief excusing a notice condition):
    1. the tenant would suffer a forfeiture (loss of improvements and goodwill);
    2. no prejudice to the landlord; and
    3. the failure was "venial inattention," not culpable misconduct (gross negligence or bad faith).
  • The landlord's knowledge that improvements were made in expectation of renewal strengthened the claim. Remanded. The parties settled on higher rent.

Implied and constructive conditions

  • Implied-in-fact conditions: (1) performance, where one party's duty hinges on the other's performance; (2) cooperation; (3) notice, where the performing party couldn't reasonably know that performance was due.
  • Constructive conditions (implied in law): supplied by the court for fairness, mainly in bilateral contracts. Each party's substantial performance is a constructive condition of the other's later duties. See Material Breach and Substantial Performance and Jacob and Youngs v. Kent.
  • Example (slides): an insurer that rejects 90% of claims without justification has materially breached, so the employer's duty to pay the premium never comes due. The employer may sue for damages, and the insurer can't collect the premium.

Exam framework

  1. Condition or promise (or both)? Look at the language, apply § 227's anti-forfeiture reading, and run the Oppenheimer readings.
  2. Express or constructive? Express → strict compliance. Constructive → substantial performance.
  3. Did the condition occur exactly? If not:
  4. Excuse? Waiver (whose benefit? material? retracted?), prevention (good faith), immateriality, impossibility, forfeiture (JNA's three parts / § 229).
  5. Consequences: an unexcused failure of condition means no duty to perform. If the term was also a promise, there are damages for breach.

Hypos (slides)

  • Oppenheimer waiver: the telephone-work condition had three parts: consent, written notice, and receipt by Feb. 25. Consent was obtained. Were the writing and the deadline material, or technical and waivable?
  • Retraction: a waiver on Feb. 15 of a Feb. 25 condition. Retracted Feb. 23? Feb. 28? Does the plaintiff's reliance matter?
  • Whose condition: the landlord's consent to the new tenant protects both parties. The telephone-work condition protects only the defendant, so only the defendant can waive it.
  • Prevention: (a) the plaintiff gets consent but withholds notice because it wants out → the condition is excused or the plaintiff is in breach; (b) the plaintiff never tries to get consent → was the defendant harmed if the landlord would have refused anyway?
  • Constructive condition: the insurer rejecting 90% of claims (above).
