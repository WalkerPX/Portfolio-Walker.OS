Quick reference

Citation: 64 F.2d 344 (2d Cir. 1933) (L. Hand, J.) · Casebook: Knapp p. 262 · Topic: Option Contracts and Firm Offers One line: A supplier's mistaken bid could be withdrawn before acceptance even though the general contractor had already used it. No unilateral contract, no promissory estoppel, no option. This is the minority view.

Overview

Judge Learned Hand's classic refusal to make a subcontractor's bid irrevocable. It is the minority view. Drennan v. Star Paving (the majority) and Restatement (Second) § 87(2) go the other way.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: James Baird Co. A general contractor bidding on a Pennsylvania public building. It used Gimbel's price in its bid and wanted damages when Gimbel withdrew.
  • Defendant / appellee: Gimbel Bros., Inc. A department store supplying linoleum. Its bid contained a large calculation error, and it wanted to withdraw.

Facts

  • Gimbel sent offers to contractors bidding on a public building, quoting linoleum prices. [Outside knowledge: the error came from computing the yardage for only about half the required amount, so the price was about half what it should have been. The offer said it was made because of the bids on the job and was for prompt acceptance after the award.]
  • Gimbel discovered the mistake and telegraphed a revocation (December 28). Baird had already submitted its general bid using Gimbel's price. It received the revocation, and its bid was accepted afterward (December 30). Baird then "accepted" Gimbel's offer.
  • Baird sued for damages for Gimbel's refusal to supply at the quoted price.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The district court ruled for Gimbel. Second Circuit: affirmed.

Issue

Did the general contractor's use of the subcontractor's bid make the bid irrevocable, under unilateral contract, promissory estoppel or option theories?

Holding

No. Gimbel's revocation was effective before acceptance. Baird has no remedy.

Reasoning

Judge Hand considered and rejected three theories (slides):

  1. Unilateral contract: using the bid was not "acceptance." Gimbel's offer sought a promise (acceptance after the award), not the act of using the bid. A contract could form only on acceptance after the award.
  2. Promissory estoppel: the doctrine applies to donative promises, not to commercial offers that contemplate an exchange. An offer is not a promise until accepted. Baird's reliance came at its own risk.
  3. Option contract: no consideration was paid for irrevocability. Hand added there was "no reason to suppose" Gimbel "meant to subject itself to such a one-sided obligation": Gimbel would be bound while Baird would be free.

Rule

(Minority / classical rule) A subcontractor's bid is an ordinary revocable offer. The general contractor's use of the bid in its own bid is neither acceptance nor detrimental reliance that makes the bid irrevocable, absent consideration for an option.

Why It Matters

  • Baird vs. Drennan is the classic split on reliance on offers. Most courts and Restatement § 87(2) follow Drennan.
  • Note 1: most courts agree with Hand that using a sub's bid is not acceptance as a unilateral contract. Counterargument: could a contract form on use, with performance conditional on winning the general contract?
  • Note 2: on the option, the better answer is simply that no consideration was paid for irrevocability (slides).
  • Contractors' practical responses: bid shopping by contractors and bid withdrawal by subs, plus the fairness of one-sided reliance.

Other Key Points

  • Hand's view of promissory estoppel as limited to gifts reflects the 1930s understanding of Restatement (First) § 90.
  • Related: Berryman v. Kmoch (options need actual consideration); Promissory Estoppel.
