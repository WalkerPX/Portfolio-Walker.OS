Quick reference

Citation: 34 Minn. 374, 26 N.W. 1 (1885) · Casebook: Knapp p. 432 · Topic: Parol Evidence Rule › Classical approach One line: A short written log-sale contract that looked complete on its face was a full integration, so evidence of an oral warranty of quality was inadmissible.

Overview

The course case for the classical ("four corners") approach to the parol evidence rule. If a writing appears complete on its face, it is treated as a full integration, and evidence of additional oral terms is excluded.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant (on the new-trial motion): J.H. Thompson (through his agent D.S. Mooers). The seller of logs marked "H.C.A.," suing for the purchase price.
  • Defendant / respondent: R.C. Libby of Hastings, Minnesota. The buyer, who claimed an oral warranty of quality and its breach as a defense.

Facts

  • Thompson owned logs marked "H.C.A.," cut in the winters of 1882-83, lying in or along the Mississippi above Minneapolis.
  • June 1, 1883: the parties signed a short writing: "I have this day sold to R.C. Libby ... all my logs marked 'H.C.A.,' ... for ten dollars a thousand feet, boom scale at Minneapolis... Payments cash as fast as scale bills are produced."
  • Thompson sued for the price. Libby pleaded an oral warranty of the logs' quality, made at the time of sale, and its breach.

Procedural History

  • At trial, oral testimony of the warranty was admitted over Thompson's objection. [Outside knowledge: the verdict was for the defendant.] The trial court denied a new trial.
  • Minnesota Supreme Court: the order refusing a new trial is reversed.

Issue

Is oral evidence of a warranty of quality admissible to add to a written contract of sale that is silent about warranties?

Holding

No. The writing was a complete contract on its face, so evidence of the oral warranty was inadmissible under the parol evidence rule. Admitting it was error.

Reasoning

  • When parties reduce their agreement to a writing that appears to be a complete expression of the bargain, the law presumes the writing contains the whole agreement. Prior or contemporaneous oral terms may not add to, vary or contradict it.
  • Completeness is judged from the writing itself: if it imports a complete legal obligation, with no uncertainty as to its object or extent, it is conclusively presumed to be the whole agreement.
  • A warranty is part of the contract of sale, not a separate, collateral agreement, so it falls within the rule.
  • The court acknowledges exceptions in passing: the writing being incomplete on its face, fraud, mistake, a collateral agreement, and ambiguity requiring explanation.

Rule

(Classical) If a writing appears on its face to be a complete agreement, it is a full integration, and extrinsic evidence of prior or contemporaneous agreements that add to or vary its terms is inadmissible.

Why It Matters

  • It is the baseline for comparing the modern, Corbin and Restatement approach (Taylor v. State Farm).
  • Note 1: admitting extrinsic evidence can cause inconvenience (time and expense) or injustice (fabricated evidence). Excluding it can cause injustice when the writing doesn't reflect the actual deal.
  • Note 2 (classical): a writing is a full integration if it has a merger clause or appears complete on its face. The "four corners" approach rests on skepticism of juries and a desire for certainty.
  • Note 3 (modern): courts look broadly at the evidence to decide whether the writing was meant to be final and complete. A merger clause is not conclusive.
  • Taylor Note 1: in Thompson, the buyer wanted to supplement the writing. The rule applies most persuasively to supplementation.

Other Key Points

  • UCC § 2-202: today an oral warranty in a sale of goods would be analyzed under § 2-202, which allows consistent additional terms unless the writing was intended as complete and exclusive, plus course of dealing, usage of trade and course of performance (§ 1-303).
  • Restatement (Second) § 216 (slides): a consistent additional term is admissible unless the agreement is completely integrated. A writing is not completely integrated if it omits a term agreed for separate consideration or one that "might naturally be omitted."
