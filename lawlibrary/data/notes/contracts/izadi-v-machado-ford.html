Quick reference

Citation: 550 So. 2d 1135 (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 1989) · Casebook: Knapp p. 38 · Topic: Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance One line: A car dealer's misleading "bait and switch" newspaper ad could be treated as a binding offer.

Coverage

Knapp cites Izadi only in Lonergan Note 2, for the "bait and switch" exception to the rule that ads are not offers. Sateriale relies on it (slides: "citing Izadi, p. 38"). Facts and reasoning below marked [Outside knowledge] are not in your materials. Verify before relying on them.

Overview

An exception to the general rule that advertisements are invitations to deal, not offers. When a seller uses a deceptive ad to lure customers in ("bait and switch"), a court may treat the ad as an offer. The seller may also face liability under unfair-trade-practice and consumer-protection statutes (Knapp p. 38).

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Izadi [Outside knowledge: details]. A consumer who tried to buy a car on the terms in the dealer's ad.
  • Defendant / appellee: Machado (Gus) Ford, Inc. A Ford dealership that ran the ad and refused to honor it.

Facts

[Outside knowledge, unverified] The dealer's newspaper ad prominently advertised a large trade-in allowance (recalled as $3,000) on a new Ford. Barely legible fine print limited the allowance to trade-ins of a particular vintage, so few readers could actually qualify. Izadi tendered a trade-in and demanded the advertised terms. The dealer refused. Check the opinion before relying on these specifics.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The trial court dismissed the complaint. The Florida District Court of Appeal reversed.

Issue

Can a deceptive car-dealer advertisement be treated as a binding offer, notwithstanding the general rule that ads are invitations?

Holding

Yes (as Knapp summarizes it). A bait-and-switch ad may be treated as an offer. [Outside knowledge] The appellate court held the complaint stated claims for breach of contract and fraud.

Reasoning

  • From Knapp: in "bait and switch" or other deceptive conduct, "the court may treat the advertisement as an offer or may subject the seller to liability under unfair trade practice or other consumer protection statutes."
  • [Outside knowledge] The court reasoned that a seller who deliberately draws customers in with a misleading ad cannot hide behind the "ads are not offers" rule, and that the ad, read objectively, was a definite offer to anyone with a trade-in.

Rule

Advertisements are ordinarily not offers. But a court may treat an advertisement as an offer, and enforce it on its apparent terms, when the advertiser engaged in deceptive "bait and switch" practices.

Why It Matters

  • It is an exception to the ad rule from Lonergan v. Scolnick and Leonard v. Pepsico.
  • Sateriale v. RJ Reynolds relied on Izadi to find that a promotional rewards program could be an offer, and doubted the general rule even applies in California.
  • Exam use: when an ad is the "offer," check for (1) language of commitment or "first come, first served" (Lefkowitz), (2) deception or bait and switch (Izadi), and (3) a request for performance (unilateral contract, Sateriale).
