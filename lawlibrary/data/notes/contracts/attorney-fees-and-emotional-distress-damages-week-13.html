Big picture

  • Two items commonly excluded from a plaintiff's contract damages: attorney fees and emotional distress damages.
  • Attorney fees: American rule = each party bears its own fees. A specific, recognizable basis is needed to shift them.
  • Emotional distress: generally not recoverable in contract; Erlich (Cal.) denies it under both tort and contract theories on those facts.
  • Zapata (7th Cir.): CISG does not supply a basis for attorney fees; result matches UCC treatment.

Doctrine outline

Attorney fees — American rule

  • American rule: parties usually bear their own attorney fees.
  • Contrasts with the English rule and civil law approach: losing party normally pays prevailing party's fees.
  • American rule still applies in U.S. courts → must identify a specific, recognizable basis to award fees to the winner.

Three principal bases for awarding attorney fees in U.S. courts

  1. A contract provision.
  2. A statutory provision.
  3. Judicial power based on court rules or inherent authority.

Zapata — trial court's two theories (both reversed)

  • Theory 1: fees as foreseeable consequential damages under CISG Article 74 (treaty = federal law equal to a statute).
  • Theory 2: fees as a sanction for defendant's bad faith conduct.
  • Seventh Circuit: CISG governs substantive contract law, not the procedural question of when attorney fees are recoverable.
  • Seventh Circuit: trial court could not award fees to sanction bad faith conduct occurring prior to litigation.
  • Remand: trial court to consider whether defendant engaged in misconduct during litigation supporting fees as a sanction.

Statutory basis (Note 2)

  • Many statutes call for awarding fees to the prevailing party.
  • UCC §§2-710, 2-715 ("consequential" and "incidental" damages) consistently interpreted as not allowing attorney fees.
  • Zapata outcome is consistent with how the case would be decided under the UCC.

Court rules (Note 3)

  • Federal or state court rules are a second type of authority.
  • Rule 11: lawyer's duty not to engage in frivolous litigation conduct; financial and disciplinary sanctions, including opposing party's attorney fees. (Civil Procedure topic.)

Contract exception (Note 4)

  • Freedom of contract supports enforcing a private agreement letting the prevailing party recover reasonable attorney fees.

Emotional distress — Erlich

  • "Dream house" construction gone wrong; defects and emotional impact enormous.
  • California Supreme Court: Erlichs cannot recover emotional distress damages under either tort or contract theory.
  • Breach of promise generally actionable only in contract, "except when the actions that constitute the breach violate a social policy that merits the imposition of tort remedies."
  • Erlich court cites the Restatement test with approval but seems to adopt an even more restrictive rule: emotional distress damages for breach of contract only when "emotional concerns are the essence of the contract" (p. 984).

Tests and elements

  • To shift attorney fees under the American rule — checklist: identify a specific, recognizable basis: (1) contract provision, (2) statutory provision, or (3) court rules / inherent judicial authority.
  • Contract exception elements: private agreement + prevailing party + reasonable attorney fees.
  • Sanction basis (Zapata remand): misconduct during litigation (not pre-litigation bad faith).
  • Erlich tort-remedy test: breach violates a social policy that merits imposition of tort remedies.
  • Erlich restrictive contract test: emotional distress recoverable only when "emotional concerns are the essence of the contract."

Cases

  • Zapata Hermanos Sucesores, S.A. v. Hearthside Baking Co. (p. 968) — 7th Cir. (Posner): reversed fees on both grounds; CISG is substantive, not procedural, on attorney fees; no fees to sanction pre-litigation bad faith; remanded on litigation misconduct.
  • Erlich v. Menezes (p. 977) — Cal. Supreme Court: no emotional distress damages under tort or contract theory on these facts.
  • UCC §§2-710, 2-715 — cited (Note 2): consequential/incidental damages provisions do not allow attorney fees.
  • CISG Article 74 — cited (Zapata): trial court's consequential-damages theory for fees; rejected.
  • Rule 11 — cited (Note 3): court-rules basis for fees as sanction.

Examples and hypos

  • Zapata: trial court awarded fees as CISG Art. 74 consequential damages and as bad-faith sanction; both reversed; remand limited to litigation misconduct.
  • Erlich: defective "dream house"; enormous defects and emotional impact; still no emotional distress recovery in tort or contract.
  • Note 2 hypo: same fee question under the UCC → fees still denied; Zapata consistent with UCC.

Exam angles

  • Start any fee question with the American rule; then run the three-basis checklist.
  • If a contract clause shifts fees: enforce under freedom of contract (Note 4).
  • If a statute is invoked: check it; note UCC §§2-710, 2-715 do not cover fees.
  • If CISG is invoked for fees: CISG is substantive only; procedural fee question left open (Note 1: issue still open in recent decisions/commentary).
  • If sanction theory: distinguish pre-litigation bad faith (no) from litigation misconduct (remand allowed).
  • Emotional distress: default = no recovery in contract; argue social-policy tort exception, then the stricter "essence of the contract" test.
  • Policy arguments (Note 6): pros — deters plaintiffs from suing, avoids cluttering courts with insignificant claims; cons — deters legitimate non-monetary suits, lets parties ignore others' rights hoping litigation costs deter suit, leaves prevailing party undercompensated.
