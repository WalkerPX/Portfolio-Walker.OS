The short version

If one side clearly says, or shows by its actions, that it won't perform when the time comes, the other side doesn't have to wait. And if there's good reason to doubt the other side will perform, you can demand reassurance.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Anticipatory Repudiation and Adequate Assurance.

What it's about

Anticipatory repudiation

A breach can happen before performance is due, if one side clearly and definitely says or shows it won't perform. The other side may then:

  • treat it as a breach right away and sue,
  • suspend its own performance, and
  • mitigate: take reasonable steps to limit its losses instead of running up costs.

The statement must be unequivocal. Grumbling, asking to renegotiate or expressing doubts is not enough. A repudiation can be retracted until the other side relies on it or says it's treating the deal as over.

Adequate assurance

What if you're not sure the other side will perform (they're in financial trouble, they've missed other deadlines)? Under UCC § 2-609 (and Restatement § 251 for other contracts), you can:

  1. demand adequate assurance of performance in writing, and
  2. suspend your own performance while you wait. If they don't give reasonable assurance within a reasonable time (no more than 30 days under the UCC), that failure is itself a repudiation.

An everyday example

You hire a contractor to start your kitchen in June. In April, the contractor emails: "We've gone out of business and won't be doing any more jobs." That's a repudiation. You can hire someone else now and sue for the extra cost. If they had just written, "Business is slow, we'll see," you'd demand assurance instead.

The cases, in plain words

  • Truman L. Flatt and Sons v. Schupf: a buyer's letter asking for a lower price was not a repudiation. It wasn't definite, and the buyer retracted it anyway.
  • Hornell Brewing v. Spry: a beverage maker with real reason to worry about a distributor's finances demanded assurance. Getting no answer counted as a repudiation.
  • Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge: after the county cancelled, the builder kept building the bridge anyway. It could recover only what it was owed up to the cancellation plus its expected profit, not the costs of building after.

Words you'll hear

  • Repudiation: a clear refusal to perform.
  • Retraction: taking back a repudiation before the other side relies on it.
  • Mitigation: reducing your losses after a breach.
