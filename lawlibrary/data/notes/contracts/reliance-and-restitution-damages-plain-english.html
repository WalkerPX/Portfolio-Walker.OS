The short version

When the benefit of the bargain can't be measured, or isn't the right fit, courts can instead reimburse what you spent relying on the deal (reliance), or give back the value of what you handed over (restitution).

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Reliance and Restitution Damages.

What it's about

Contract remedies protect three different "interests":

InterestGoalPlain meaning
ExpectationWhere you'd be if the deal were keptThe usual measure (Expectation Damages (Plain English))
RelianceWhere you'd be if the deal never happenedGet back what you spent relying on it
RestitutionMake the other side give back what it gotReturn the value of the benefit you conferred

Reliance damages

  • Used when profits are too uncertain to prove, or when the claim is based on promissory estoppel.
  • Covers out-of-pocket costs (preparation, investment).
  • If the other side proves you would have lost money on the deal anyway, reliance damages are reduced by that loss.

Restitution as a remedy

  • The injured party can recover the reasonable value of what it provided, sometimes even more than the contract price on a losing contract.
  • Even a breaching party may recover benefits it conferred, minus the harm it caused (Restatement § 374).

An everyday example

You sign up to open a franchise, spend $50,000 on equipment and training, and the company backs out before opening day. Because nobody can say what a brand-new location would have earned, you'd probably recover your $50,000 in reliance damages.

The cases, in plain words

  • Wartzman v. Hightower Productions: a stunt's profits were too speculative, so the investors got their reliance losses back.
  • Walser v. Toyota Motor Sales: would-be dealers who relied on a promise recovered $232,131 in out-of-pocket costs, not lost dealership profits.
  • US ex rel Coastal Steel v. Algernon Blair: a subcontractor that quit after the prime contractor didn't pay recovered the reasonable value of its work, even though it would have lost money on the full job.
  • Lancellotti v. Thomas: a buyer who walked away from a business purchase could recover what he paid beyond the sellers' actual harm.
  • Shaffer v. George Washington University: students could seek restitution for in-person tuition when classes moved online.

Words you'll hear

  • Out-of-pocket: money actually spent.
  • Quantum meruit: reasonable value of services.
  • Losing contract: one where performance would have cost more than it earned.
