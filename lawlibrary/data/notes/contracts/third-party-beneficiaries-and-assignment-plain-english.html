The short version

Sometimes a person who isn't a party to a contract can still enforce it, because the contract was meant to benefit them. Contract rights can usually be transferred to someone else, but duties can't always be handed off.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Third-Party Beneficiaries and Assignment.

What it's about

Third-party beneficiaries

Usually only the people who made a contract can enforce it. But if the contract was intended to benefit someone else, that person may be able to sue.

  • Intended beneficiary: the parties meant to benefit them (for example, a life insurance beneficiary). They can enforce.
  • Incidental beneficiary: they just happen to benefit (a neighbor whose property value rises when you renovate). They can't enforce.
  • Once the beneficiary's rights vest (they rely on it, sue on it or agree to it), the original parties generally can't take them away.

Assignment and delegation

  • Assignment transfers your rights under a contract to someone else (like selling the right to be paid). It's generally allowed unless it would materially change the other side's burden or risk.
  • Delegation hands off your duties. It's allowed unless the other side has a strong interest in you personally performing (like a famous painter hired for a portrait). You stay responsible if the delegate fails.
  • Anti-assignment clauses are read narrowly.

An everyday example

You buy life insurance naming your daughter as beneficiary. If the insurer refuses to pay after you die, your daughter can sue the insurer, even though she never signed the policy.
