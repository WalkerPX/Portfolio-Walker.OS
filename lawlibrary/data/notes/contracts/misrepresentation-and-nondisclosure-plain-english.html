The short version

If you were talked into a deal by a false statement, whether a lie or an important honest error, you can usually cancel it. Staying silent can also count when there's a duty to speak, such as hiding a known serious defect.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure.

What it's about

A contract can be cancelled (it's voidable) if:

  1. the other side made a false statement of fact,
  2. it was either fraudulent (they knew it was false or didn't care) or material (important enough to matter to a reasonable person),
  3. it induced you to agree, and
  4. your reliance on it was justified.

When silence counts

The traditional rule was "buyer beware": no duty to tell the other side what you know. Today, staying quiet can count as a misrepresentation when:

  • you actively hide something (painting over water damage),
  • you know a serious hidden defect the other side can't easily discover, or
  • you know the other side is mistaken about a basic assumption and good faith calls for correcting it.

Fraud vs. the duty to read

Normally you're bound by what you sign, even if you didn't read it. But if someone lies about what the document is ("these are just the lease papers"), you may not be bound.

An everyday example

You buy a house. The sellers know the basement floods every spring, and they put a rug over the water stain. That's concealment. Even if they never said a word, you can likely cancel the sale or recover damages.

The cases, in plain words

  • Hill v. Jones: home sellers had to disclose a known past termite infestation that buyers couldn't easily see.
  • Laidlaw v. Organ: the classic old rule. A tobacco buyer who learned before the seller that the War of 1812 had ended didn't have to share that news.
  • Syester v. Banta: an elderly widow flattered into buying nearly $30,000 of dance lessons could recover for fraud.
  • Park 100 Investors v. Kartes: business owners told they were signing "lease papers" had actually signed a personal guaranty, and weren't bound.
  • Ray v. Eurice Bros: by contrast, without any trick, you're bound by what you sign.
  • Sherrodd v. Morrison-Knudsen: a written contract can block fraud claims that contradict its terms.

Words you'll hear

  • Material: important enough to affect a reasonable person's decision.
  • Concealment: actively hiding a fact.
  • Fraud in the execution: being tricked about what document you're signing.
