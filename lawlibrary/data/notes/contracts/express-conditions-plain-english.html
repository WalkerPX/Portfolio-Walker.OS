The short version

A condition is an event that must happen before someone has to perform. When the contract clearly makes something a condition, it usually must be met exactly. 'Close enough' doesn't count, though courts may excuse it to avoid a harsh forfeiture.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Express Conditions.

What it's about

A promise is something a party commits to do. A condition is an event that must happen before the other party's duty kicks in. Examples: "if the buyer gets a mortgage," "provided notice is given in writing by March 1."

Why it matters:

  • If a promise isn't kept exactly, the other side gets damages but usually still has to perform, as long as the breach isn't serious (see Material Breach and Substantial Performance (Plain English)).
  • If an express condition isn't met, the other side doesn't have to perform at all. Courts generally require strict compliance with express conditions.

When is something a condition?

Look for words like "if," "on condition that," "provided that," "unless and until." When it's unclear, courts lean toward reading the term as a promise, which avoids harsh results.

Ways a condition can be excused

  • Waiver: the protected party gives up the condition (by words or conduct).
  • Prevention: you can't block a condition from happening and then rely on it not happening.
  • Avoiding forfeiture: a court may excuse a minor, non-essential condition if insisting on it would cause a big loss and the other side isn't really harmed.

An everyday example

Your home purchase contract says the deal goes ahead only "if the buyer obtains mortgage approval by June 1." The bank approves you on June 5. Strictly, the condition failed, and the seller doesn't have to sell, unless the seller waived the deadline or the delay was the seller's fault.

The cases, in plain words

  • Oppenheimer v. Oppenheim Appel Dixon: the contract required written notice by a deadline. Phone notice wasn't enough, and a $1.2 million verdict was wiped out.
  • Maxton Builders v. Lo Galbo: written cancellation notice that arrived a few days late didn't count, even though the other side already knew.
  • JNA Realty v. Cross Bay Chelsea: a restaurant tenant gave lease-renewal notice months late, but a court may excuse that to avoid losing its big investment, if the landlord wasn't hurt.
  • Jacob and Youngs v. Kent: compare. The pipe brand was a promise, not a condition, so "close enough" performance still earned payment.

Words you'll hear

  • Condition precedent: an event that must happen before a duty arises.
  • Strict compliance: exact performance, not substantial.
  • Forfeiture: losing something valuable, such as investments or improvements, because a condition failed.
