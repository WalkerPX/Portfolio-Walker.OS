In one paragraph

Some contracts are unenforceable unless there is a signed writing. The categories are suretyship, marriage, land, contracts that can't be performed within one year, and goods of $500 or more. The writing must identify the subject matter, show that a contract was made, state the essential terms, and be signed by the party to be charged. Several documents can be read together (Crabtree v. Elizabeth Arden). Without a sufficient writing, look for an exception: part performance for land (Beaver v. Brumlow, Burns v. McCormick), promissory estoppel under Restatement § 139 (Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice), or for goods, the UCC § 2-201 exceptions (Buffaloe v. Hart). The purpose is to stop people from fabricating contracts.

Three-step frame

  1. Is the contract within the statute?
  2. Is there a sufficient signed writing?
  3. If not, does an exception apply?

Categories within the statute

  1. Suretyship: a promise to answer for another's debt (a guaranty).
  2. Marriage: a promise made in consideration of marriage.
  3. Land: a promise to transfer an interest in land, including sales, mortgages and (longer) leases.
  4. One-year provision: a contract that by its terms cannot possibly be performed within one year of its making.
  5. Goods of $500 or more: UCC § 2-201 (below).

The one-year provision

  • If there is any possibility, however unlikely, that the contract can be fully performed within a year, it is outside the statute.
  • Contracts of indefinite or no stated duration are outside the statute.
  • Performance vs. termination: the chance that a contract ends within a year by breach or excuse doesn't take it out of the statute.
  • Lifetime employment: traditionally outside, since the employee could die within a year and that completes performance. Some recent decisions treat it as a term of years that needs a writing.
  • Crabtree v. Elizabeth Arden: a two-year employment contract was within the one-year provision, so a writing was needed. It was satisfied by combining documents.
  • Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice: an oral promise of a two-year job was within the provision. Promissory estoppel was available anyway (below).

The writing requirement

Common-law checklist. The signed writing must:

  1. reasonably identify the subject matter;
  2. show that a contract has been made between the parties;
  3. state the essential terms with reasonable certainty; and
  4. be signed by or on behalf of the party to be charged.

Combining several documents (Crabtree v. Elizabeth Arden):

  • Together, the documents contain all the essential terms.
  • At least one is signed by the party to be charged.
  • The unsigned document shows on its face that it relates to the same transaction. No explicit cross-reference is needed.
  • Parol evidence may be used to show assent to the unsigned writing.
  • In Crabtree, the signed payroll change cards and an unsigned memo of the negotiation were read together to show the two-year term.

Part performance (land)

  • The oldest exception. The typical part performance is the buyer taking possession plus making valuable improvements (often with payment).
  • "Unequivocally referable" test (Cardozo, Burns v. McCormick): the acts must be explainable only by the oral contract.
  • Beaver v. Brumlow: uses "unequivocally referable" language but rejects a narrow reading. The performance need not relate exclusively to the contract. It is enough if it would lead an outsider to "naturally and reasonably" conclude that a contract exists. In Beaver, the buyers' possession and improvements (they moved a double-wide home onto the land and spent about $85,000 on improvements) supported specific performance.
  • Restatement (Second) § 129: focuses on whether the promisee reasonably relied and whether justice requires specific enforcement, not on the "unequivocally referable" label (Note 2). [Outside knowledge] the section number § 129 is supplied from general knowledge; the slides cite only "the Restatement."
  • The usual remedy is specific performance (Specific Performance).

Reliance and promissory estoppel

  • First Restatement: estoppel was allowed only if the promisor misrepresented that a writing existed or promised to sign one, which required fraud-like conduct.
  • Restatement (Second) § 139: a promise that induces foreseeable, reasonable reliance is enforceable despite the statute if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. The remedy may be limited as justice requires.
  • § 139(2) factors (the slides say "see § 139(2)" but don't list them). [Outside knowledge]: (a) whether other remedies such as restitution are available and adequate; (b) how definite and substantial the reliance is relative to the remedy sought; (c) how far the reliance corroborates the making and terms of the promise, or those are otherwise shown by clear and convincing evidence; (d) how reasonable the reliance was; (e) whether the promisor could foresee it.
  • Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice: Rice gave up her job in Maryland and moved to Alaska based on an oral promise of a two-year job as the party's executive director. The court applied § 139 without requiring fraud. Reliance must be reasonable, including a reasonable belief that the person making the promise had authority to make it.
  • See Promissory Estoppel.

UCC 2-201

Applies to contracts for the sale of goods for $500 or more (check goods and sale first; UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation).

The writing must (1) indicate that a contract was made, (2) state a quantity, and (3) be signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought. Other terms, including price, can be supplied by course of performance, course of dealing, trade usage or gap-fillers. The contract is not enforceable beyond the quantity stated.

The five exceptions:

  1. Merchant's confirmation, or "reply doctrine" (§ 2-201(2)): between merchants, a written confirmation that would bind the sender binds the recipient too, unless the recipient objects in writing within 10 days of receipt.
  2. Specially manufactured goods (§ 2-201(3)(a)): the goods were made specially for the buyer, are not suitable for sale to others in the seller's ordinary business, and the seller has substantially begun making them or committed to buy them.
  3. Admission (§ 2-201(3)(b)): a party who admits in pleadings, testimony or otherwise in court that a contract was made is bound, up to the quantity admitted.
  4. Payment made and accepted (§ 2-201(3)(c)): enforceable to the extent of the payment.
  5. Goods received and accepted (§ 2-201(3)(c)): enforceable to the extent of the goods accepted. Example: if 50 of 100 units are delivered and accepted, the contract covers those 50.

Merchant status (§ 2-104) depends on the transaction: someone who deals in goods of the kind, or who holds themselves out as having knowledge or skill about the practices or goods involved. A plumber who sells a plunger is a merchant for that sale; a plumber selling their own car is not.

  • Buffaloe v. Hart: sale of tobacco barns. An unendorsed check was not a signed memorandum under § 2-201(1). But the buyer's delivery of the check counted as payment under § 2-201(3)(c) even though it was never cashed and was later returned. The buyer's possession, repairs and insurance of the barns also showed acceptance.

Exam framework

  1. Name the category (land, one-year, goods of $500 or more, suretyship, marriage). For one-year, ask whether full performance within a year is possible by the contract's terms.
  2. Writing: run the four-part checklist and combine documents under Crabtree. For the UCC, check for a quantity term and the signature of the party to be charged.
  3. Exceptions:
    • Land: part performance (Burns test vs. Beaver's softer test; Restatement reliance).
    • Any contract: § 139 promissory estoppel (ADP v. Rice; the § 139(2) factors; authority).
    • Goods: the five § 2-201 exceptions. Check merchant status for the confirmation rule, and limit the recovery to the extent of payment or acceptance.
  4. Remedy: part performance usually leads to specific performance; § 139 relief may be limited to reliance.

Hypos (slides)

  • Hypo 1, "One Year or More?": (a) three years at $80,000 a year → within the statute; (b) "as long as the Henderson project lasts, about 18 months" → outside if the project could end within a year; (c) "for life" → traditionally outside, with contrary recent cases.
  • Hypo 2, "Quit Your Job and Come to Us": Maria moves, resigns and turns down another offer based on an oral offer. Apply § 139 and the § 139(2) factors. Was the regional director authorized? That goes to whether her reliance was reasonable.
  • Walter / Total Labs (reply doctrine): goods of $500 or more, and Total Labs never signed. Walter, a chemist, is a merchant for these goods. His email confirmation stated a quantity and was signed, and Total Labs did not object within 10 days. Total Labs is bound.
  • Hypo 3, "The Bunsen Burner Dispute": 500 specialized containers for $25,000, made with Apex's design and logo. Apex's email: "Confirming our call today — 500 containers, $25,000 total." There was a $5,000 deposit. Work through specially manufactured goods, the merchant's confirmation, and partial payment (enforceable only to the extent paid).
