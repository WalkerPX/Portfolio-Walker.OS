The short version

The usual remedy for breach is money that puts the injured side where it would have been if the contract had been performed: the 'benefit of the bargain.'

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Expectation Damages.

What it's about

Contract law's default goal is to give the injured party the benefit of the bargain. Not punishment, and not a windfall. Just the position they expected to be in.

The basic formula

Damages = (value you lost because they didn't perform) + (other losses the breach caused) − (costs you saved by not having to perform)

Common versions:

  • Buyer wasn't delivered goods: cost of buying replacements ("cover") or market price, minus the contract price.
  • Buyer backed out of buying property: contract price minus the property's market value at the time of breach.
  • Employer fired an employee early: remaining salary, minus what the employee earned or reasonably could have earned elsewhere.
  • Defective construction: usually the cost to fix, but if that's wildly out of proportion to the gain, the difference in value instead.

An everyday example

You agree to buy a used car for $10,000. The seller backs out, and the same model now costs $12,000 elsewhere. Your expectation damages are $2,000, the extra you have to pay to get what you bargained for.

The cases, in plain words

  • Crabby's v. Hamilton: buyers backed out of buying commercial property, so the seller recovered the contract price minus the market value at the time of breach.
  • Handicapped Childrens Education Board v. Lukaszewski: a therapist quit mid-year, so she paid the extra cost of the replacement, even though he was more experienced, because no one cheaper was available.
  • Jacob and Youngs v. Kent: when fixing a defect would mean tearing out walls for almost no gain in value, damages are the small difference in value instead.
  • Maness v. Collins: a fired employee got full damages because the employer didn't prove comparable jobs were available.

Words you'll hear

  • Benefit of the bargain: what you would have had if the deal was kept.
  • Cover: buying substitute goods.
  • Cost of completion vs. diminution in value: cost to fix vs. drop in market value.

Limits on these damages are in Limits on Damages (Plain English). Alternatives are in Reliance and Restitution Damages (Plain English) and Specific Performance (Plain English).
