Big picture

  • Four questions for any 1L contract action: (1) Is there a contract? (2) What are its terms? (3) Performed or breach? (4) Remedies for the nonbreaching party.
  • Preliminary question comes first: what law applies?
  • Services → common law (case law). Goods → UCC (statutory).
  • UCC first promulgated 1952; 11 articles. Article 2 governs sale of goods.
  • UCC adopted in all 50 states (whole or large part) → legal certainty for interstate transactions.
  • Article 2 does not apply to every contract — only contracts for the sale of goods.

Doctrine outline

  • Goods (§ 2-105): things identifiable when the contract is formed, and movable (car, computer).
  • Article 2 does not cover services, leases, real estate, or IP on their own.
  • "Sale" (§ 2-106): second threshold question — does the transaction qualify as a sale?
  • Mixed contracts (goods + services): courts look to the predominant purpose of the transaction.
  • § 2-204 liberal formation: contract may form despite open terms or unresolved details.
  • § 2-204(2): binding contract can form even when the precise moment of formation can't be pinned down — acceptance through conduct, unlike classical offer/acceptance.
  • UCC Statute of Frauds: sale of goods ≥ $500 generally must be in writing, unless an exception applies (covered later).

Tests and elements

  • Two threshold questions for Article 2 (checklist):
    • Is the subject matter a "good" under § 2-105?
    • Does the transaction qualify as a "sale" under § 2-106?
  • Predominant Purpose test (mixed goods/services): look to the predominant purpose of the transaction.
  • § 2-204 formation inquiry: did the parties intend to form a contract despite open terms or continued bargaining?
  • UCC Statute of Frauds trigger: goods ≥ $500 → writing required unless exception.

Cases

  • Jannusch v. Naffziger — sale of a food-truck business; mixes goods and intangible assets. Issue: enforceable sales contract under § 2-204 despite unresolved details? Buyers' conduct (took truck, ran business, replaced equipment, paid employees, paid part of price, arranged financing, asked lawyer to draft contract) strongly supported an existing contract with a later writing contemplated.
  • E.C. Styberg Engineering Co. v. Eaton Corp. — contrast to Jannusch: sophisticated parties, extensive written communications, longer-term relationship, millions at stake. Court finds no contract under § 2-204 despite open terms/continued bargaining — even though Styberg had a colorable argument the parties agreed on a minimum quantity. Past practice of concluding prior deals was the strongest evidence they hadn't reached agreement this time.

Examples and hypos

  • Car, computer → goods (movable).
  • Services, leases, real estate, IP on their own → not Article 2.
  • Food-truck business sale (Jannusch) → mixed goods + intangibles; apply predominant purpose.
  • Sophisticated parties with extensive writings and ongoing bargaining (Styberg) → no contract despite colorable minimum-quantity argument.

Exam angles

  • Always ask the threshold question first: common law or UCC?
  • Run the two-question checklist (good? sale?) before applying Article 2.
  • Mixed contract → predominant purpose analysis.
  • Use party conduct as evidence of contract formation (Jannusch) — and past practice as evidence of non-formation (Styberg).
  • § 2-204(2): don't get stuck on the moment of formation; conduct can form a contract.
  • Flag the ≥ $500 writing requirement, but note exceptions are covered later.
