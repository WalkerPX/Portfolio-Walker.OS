The short version

A contract starts when one side makes an offer and the other accepts it. Courts judge this by what a reasonable person would understand from the words and actions, not by what someone secretly intended.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance.

What it's about

"Mutual assent" just means both sides agreed. The usual way to show agreement is an offer followed by an acceptance.

  • An offer says, in effect, "If you say yes, we have a deal." It has to be definite enough that a yes would close the deal.
  • An acceptance is the yes. Under the traditional rule it must match the offer exactly (the "mirror image" rule). A "yes, but…" is usually a counteroffer, which kills the original offer.

The "reasonable person" test

Courts don't read minds. They ask what a reasonable person in the other party's shoes would have understood. If you sign a contract as a joke, but nothing about the situation signals it's a joke, you're bound.

An everyday example

A neighbor says, "I'll sell you my bike for $100." You say "Deal" and hand over the cash. That's an offer and an acceptance. If you had said "How about $80?", that's a counteroffer, and you can't later go back and accept the $100 offer unless the neighbor renews it.

Ads, rewards and changing your mind

  • Ads usually aren't offers. They're invitations to come in and negotiate. An ad can be an offer if it's specific and leaves nothing to negotiate.
  • Rewards and contests can be offers that you accept by doing something (a "unilateral contract").
  • Offers can usually be withdrawn before they're accepted, even if the offeror promised to hold them open, unless the offeree paid for that promise (see Option Contracts and Firm Offers (Plain English)).

The cases, in plain words

  • Lucy v. Zehmer: a farmer signed a deal to sell his farm over drinks and later claimed he was joking. A reasonable buyer would have taken it seriously, so he was bound.
  • Ray v. Eurice Bros: a builder signed a contract without paying attention to which specifications it adopted. Signing means agreeing to what's written.
  • Leonard v. Pepsico: a TV commercial offering a fighter jet for Pepsi Points was obviously a joke, not an offer.
  • Lonergan v. Scolnick: letters answering a buyer's questions about land were just negotiations, not an offer.
  • Izadi v. Machado Ford and Sateriale v. RJ Reynolds: some ads and rewards programs can be real offers.
  • Normile v. Miller: once you learn that the seller has sold the property to someone else, the offer to you is dead.
  • Cook v. Coldwell Banker: once someone has mostly done what a reward-type offer asked, the offeror can't change the rules.

Words you'll hear

  • Offeror / offeree: the person making the offer / the person receiving it.
  • Counteroffer: a response that changes the terms; it rejects the original offer.
  • Unilateral contract: a promise accepted by performing (finish the job), not by promising.
  • Revocation: taking back an offer before it's accepted.
