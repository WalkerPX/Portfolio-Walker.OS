The short version

If something unexpected happens after the deal, nobody's fault, and it makes performance impossible, far more burdensome, or pointless, a party may be excused. But ordinary bad luck and price swings don't count.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Impracticability and Frustration.

What it's about

Contracts allocate risk about the future. These doctrines cover rare cases where an event nobody planned for wrecks the deal. Three related ideas:

  • Impossibility: performance literally can't happen (the concert hall burned down).
  • Impracticability: performance is possible but would cost wildly more than anyone expected, far beyond normal business risk.
  • Frustration of purpose: performance is still possible, but the reason for the deal is gone, and both sides knew that reason.

How a court decides (plain checklist)

  1. An event happened after the contract was made.
  2. Its non-occurrence was a basic assumption of the deal.
  3. It wasn't the fault of the party seeking excuse.
  4. That party didn't take on the risk (by contract terms, or because it was foreseeable and they could have planned for it).
  5. The effect is extreme, not just a loss of profit.

Courts are strict. Price changes and market crashes almost never count. That kind of risk is what fixed-price contracts are for.

An everyday example

You rent a hall for a wedding, and a fire destroys the hall the week before. You're both excused. The hall can't be provided, and its existence was the basis of the deal. But if fuel prices double and the caterer's costs rise 20%, the caterer still has to perform.

The cases, in plain words

  • Taylor v. Caldwell: a music hall burned before the concerts, so both sides were excused.
  • Mineral Park Land v. Howard: gravel below the water line would cost about ten times the normal price to dig out, so the contractor was excused. This is the origin of "impracticability."
  • Krell v. Henry: a room rented to watch a coronation parade. The parade was cancelled, so the renter was excused (frustration of purpose).
  • Lloyd v. Murphy: wartime limits on new car sales didn't excuse a car dealer's lease. The property still had uses and the risk was foreseeable.
  • Mel Frank Tool v. Di-Chem: a new law barred storing hazardous chemicals, but the warehouse still had other uses. No excuse.
  • Hemlock Semiconductor v. Solarworld: a buyer locked into fixed prices wasn't excused when world prices crashed.

Words you'll hear

  • Excuse / discharge: being released from your duty to perform.
  • Force majeure clause: a contract term listing events that excuse performance. It often matters more than these default rules.
