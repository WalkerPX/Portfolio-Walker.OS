In one paragraph

A promise is enforceable as a bargain only if supported by consideration: a performance or return promise that is bargained for, sought by the promisor in exchange for its promise and given by the promisee in exchange for that promise (Restatement (Second) § 71). The older formulation asks for a legal benefit to the promisor or legal detriment to the promisee (Hamer v. Sidway). Courts don't weigh adequacy (Batsakis v. Demotsis, Harris v. Time). Gifts, past consideration, moral obligation and illusory promises fail. Substitutes include Promissory Estoppel and promissory restitution.

Two formulations

  1. Benefit / detriment (traditional): consideration exists if the promisee suffers a legal detriment (does or refrains from doing something it had a legal right to do) or the promisor gets a legal benefit. Detriment alone suffices (Hamer v. Sidway: giving up drinking and smoking).
  2. Bargained-for exchange (Restatement § 71, modern): the performance or promise must be sought in exchange for the promise and given in exchange for it. Holmes: "reciprocal conventional inducement" (Pennsy Supply v. American Ash Recycling).
  • Most courts use both. Run both tests on the exam (existing note).

Gift vs. bargain

  • Conditional gift: an act the promisee must do to receive a gift is not consideration unless the promisor wanted it. Williston's tramp: "go around the corner to the clothing shop" is a condition, not a price.
  • Test: does the condition benefit the promisor? Yes → likely a bargain (Pennsy Supply v. American Ash Recycling: taking the AggRite saved disposal costs). No → likely a gift (Plowman v. Indian Refining: picking up checks; Kirksey v. Kirksey: moving to the brother-in-law's land).
  • Motive vs. consideration (§ 81): love, affection and gratitude are motive (Dougherty v. Salt).
  • Recitals (§ 218): "value received" or "$10 paid" does not create consideration if none was given (Dougherty v. Salt, Berryman v. Kmoch). The exception is the § 87(1)(a) option recital.

Past consideration and moral obligation

  • Past consideration is no consideration: services already performed weren't bargained for (Plowman v. Indian Refining, Mills v. Wyman).
  • Moral obligation isn't consideration (classical: Mills v. Wyman). The modern exception for a material benefit received is § 86 promissory restitution (Webb v. McGowin).
  • Pre-existing legal obligations that are revived by a new promise are enforceable: debts barred by the statute of limitations, debts discharged in bankruptcy, a minor's voidable contracts (Mills v. Wyman Notes 3-6).

Adequacy of consideration

  • Courts do not inquire into adequacy (Batsakis v. Demotsis: 500,000 drachmae for $2,000). Any bargained-for detriment suffices, even trivial (Harris v. Time: opening an envelope).
  • Gross inadequacy may be evidence of fraud, duress, incapacity, undue influence or unconscionability (Harris Note 3).
  • De minimis non curat lex: courts may decline trivial claims (Harris v. Time).
  • Nominal consideration: for options, small amounts work if actually paid (Berryman v. Kmoch).

Illusory promises and mutuality

  • Illusory promise: a promise that leaves performance entirely to the promisor's discretion. It "promises nothing."
  • Conditional promises are not illusory if the promisor is bound when the condition occurs (Marshall Durbin Food v. Baker).
  • Implied promises can save an apparently illusory deal: reasonable efforts in exclusive dealing (Wood v. Lucy Lady Duff-Gordon; UCC § 2-306(2)); good-faith limits on discretion (Locke v. Warner Bros).
  • Mutuality of obligation: "both must be bound or neither is." The modern view (§ 79(c)) says no strict mutuality is required if consideration exists. Mutuality does not apply to unilateral contracts (Sateriale v. RJ Reynolds).
  • Consideration by performance: an at-will employee's continued work (Marshall Durbin Food v. Baker, Cook v. Coldwell Banker).

Exam framework

  1. Identify the promise sought to be enforced.
  2. What did the promisee give (a promise, an act, a forbearance)?
  3. Bargain test: did the promisor seek it in exchange, and did the promisee give it in exchange?
  4. Detriment test: did the promisee give up a legal right?
  5. Red flags: gift condition, motive, recital, past consideration, moral obligation, illusory promise.
  6. If consideration fails: Promissory Estoppel (reliance), § 86 (material benefit), restitution (unjust enrichment).

Hypos (slides)

  • Aunt Phyllis and Ryan: $25,000 not to drink, smoke or gamble until 21, though Ryan doesn't enjoy those anyway. Under Hamer, yes, there is consideration.
  • IU fill dirt: "FREE CLEAN FILL DIRT." IU would pay $400,000 to remove it, and contamination costs ABC $150,000. Under Pennsy, a bargain.
  • Five-part Office hypo: (1) Jim/Pam logo: a bargain. (2) Michael/Erin "best receptionist": a gift (past and motive). (3) Dwight "come pick up the harvester": a gift condition. (4) Dwight "haul it away by Friday," saving $1,000: a bargain. (5) Stanley/Phyllis: apply the same tests.
  • Margaret paralegal pension; Priya scratch-off; Darius / Apex retention bonus in "sole discretion."
