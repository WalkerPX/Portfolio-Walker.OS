Quick reference

Citation: 26 Cal. 4th 261, 109 Cal. Rptr. 2d 807, 27 P.3d 702 (2001) · Casebook: cited in Sateriale (pp. 58-61) and discussed in the Notes after BMW Financial (pp. 757-764) · Topics: Mistake › Unilateral mistake, Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance One line: A car dealer's newspaper ad listing a used Jaguar at a price thousands below the intended price was an offer, but the dealer could rescind for unilateral mistake because enforcing it would be unconscionable.

Coverage

Not reprinted as a principal case. Knapp discusses it in two places, and the slides use it as the source of the four-element unilateral mistake test. Specific figures are [Outside knowledge].

Overview

Donovan does double duty: (1) an advertisement can be an offer (under California's vehicle-advertising statute), and (2) the advertiser may still avoid the contract for unilateral mistake under Restatement § 153(a) when enforcement would be unconscionable.

Parties

  • Plaintiff: Donovan. A consumer who wanted to buy the advertised car at the ad price.
  • Defendant: RRL Corp. (Lexus of Westminster) [Outside knowledge: dealership name]. The dealer whose ad contained a newspaper error.

Facts

  • From Knapp: the dealer made a clerical error in an ad and would have been forced to sell for far below market value.
  • [Outside knowledge]: the newspaper ad listed a used 1995 Jaguar XJ6 at $25,995 instead of $37,995 (a proofreading error). Donovan tried to buy at the advertised price, and the dealer refused once it discovered the mistake.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The municipal court ruled for the dealer. The appellate department reversed. The California Supreme Court reversed, ruling for the dealer.

Issue

Is a dealer's erroneous advertised price an offer that a customer can accept, and if so, can the dealer avoid the resulting contract for unilateral mistake?

Holding

The ad was an offer that Donovan accepted, but the dealer could rescind for unilateral mistake. Enforcement would be unconscionable, and the dealer did not bear the risk of the error.

Reasoning

  • Knapp p. 58: Donovan is quoted for the proposition that an ad can be an offer where it shows assent "to that bargain is invited and will conclude it." California's Vehicle Code required dealers to sell at advertised prices.
  • Unilateral mistake elements (slides; Restatement § 153): (1) a mistake as to a basic assumption; (2) a material adverse effect; (3) the mistaken party does not bear the risk; (4) unconscionable enforcement (or the other party knew or caused it).
  • Negligence did not bar relief. Only gross negligence or a breach of good faith would (§ 157).
  • Knapp (BMW Notes): "unconscionable" here focuses on whether the mistaken party would suffer a substantial out-of-pocket loss. The dealer would have to part with a car far below value. An unrelated third party (the newspaper) caused the error, and Donovan was told promptly.

Rule

A party may avoid a contract for unilateral mistake if the mistake concerns a basic assumption, materially affects the exchange, the mistaken party does not bear the risk, and enforcement would be unconscionable, even if the mistaken party was negligent but not grossly so (Restatement §§ 153, 154, 157).

Why It Matters

  • It is the source of the four-part test applied in BMW Financial (slides).
  • Note 4, reconciling: Donovan got relief because the error was not its own gross fault, it caught it immediately, and it faced a real loss of value. BMW Financial was more at fault and lost only a windfall.
