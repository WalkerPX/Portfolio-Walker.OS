Quick reference

Citation: 382 S.W.2d 198 (Ky. 1964) · Casebook: Knapp p. 67 · Topic: Agreements to Agree and Letters of Intent One line: A lease renewal option leaving rent to be "agreed upon" on a vague comparative basis was too indefinite to enforce, and courts will not set the rent themselves.

Overview

The classical approach to "agreements to agree." If the parties leave a material term (rent) to future agreement without a definite method for fixing it, there is no enforceable contract. Courts will not supply a "reasonable" term.

Court

The Kentucky Court of Appeals was Kentucky's highest court in 1964. [Outside knowledge]

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Keith, the lessee. He held a 10-year lease of a small lot and an option to renew for another 10 years. He wanted a declaration that he had validly renewed, with the rent fixed by the court.
  • Defendants / appellants: Walker, the lessors. They disputed the renewal and the rent.

Facts

  • July 1951: a 10-year lease at $100/month, with an option to extend for 10 more years "under the same terms and conditions except as to rental."
  • Renewal clause: "rental will be fixed in such amount as shall actually be agreed upon by the lessors and the lessee with the monthly rental fixed on the comparative basis of rental values as of the date of the renewal with rental values at this time reflected by the comparative business conditions of the two periods."
  • The lessee gave proper notice. The parties could not agree on rent.

Procedural History

  • Declaratory judgment action. On an advisory jury's verdict, the Chancellor fixed the new rent at $125/month and granted relief to the lessee.
  • The lessors appealed. Reversed.

Issue

Is a lease renewal option enforceable when it leaves the rent to future agreement, guided only by a vague "comparative" standard, with no agreed method such as arbitration?

Holding

No. The renewal provision was "fatally defective in failing to specify either an agreed rental or an agreed method by which it could be fixed with certainty." The option was illusory, and the Chancellor erred in enforcing it.

Reasoning

  • Rent is a material term of a lease. Contract law requires "substantial certainty as to the material terms upon which the minds of the parties have met."
  • Decisions elsewhere are in "hopeless conflict." Some courts supply a reasonable rent. The court declines to follow them.
  • The clause's standard ("comparative business conditions") does not supply a workable formula. Determining it would require extensive judicial fact-finding.
  • Judicial role: fixing rent for the parties would be a kind of "judicial paternalism" that should be "as obnoxious to courts as ... legislation by judicial fiat." Courts are not equipped to make such business decisions.
  • A renewal option stands on the same footing as any other contract right.

Rule

An agreement that leaves an essential term to future agreement, without an objective standard or agreed mechanism (e.g., arbitration, appraisal, a formula) for fixing it, is too indefinite to enforce. Courts will not make the contract for the parties.

Why It Matters

  • It is the classical anchor for indefiniteness and agreements to agree.
  • Contrast the modern trend: UCC gap-fillers (§ 2-204(3), § 2-305 open price) and courts willing to supply a reasonable rent, often because the tenant reasonably and materially relied on an apparent assurance of renewal (Note 3). Refusing to fix a rate can read the renewal clause out of the contract entirely.
  • Lesson for drafting: include a definite method (formula, appraisal, arbitration).

Other Key Points

  • Note 1: do you accept the court's view that rate-setting is "paternalism"?
  • Note 2: two reasons for refusing: (1) the cost and time of judicial fact-finding, (2) paternalism when the parties could have resolved it themselves.
  • Note 3: other courts go the other way, often on reliance.
  • Hypo (slides): Michael's coffee shop renewal at $4,000/mo after a $200,000 renovation, with comparables at $5,500. Is there reliance? Would a court supply rent?
