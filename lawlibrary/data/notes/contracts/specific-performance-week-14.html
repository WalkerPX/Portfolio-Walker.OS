Big picture

  • Specific performance = court orders the breaching party to perform the contract.
  • Fundamental limit: money damages must be inadequate. If damages can easily make the innocent party whole, specific performance cannot be awarded.
  • Three limitation types courts weigh:
    1. Inadequacy of money damages.
    2. Equitable factors (hardship on breaching party; timely pursuit of the claim).
    3. Practical difficulties in shaping and enforcing the decree.
  • Modern courts (e.g., City Stores) are more willing to grant specific performance than historically.
  • UCC loosened the "unique goods" rule but kept the inadequacy requirement.

Doctrine outline

  • Inadequacy of damages — historical major limitation. Plaintiff must show damages would be inadequate.
  • Equitable factors — hardship on breaching party; whether plaintiff timely pursued the claim; defendant's receipt of benefit from plaintiff's performance; plaintiff's reliance/change of position.
  • Practical limitations — court must be able to shape and supervise an enforceable decree.
  • Personal services contracts — traditional rule: no affirmative specific performance (difficulty of compulsion; looks like involuntary servitude).
  • UCC (Note 7) — specific performance allowed where goods are unique or "in other proper circumstances" (e.g., tracking down a replacement entails considerable burden). Inadequacy remains a barrier; if goods are readily available on the market, specific performance will almost certainly be denied.

Tests and elements

  • Checklist — threshold test: damages inadequate? If not, no specific performance.
  • Checklist — equitable factors:
    • Hardship on breaching party.
    • Timely pursuit of the claim.
    • Defendant received benefit from plaintiff's performance.
    • Plaintiff changed position in reliance on defendant's promise.
  • Checklist — practical limitations:
    • Is the agreement sufficiently definite?
    • Can the court supervise the decree?
    • Can the court shape an enforceable order?
  • Checklist — UCC: goods unique or "in other proper circumstances"; readily available goods → deny.

Cases

  • City Stores Co. v. Ammerman (p. 1068) — Court orders defendants to specifically perform agreement to lease plaintiff a department store in their shopping center on terms at least as favorable as those given other major department stores. Rejects defendants' indefiniteness, supervision, and hardship arguments. Considers all three limitation types.
    • Note 1: Definiteness satisfied — standard lease provisions predictable in the trade; other major tenants' leases supply material terms. Court more willing where equitable factors present: defendants used plaintiff's letter to obtain zoning approval (benefit received); plaintiff substantially relied by forgoing a comparable opportunity in another shopping center (supported Tyson's Corner project). Restitutionary, reliance, and expectation interests all affected.
    • Note 2: Damages inadequate — plaintiff would face difficult if not impossible task proving lost profits many years out; damages would not compensate "almost incalculable future advantages" from extending operations into the suburbs (p. 1073).
    • Notes 4–5: Equitable factors include defendants' profit from plaintiff's intervention and plaintiff's change of position in reliance. Counterfactual: if defendants had already signed binding leases with independent parties for all department store spaces, specific relief probably would not be granted — court unlikely to order mall expansion or force breach of existing leases.
    • Note 7: UCC liberalization (see Doctrine outline).
  • Reier Broadcasting Co., Inc. v. Kramer (p. 1079) — named only; deals with availability of equitable relief against breach of personal service contracts — now briefed: Reier Broadcasting v. Kramer.

Examples and hypos

  • City Stores hypothetical (Notes 4–5): defendants had bound themselves to independent parties for all department store spaces → specific relief probably denied; no order to expand mall; no decree forcing breach of existing leases.
  • UCC example: tracking down a replacement good entails considerable burden → supports specific performance.
  • UCC counterexample: goods readily available on the market → specific performance almost certainly denied.

Exam angles

  • Run the three limitations in order: inadequacy → equitable factors → practical difficulties.
  • Argue inadequacy via difficult/impossible lost-profit proof and incalculable future advantages (City Stores).
  • Argue definiteness using trade-standard terms and comparable leases (City Stores Note 1).
  • Weigh reliance and restitution interests — they strengthen the equitable case (City Stores).
  • Check for a hardship defense and whether plaintiff timely pursued the claim.
  • Personal services contract? Traditional rule bars affirmative specific performance.
  • UCC sale of goods? Apply "unique or other proper circumstances," then still test inadequacy.
