Big picture

  • Certain contracts must be in writing to be enforceable.
  • Two regimes: common-law SOF categories (suretyship, marriage, land, one-year, goods ≥ $500) and UCC §2-201 for goods.
  • UCC keeps the signed-writing requirement but expands the exceptions.
  • Purpose of the writing requirement: prevent fabrication of contracts where none existed.
  • Recurring exam move: (1) does SOF apply? (2) is there a sufficient signed writing? (3) does an exception apply?

Doctrine outline

Common-law categories (must be in writing)

  1. Suretyship — a guaranty (promise to pay the legally enforceable debt of another).
  2. Marriage provision — a promise for which the consideration is marriage (or a promise of marriage).
  3. Land contract — a promise to transfer an interest in land (not just sales; also mortgages and leases).
  4. One-year provision — a contract which cannot possibly be performed within a year of its making.
  5. Sale of goods — a contract for the sale of goods for $500 or more.

One-year provision — rules

  • If there is any possibility, no matter how small, that the contract can be completed in a year, it need not be in writing.
  • Contract is within the statute only if by its express terms it cannot be performed in less than one year.
  • Contracts of no duration or indefinite duration are NOT within the statute.
  • Lifetime employment: traditionally not subject to SOF because it can be fully performed within one year (employee could die within a year). Some recent decisions treat such contracts as contracts for a term of years → must be in writing.
  • Performance vs. termination: if the contract can be completed by performance within one year, not subject to SOF. The fact that a contract may be brought to an end within one year by breach or excuse does NOT remove it from the statute — any contract may be terminated by breach or excuse within a year.

Elements of the writing (common law)

  • There must be a signed writing.
  • The signed writing must:
    1. Reasonably identify the subject matter of the contract.
    2. Indicate a contract has been made between the parties.
    3. State with reasonable certainty the essential terms of the contract.
    4. Be signed by or on behalf of the party to be charged.

Linking multiple documents (Crabtree)

  • Two documents may be combined if the documents taken together have all the essential terms.
  • At least one document is signed by the party to be charged.
  • The unsigned document shows on its face that it relates to the same transaction.
  • The evidence shows assent to the unsigned writing.
  • The unsigned document need not contain an explicit reference to the signed document.
  • Parol evidence may be introduced to show assent to the unsigned document.

Part performance exception (real estate)

  • Earliest exception English courts developed to the statute.
  • Traditional American test: "unequivocally referable" test (Cardozo, Burns v. McCormick).
  • Typically part performance = promisee's taking possession coupled with making valuable improvements.
  • Beaver uses the "unequivocally referable" language but rejects a narrow reading: performance need not relate exclusively to the oral contract; sufficient if it would lead an outsider to "naturally and reasonably" conclude a contract exists.
  • Restatement does not use "unequivocally referable"; focuses on whether promisee's reliance was reasonable and whether justice requires specific enforcement (Note 2).

Promissory estoppel exception

  • First Restatement: exception only where party (1) misrepresented that a writing had been executed or (2) promised to execute a signed writing — required fraudulent conduct.
  • ADP v. Rice does not insist on that factor; applies Restatement (Second) §139.
  • §139: detrimental reliance may be sufficient to enforce a promise of performance even absent either a writing or an express promise to create a writing.
  • §139(2) lists factors a court should consider (slides say "see §139(2)" — factors not listed).

UCC §2-201 — sale of goods

  • Applies to contracts for the sale of goods for $500 or more.
  • Writing must: indicate a contract was formed, contain a quantity term, be signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought.
  • Other terms, including price, can be supplied by course of performance, course of dealing, trade usage, or Code gap-fillers.
  • If oral deal or insufficient writing, check the five exceptions.

Article 2 scope — two questions

  1. Is the subject matter a "good" under §2-105? Requires "tangible moveability" and identifiability. Examples: parts, products, food, equipment, pens, boats, computers, cars, animals.
  2. Is the transaction a "sale" under §2-106? Seller transfers ownership to buyer and buyer pays. Gifts, leases, and bailments are NOT sales.
    • Gift of goods → no sale.
    • Lease of goods → no sale (title/ownership does not pass).
    • Bailment of goods → no sale (e.g., drop clothes off at laundromat).

Hybrid transactions

  • Most courts apply the predominant purpose test: in a transaction involving goods and services, determine whether it was predominantly a sale of goods with services incidental, or a contract for services with transfer of goods incidental.

The five UCC exceptions

  1. Reply doctrine / written confirmation / between-merchants (§2-201(2)).
  2. Admissions (§2-201(3)(b)).
  3. Specially manufactured goods (§2-201(3)(a)).
  4. Partial payment (§2-201(3)(c)).
  5. Partial delivery / partial acceptance (§2-201(3)(c)).

Reply doctrine (§2-201(2))

  • Deems a letter of confirmation or confirmatory memo a reliable method of satisfying SOF.
  • Effect: takes away from the party who fails to answer the SOF defense.
  • If an oral agreement is reached and one party sends a written statement confirming it, the other party has ten days to object in writing or the agreement is enforceable.
  • Requirements: (a) contract must be between merchants; (b) receiving party must not object in writing within ten days after receipt.

Merchant status (§2-104)

  • Merchant if: (1) deals in goods that are the subject matter of the transaction, or (2) holds himself out as having knowledge or skill peculiar to the practices or goods involved.
  • Category 1: local shopkeeper, car dealer, department store sales clerk.
  • Category 2: plumber who sells a customer a plunger; photographer who sells a client a camera.
  • Plumber selling his car is NOT a merchant for that car sale; photographer selling his sofa is NOT a merchant for that sofa sale.

Admissions (§2-201(3)(b))

  • Party against whom enforcement is sought cannot admit the contract in court pleadings and simultaneously claim the benefit of the statute.
  • One who admits — in pleadings, testimony, or otherwise — in court that a contract was made is bound, up to the quantity of goods admitted.

Specially manufactured goods (§2-201(3)(a))

  • Exempted from writing requirement if: (a) goods were specially manufactured for the buyer; AND (b) they are not suitable for sale to others in the ordinary course of the seller's business.
  • Examples: monogram shirts; 100K bags with buyer's corporate name and logo.

Partial payment (§2-201(3)(c))

  • Absent a writing, an otherwise valid contract is enforceable to the extent payment has been made and accepted.

Partial delivery / partial performance (§2-201(3)(c))

  • Absent a writing, enforceable with respect to goods received and accepted.
  • 50 of 100 goods delivered and accepted → contract for those 50 goods.
  • Sale of 100 units for $1,000, buyer accepts 10 units → pays $100.

Tests and elements

Common-law signed writing — checklist

  • Signed writing exists
  • Reasonably identifies subject matter
  • Indicates a contract has been made between the parties
  • States essential terms with reasonable certainty
  • Signed by or on behalf of the party to be charged

Crabtree document-linking — checklist

  • Documents together contain all essential terms
  • At least one document signed by party to be charged
  • Unsigned document shows on its face it relates to same transaction
  • Evidence shows assent to the unsigned writing
  • (No explicit cross-reference required; parol evidence admissible to show assent)

One-year provision — checklist

  • Contract cannot possibly be performed within one year by its express terms
  • Any possibility of completion within a year → outside statute
  • Indefinite/no-duration contracts → outside statute
  • Termination by breach/excuse within a year does NOT bring contract within statute

UCC §2-201 analysis — checklist

  • Contract for sale of goods ≥ $500?
  • Sufficient writing? (indicates contract formed + quantity term + signed by party against whom enforcement sought)
  • If not, does one of the five exceptions apply?

Reply doctrine — checklist

  • Contract between merchants
  • Written confirmation sent
  • Receiving party did not object in writing within ten days

Specially manufactured goods — checklist

  • Goods specially manufactured for the buyer
  • Not suitable for sale to others in ordinary course of seller's business

Part performance (real estate) — checklist

  • Possession + valuable improvements (typical)
  • Performance would lead outsider to "naturally and reasonably" conclude a contract exists (Beaver)
  • Restatement alternative: reliance reasonable + justice requires specific enforcement

Promissory estoppel (§139) — checklist

  • Detrimental reliance
  • Reliance reasonable — including reasonable belief the promisor was authorized
  • §139(2) factors considered (list not on slides)

Cases

  • Crabtree v. Elizabeth Arden Sales Corp. — two documents may be linked to satisfy the writing requirement (rule detailed above).
  • Burns v. McCormick (pp. 350, 355 per the casebook index; p. 365 was a mis-cite) — Cardozo; source of the "unequivocally referable" test. Named only — now briefed: Burns v. McCormick.
  • Beaver v. Brumlow — part performance exception to real estate SOF; uses "unequivocally referable" language but rejects narrow interpretation; "naturally and reasonably" conclude a contract exists.
  • Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice — promissory estoppel overcomes lack of writing; reliance must be reasonable, including reasonable belief the person assuring employment was authorized; applies Restatement (Second) §139 rather than requiring fraudulent conduct.
  • Buffaloe v. Hart — check never endorsed, no other signed writing → §2-201(1) memorandum requirement not satisfied; but §2-201(3)(c) "payment" satisfied by receipt of check even though never cashed and later returned; also evidence supported "acceptance" of tobacco barns by possession, improvements, insurance, conduct of apparent ownership.

Examples and hypos

Hypo 1 — "One Year or More?"

  • (a) Three years at $80,000/year → cannot be performed within one year → must be in writing.
  • (b) "As long as the Henderson project lasts — estimated 18 months" → if project could theoretically end within a year (e.g., 6 months), outside the statute.
  • (c) "For life" → traditional rule: not within statute (could die within a year); some recent decisions treat as term of years → writing required.
  • Distinction: performance vs. termination.

Hypo 2 — "Quit Your Job and Come to Us"

  • Maria relocates, resigns, turns down competing offer based on oral offer; company says never formalized.
  • Analyze: SOF bar? Promissory estoppel under §139? §139(2) factors? Was regional director authorized — goes to reasonableness of reliance.

Reply doctrine hypo — Walter/Total Labs

  • Answer: YES, Total Labs liable.
  • (1) Writing needed — sale of goods ≥ $500.
  • (2) No sufficient writing — Total Labs never signed.
  • (3) Exception applies — reply doctrine: Walter is a merchant (chemist — goods related to profession/training); email is confirmatory memo containing quantity and signed; Total Labs did not object in writing within 10 days.

Hypo 3 — "The Bunsen Burner Dispute"

  • 500 specialized containers, $25,000, no signed contract; BioSupply begins manufacturing with Apex's design/logo; Apex cancels; Apex email: "Confirming our call today — 500 containers, $25,000 total."
  • Analyze: SOF applies? Specially manufactured goods exception (both elements)? Reply doctrine independently (each requirement)? Effect of $5,000 deposit — partial payment interacting with other exceptions.

Exam angles

  • Always run the three-step frame: SOF applies? Sufficient writing? Exception?
  • One-year: ask whether contract by its express terms cannot be performed within one year; any possibility → outside statute.
  • Distinguish performance from termination — breach/excuse termination does not trigger SOF.
  • Lifetime employment: flag both the traditional rule and the recent contrary decisions.
  • Multiple documents: apply Crabtree checklist; parol evidence admissible to show assent.
  • Real estate part performance: apply Beaver's softened "naturally and reasonably" standard; note Restatement alternative.
  • Promissory estoppel: apply §139; reasonableness of reliance includes authority of promisor.
  • UCC: confirm good + sale first; use predominant purpose test for hybrids.
  • UCC writing: quantity term is essential; price can be gap-filled.
  • Run all five UCC exceptions; note partial payment and partial delivery enforce only to the extent of payment/acceptance.
  • Reply doctrine: merchant status on both sides + no written objection within 10 days.
  • Merchant status is transaction-specific (plumber's car, photographer's sofa).
