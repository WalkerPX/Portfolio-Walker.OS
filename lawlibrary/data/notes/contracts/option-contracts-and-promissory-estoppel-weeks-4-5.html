Big picture

  • Traditional formation needs offer, acceptance, consideration. Today: enforce obligations even when a formation element is missing.
  • Two doctrines: (1) option contracts — consideration keeps an offer irrevocable; (2) promissory estoppel / § 87(2) — reasonable pre-acceptance reliance can bind an offer.
  • Also: every contract implies a duty of good faith / fair dealing.
  • Promissory estoppel is not a substitute for consideration — it is an alternative basis for enforcement.
  • Courts police injustice; they do not enforce promises wholesale.

Doctrine outline

Option contracts

  • Arises when the offeror's power of revocation is bargained away for consideration.
  • Purpose: let a party investigate a deal without fear that delay forfeits acceptance.
  • Offeror keeps the consideration even if the option lapses without acceptance.
  • Consideration must be real, not just recited (Berryman).
  • Nominal consideration: even a very small amount works if actually paid.
  • Consideration need not be money — property or services work if bargained for.
  • Courts are more lenient on nominal consideration when the issue is merely keeping an offer open (vs. consideration for substantial return performance).
  • Some courts: a recital suffices even if not paid — promise to pay implied. Berryman rejects this.
  • Mailbox rule generally does NOT apply to acceptance of an option — effective only when received.
  • A revocable offer still binds if accepted before effective revocation. Indirect revocation (e.g., learning of a sale) terminates the power to accept — compare Normile v. Miller.

Promissory estoppel (§ 90)

  • Enforcement without traditional consideration when reliance makes injustice unavoidable.
  • Reliance must be reasonable — promisee's subjective belief does not control.
  • Remedy may be limited: reliance damages instead of full expectation damages.

§ 87(2) / offer estoppel

  • Reasonable pre-acceptance reliance can make an offer binding (Drennan; codified in Restatement (Second) § 87(2)).
  • Some call this "offer estoppel."

Tests and elements

Restatement (Second) § 90 — four elements (checklist):

  1. A promise
  2. Promisor should reasonably expect it to induce reliance
  3. Promise does induce definite and substantial reliance
  4. Injustice can be avoided only by enforcement

Option contract (checklist):

  • Offeror's power of revocation bargained away
  • Real consideration actually paid (not merely recited)
  • Nominal amount OK if paid; property/services OK if bargained for

Drennan limitations — know all four (checklist):

  1. Offer expressly or by clear implication allowing revocation before acceptance is NOT protected
  2. Offeree may not "shop around" or reopen negotiations for a better bid
  3. Bona fide mistake known to the offeree → bid not enforceable
  4. Mere estimates (not formal bids) not enforceable

Cases

  • Berryman v. Kmoch (p. 254) — 120-day option recited "$10 and other valuable consideration"; $10 never paid. No enforceable option — no consideration actually paid. Promissory estoppel rejected: reliance "could not have been reasonably expected." Court declined Restatement § 87(1)(a) "purported consideration" rule. TM critique: Kmoch's efforts were not bargained for as the price of the option.
  • James Baird Co. v. Gimbel Bros., Inc. (p. 262) — Sub's bid used in general bid; sub revoked before formal acceptance. Hand rejects (1) unilateral contract (use not "acceptance"), (2) promissory estoppel (applies to donative promises, not commercial offers contemplating exchange), (3) option (no consideration for irrevocability). Revocation effective; no remedy. Minority view.
  • Drennan v. Star Paving Co. (p. 265) — Traynor: promissory estoppel makes sub's bid irrevocable. (1) § 45 by analogy; (2) sub should have realized general would rely; (3) sub had financial interest in the reliance — not an idle offer. Star bound; Drennan recovers difference between Star's bid and next lowest sub. Majority view; § 87(2) follows Drennan.
  • Pop's Cones, Inc. v. Resorts International Hotel, Inc. (p. 272) — Resorts assured "the deal is done"; Pop's declined lease renewal, paid deposits, ordered equipment; Resorts gave space to TCBY. NJ no longer requires a "clear and definite" promise; assurances sufficient. Pop's seeks reliance damages only; Note 4: expectation damages also provable.
  • Kirksey v. Kirksey (p. 215) — Uncle's promise of a place to live; sister-in-law sold land and moved; later evicted. 1845 court: no enforcement — "mere gratuity." Slides' explanations: judicial reluctance in family disputes; unwillingness to let property pass from male to female. Modern courts likely different under § 90.
  • Normile v. Miller — named only (compared on indirect revocation) — now briefed: Normile v. Miller.

Examples and hypos

Hypo 1 — "I'll Keep It Open for 30 Days" — Written option, "$1 paid," never paid; Buyer spends $15,000; Seller revokes Day 25; Buyer accepts Day 28. Questions: enforceable? does unpaid $1 matter? does $15,000 support § 87(2)? what changes under § 87(1)(a)? what should Buyer have done?

Hypo 2 — "The Low Bid" — FastFloor bids $180,000; ABC uses it and wins; FastFloor claims math error, needs $230,000; ABC sues for $50,000. Questions: apply Drennan; effect of math error / ABC's knowledge; effect of "estimate, not a firm bid" language; effect of ABC shopping around.

Hypo 3 — "It's Basically Yours" — Landlord: "the space is basically yours"; tenant declines renewal, spends $22,000 fitting out; landlord leases elsewhere. Questions: enforceable promise and standard; stronger/weaker than Pop's Cones; expectation vs. reliance damages; what tenant should have done.

Hypo 4 (9-9 deck) — "Move Here and I'll Take Care of You" — Uncle's promise; Niece quits job, moves states, two years; support stops. Questions: enforceable? reliance present — why doesn't that end analysis? which element is hardest?

Exam angles

  • Option: was consideration actually paid? Recital alone fails under Berryman; § 87(1)(a) may save it in other courts.
  • Nominal consideration: small amount OK if paid; leniency greater when merely keeping offer open.
  • Option acceptance: no mailbox rule — receipt required.
  • Race to accept: acceptance before effective revocation wins; indirect revocation cuts off power (Normile).
  • Sub-bid cases: Baird (minority) vs. Drennan (majority, § 87(2)). Run the four Drennan limitations.
  • § 90: run all four elements; stress reasonableness and definite/substantial reliance.
  • Remedy: reliance vs. expectation — Pop's sought reliance only, but expectation was provable.
  • "Clear and definite promise": NJ (Pop's) doesn't require it; many jurisdictions still do — know your jurisdiction.
  • Good faith: Pop's as paradigm "duty to negotiate in good faith" case — all material terms agreed, walked away without reason.
