Quick reference

Citation: 313 N.C. 98, 326 S.E.2d 11 (N.C. 1985) · Casebook: Knapp p. 39 · Topic: Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance One line: "You snooze, you lose": once the offeree learns the property has been sold to someone else, the offer is revoked and can no longer be accepted.

Overview

The course case on counteroffers and revocation. A conditional or modified "acceptance" is a counteroffer that rejects the original offer. An offer can be revoked indirectly, when the offeree reliably learns the offeror has taken definite action inconsistent with the offer (e.g., sold the property).

Parties

  • Plaintiffs / appellants: Normile and Kurniawan. Prospective buyers of a house in Charlotte. They wanted to enforce a purchase contract.
  • Defendant: Hazel Miller. The owner and seller. She wanted to be free to sell to someone else.
  • Plaintiff-appellee / intervenor: Segal. A second buyer who signed a binding contract with Miller. (Brokers: Gladys Hawkins, listing agent; Richard Byer, Gallery of Homes, the buyers' agent.)

Facts

  • August 4, 1980: Normile and Kurniawan signed a written offer on a form. It said "Time is of the essence, therefore this offer must be accepted on or before 5:00 p.m. Aug. 5th 1980."
  • That evening Miller signed the form under seal, but changed several terms: earnest money $100 → $500, down payment $875 → $1,000, mortgage $18,525 → $18,000, loan term 25 → 20 years, plus a buyer-qualification contingency. This was a counteroffer.
  • Byer delivered the counteroffer. Normile neither accepted nor rejected it. Byer believed Normile thought he had a "first option" and could wait.
  • August 5: Miller accepted Segal's offer, a binding contract.
  • That afternoon Byer told Normile: "You snooze, you lose; the property has been sold." Later that afternoon, before 5:00 p.m., the plaintiffs initialed the counteroffer and delivered it with the $500 deposit.

Procedural History

  • The plaintiffs sued for specific performance. Summary judgment went for the defendants. The Court of Appeals affirmed (on reasoning the Supreme Court modified).
  • The Supreme Court of North Carolina modified and affirmed.

Issue

  1. Did the 5:00 p.m. deadline in the buyers' offer make the seller's counteroffer irrevocable until then?
  2. Was the counteroffer revoked before the plaintiffs tried to accept it?

Holding

  1. No. The time limit belonged to the buyers' original offer. It did not carry over to bind the seller's counteroffer as an option. The seller's counteroffer was revocable at any time before acceptance, because no consideration made it an option contract.
  2. Yes. The counteroffer was revoked indirectly when Normile learned from Byer that the property had been sold. The later attempt to accept was ineffective, and at most was a new offer that Miller did not accept.

Reasoning

  • Counteroffer = rejection + new offer. Miller's changes made her signature a counteroffer, which terminated the buyers' original offer. A reply that adds conditions is not an acceptance (mirror-image rule).
  • No option. An offer stays open for a stated time only if consideration is given for that promise (an option contract). None was given here, so Miller could revoke.
  • Indirect revocation (Restatement (Second) § 43): an offeree's power of acceptance ends when the offeror takes definite action inconsistent with the offer and the offeree acquires reliable information of it (citing Farnsworth and Dickinson v. Dodds).
  • Byer's statement was reliable information, so the power to accept was gone (§ 36). The plaintiffs' initialing was "a new offer," never accepted, because Miller had already contracted with Segal.

Rule

  • A purported acceptance that changes terms is a counteroffer, which rejects the original offer.
  • An ordinary offer is revocable until accepted, even if it states a deadline, unless consideration (or a statute or reliance doctrine) makes it irrevocable.
  • Indirect revocation: an offer is revoked when the offeree learns from a reliable source that the offeror has taken definite action inconsistent with the offer.

Why It Matters

  • It is a core formation timeline case: offer, counteroffer, revocation, attempted acceptance.
  • Slides (Week 1): "cannot accept after notice of revocation; conditional acceptance is counteroffer." Note 3 introduces option contracts.
  • Later weeks compare it: GOC Problem 2-2 (an immediate "not an offer" notice may act like Normile's revocation) and Berryman v. Kmoch (indirect revocation of an option that lacked consideration).

Other Key Points

  • Firm offers under UCC § 2-205 (merchants, signed writing) are an exception to free revocability, but this is a land sale, not goods. See UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation.
  • Contrast reliance-based irrevocability: Drennan v. Star Paving and Restatement (Second) § 87(2).
  • Exam trap: a deadline alone ("must be accepted by 5 p.m.") limits the offeree's time. It does not promise to keep the offer open.
