Big picture

  • Traditional view: a promise is not enforceable unless supported by consideration.
  • Traditional definition: "benefit to the promisor" or "detriment to the promisee."
  • Restatement definition (also common): consideration = "bargained-for exchange" — one party's performance is the "price" of the other's.
  • In commercial transactions, consideration is almost always satisfied (goods for money).
  • Mutuality of consideration: each party must furnish consideration to the other. If A's promise lacks consideration from B, neither is bound.
  • Modern law (Restatement (Second) § 79(c)) does not generally require strict mutuality of obligation — but some courts still use it, mainly to guard against illusory promises.
  • Courts generally do not weigh the relative value exchanged or police bargains for fairness — a genuine bargain is enough, even if trivial (Harris).
  • Knowledge of these rules is often more useful for avoiding disputes than resolving them (counselor's role).

Doctrine outline

Traditional benefit/detriment

  • Ask: did the promisor receive a benefit, or did the promisee suffer a detriment?
  • Hamer: detriment to promisee alone suffices; no separate benefit to promisor needed.

Bargained-for exchange (Restatement)

  • Performance must be part of a bargained-for exchange; one party's performance is the "price" of the other's.
  • Pennsy: consideration requires more than benefit/detriment — the detriment must be a "quid pro quo," bargained for and given in exchange for the promise.
  • Bargain theory does not require actual haggling — only "reciprocal conventional inducement" (Holmes).

Consideration vs. condition to a gift

  • Test: does the action benefit the promisor? If yes → consideration. If no → mere condition on a gift.
  • Pennsy: Pennsy's assumption of disposal responsibility was consideration, not a condition attached to a gift.
  • Plowman: coming in to pick up checks = condition on a gift, not consideration ("tramp" analysis).

Gift / motive distinction

  • A promise to make a gift is not enforceable (Dougherty).
  • Affection and good behavior generally, without more, are motive, not bargained-for consideration (Restatement § 81).
  • A "recital" of consideration in a document does not itself establish consideration (Restatement § 218).

Illusory promises

  • An illusory promise promises nothing — leaves performance entirely optional.
  • A conditional promise is not illusory (Marshall Durbin).
  • Marshall Durbin: employer's promise of post-employment compensation, though conditional, was not illusory; Baker gave consideration through performance (continuing to work), not a promise.

Inadequacy of consideration

  • Inadequacy alone is not enough to make an agreement unenforceable (Harris Note 1).
  • Extreme imbalance can invalidate — but usually only alongside other red flags: fraud, duress, incapacity, undue influence (Harris Note 3).
  • De minimis non curat lex: the law disregards trifles (Harris).

Revocation in unilateral contracts

  • Classical theory: offer can be revoked before performance is complete — no contract (Hamer Variation (b)).
  • Modern theory (Restatement § 45): once performance begins, an option contract forms, keeping the offer open despite attempted revocation — especially under Cook's "substantial performance" approach.

Objective theory

  • Question is not subjective intent but whether the promisee reasonably understood the promise was serious (Hamer Variation (a)).

Cases

  • Hamer v. Sidway — Uncle promised nephew $5,000 to refrain from drinking, smoking, swearing, gambling until 21. Court finds clear detriment to nephew (giving up legal rights); no separate benefit to uncle needed (though one may be present — peace of mind, family reputation).
  • Pennsy Supply v. American Ash Recycling Corp. — Free AggRite turned out hazardous; Pennsy incurred $385,000+ in cleanup. Holding: consideration requires bargained-for quid pro quo; American Ash promoted AggRite to induce contractors to take on disposal and cut its own costs — bargained-for detriment. Bargain theory = "reciprocal conventional inducement" (Holmes), no actual haggling required.
  • Dougherty v. Salt — Aunt Tillie's $3,000 promissory note to nephew "because you have been a good boy." Holding: promise to make a gift is not enforceable; affection/good behavior = motive, not consideration.
  • Plowman v. Indian Refining Co. — Lifetime half-wage payments to longtime employees. Court rejects past services (not bargained for), moral obligation (not legal consideration), and picking up checks (condition on a gift, not consideration). Note 4: manager Anglin may have lacked authority to bind company — agency problem.
  • Harris v. Time, Inc. — Free calculator watch for opening mailer. Holding: opening the mailer was consideration; contract formed. But unenforceable under de minimis non curat lex. Courts won't weigh relative value or police fairness.
  • Marshall Durbin Food Corp. v. Baker — No fixed duration, but employer promised post-employment compensation if Baker stayed until triggering events. Holding: promise not illusory though conditional; Baker gave consideration through performance (continuing to work); employer got real benefit. Promise enforceable. Note 2: legal detriment mirrors Hamer — Baker gave up right to leave at any time.
  • Batsakis v. Demotsis — named only (Note 2 to Harris) — now briefed: Batsakis v. Demotsis. Traditional "gross inadequacy" case; compare/distinguish from Harris.

Examples and hypos

  • Aunt Phyllis / Ryan: $25,000 to abstain from drinking, smoking, gambling until 21. Ryan abstains though he doesn't enjoy those activities. Phyllis argues no harm, no benefit. Issue: is there consideration? (Apply Hamer — detriment = giving up legal right; benefit need not be shown.)
  • IU free fill dirt: IU posts "FREE CLEAN FILL DIRT," would otherwise pay $400,000 to remove. ABC takes 2,000 tons; dirt contaminated, $150,000 cleanup. IU argues no contract, dirt was free. Issue: was there consideration? (Apply Pennsy — bargained-for quid pro quo / benefit to promisor.)
  • Five-party hypo (The Office):
    1. Jim promises Pam $500 to design a logo.
    2. Michael tells Erin she's the best receptionist, "I'm giving you $500 next month."
    3. Dwight tells Angela, "I'll give you my old beet harvester if you come to Schrute Farms and pick it up."
    4. Dwight needs to pay $1,000 to dispose of harvester; tells Angela, "If you haul this away by Friday, it's yours."
    5. Stanley tells Phyllis, "I'll buy you lunch if you'll sit with me because I don't want to eat alone."
  • Margaret the paralegal: 28 years' service; founding partner promises $2,000/month for life "you've earned it." Payments for 8 months, then terminated (no authority; no consideration). Q: Is 28 years' service consideration? Any other theory? Fact twist: promise conditioned on coming in every Friday to help new team — now consideration? (Apply Plowman; compare Marshall Durbin.)
  • Grocery scratch-off mailer: "You've won a $500 gift card!" Fine print: odds 1 in 50,000. Priya scratches, drives to store, told not a winner. Q: Contract? Consideration? Does fine print matter? (Apply Harris.)
  • Apex / Darius retention bonus: $50,000 if he stays through 18-month launch and doesn't resign; offer letter says at-will termination. Apex argues illusory. Q: Is promise illusory? Consideration? Enforceable? Fact twist: "Apex may pay at its sole discretion" — now illusory? (Apply Marshall Durbin.)

Exam angles

  • Run the traditional test (benefit/detriment) and the Restatement test (bargained-for exchange) separately; say which the court used.
  • Hamer: detriment alone suffices — don't hunt for a benefit to the promisor.
  • Distinguish consideration from a condition to a gift using the "does it benefit the promisor?" test (Pennsy, Plowman).
  • Distinguish motive from consideration (Dougherty, § 81).
  • Watch for recitals of consideration — they don't establish it (§ 218).
  • Illusory promise vs. conditional promise: conditional ≠ illusory (Marshall Durbin); performance can supply consideration even without a promise.
  • Inadequacy: alone insufficient (Harris); extreme imbalance + red flags can invalidate.
  • De minimis non curat lex: contract may form yet be unenforceable (Harris).
  • Unilateral contract revocation: classical vs. § 45 option-on-performance (Hamer Variation (b)).
  • Objective theory: reasonable person's understanding, not subjective intent (Hamer Variation (a)).
  • Watch for agency problems layered on consideration problems (Plowman Note 4).
  • Past services and moral obligation are not consideration (Plowman).
