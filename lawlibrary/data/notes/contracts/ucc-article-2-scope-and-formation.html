In one paragraph

Before anything else, ask which law applies. Contracts for the sale of goods fall under UCC Article 2. Services, real estate, leases and IP fall under the common law. Goods are things movable and identifiable when the contract is made (§ 2-105). For mixed contracts, courts apply the predominant purpose test: goods in Jannusch v. Naffziger, services in Princess Cruises v. General Electric. Article 2 makes formation easier: a contract can arise from conduct even with open terms and no identifiable moment of formation (§ 2-204; Jannusch v. Naffziger). But continued bargaining between sophisticated parties can show they never agreed (EC Styberg Engineering v. Eaton). § 2-207 replaces the common-law mirror image rule in the battle of the forms.

Which law applies

  1. Is the subject matter a "good"? § 2-105: things movable and identifiable at the time of contracting (a car, a computer). Services, real estate, leases (Article 2A) and IP alone are not Article 2 goods.
  2. Is there a "sale"? § 2-106: passing title for a price.
  3. Mixed goods and services → predominant purpose test. Look at the main thrust of the deal. [Outside knowledge] factors courts often use: the contract's language, the nature of the supplier's business, how the price is allocated between goods and services, and what the buyer actually wanted.
  • Jannusch v. Naffziger: sale of a food-truck business (truck, trailer and equipment plus goodwill) for $150,000. Predominantly goods → Article 2.
  • Princess Cruises v. General Electric: routine maintenance on a cruise ship, with replacement parts. Predominantly services → common law. That choice decided whose terms controlled (see below).
  • Why it matters: formation rules (§ 2-204, § 2-207), the statute of frauds (Statute of Frauds › UCC 2-201), gap-fillers, firm offers (Option Contracts and Firm Offers), and adequate assurance (Anticipatory Repudiation and Adequate Assurance › Adequate assurance (UCC 2-609)).

Formation under § 2-204

  • § 2-204(1): a contract may be made in any manner sufficient to show agreement, including conduct.
  • § 2-204(2): a contract can exist even if the moment of formation can't be pinned down.
  • § 2-204(3): open terms don't defeat a contract if the parties intended to contract and there is a reasonably certain basis for a remedy.
  • Jannusch v. Naffziger: the buyers took the truck, ran the business, replaced equipment, paid employees, paid part of the price, arranged financing and asked a lawyer to draft papers. That conduct showed a contract existed. Returning the truck later was a breach, not a rejection.
  • EC Styberg Engineering v. Eaton: sophisticated parties, extensive writings and millions at stake. Their past practice of signing final agreements was strong evidence that they had not yet agreed this time. No contract.
  • Compare Agreements to Agree and Letters of Intent (common-law intent to be bound).

Battle of the forms (UCC 2-207)

Common law: mirror image rule. An acceptance must match the offer exactly. Any added or different term makes it a counteroffer, and the "last shot" (the last form before performance) usually wins once the other side performs. See Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance.

§ 2-207(1): formation. A definite and seasonable expression of acceptance works as an acceptance even if it states additional or different terms. The exception is an acceptance expressly conditioned on assent to those terms.

§ 2-207(2): what happens to additional terms.

  • If either party is not a merchant, the additional terms are mere proposals. They drop out unless expressly accepted.
  • If both are merchants, the additional terms become part of the contract unless:
    • (a) the offer expressly limits acceptance to its own terms;
    • (b) they materially alter the contract (Official Comment 4: they would cause surprise or hardship if incorporated without express awareness, e.g., negating warranties or limiting remedies); or
    • (c) the offeror has already objected, or objects within a reasonable time.
  • "Different" (conflicting) terms: the existing note says the slides treat "additional" and "different" terms together. [Outside knowledge] courts split three ways: treat them like additional terms, let the offeror's term control, or use the majority "knockout" rule (both conflicting terms drop out and Code gap-fillers apply).

§ 2-207(3): contract by conduct. If the writings don't form a contract but the parties' conduct recognizes one, the terms are those on which the writings agree, plus UCC gap-fillers (course of performance, course of dealing, usage of trade, default rules).

  • Princess Cruises v. General Electric: Princess sent a purchase order, and GE accepted with its Final Price Quotation, which excluded consequential damages and capped liability. The jury awarded about $4.5 million. Because the deal was predominantly services, the court applied the common law: GE's response was a counteroffer, Princess accepted it by performing, and judgment was cut to $231,925. Under § 2-207, the result could have gone the other way, since a damages cap might be a material alteration.

Exam framework

  1. Goods or services? Apply the predominant purpose test to mixed deals and explain why the answer matters.
  2. Formation: offer and acceptance, or conduct (§ 2-204)? Were there open terms? Did the parties intend to be bound (Jannusch vs. Styberg)?
  3. Forms don't match?
    • Common law: mirror image → counteroffer → last shot.
    • UCC § 2-207(1): an expression of acceptance? Expressly conditional?
    • § 2-207(2): are both parties merchants? Then run the three exceptions (limitation, material alteration, objection).
    • Different terms: name the knockout rule as the majority view [Outside knowledge].
    • § 2-207(3): conduct → agreed terms plus gap-fillers.
  4. Statute of frauds: goods of $500 or more need a writing (Statute of Frauds › UCC 2-201).

Hypos (slides)

  • Hypo 1, Sandra/Marcus catering business ($180,000): kitchen equipment about $90,000, client list and vendor relationships about $60,000, a three-year non-compete $30,000. A handshake deal, $50,000 paid, and Marcus is running the business. Is it predominantly goods? Compare Jannusch.
  • Hypo 2, BuyerCo/SellerCo industrial parts: the PO has an Indiana federal court venue clause, and the acknowledgment adds New York arbitration. Both are merchants, and the goods shipped and were paid for. § 2-207(1) forms a contract. Then argue whether the arbitration clause is a material alteration or a different term (knockout).
  • Hypo 3, hotel chain/elevator maintenance: the PO says consequential damages are available; the acceptance excludes them. There is $3 million in third-party liability. Is it services (common law, like Princess)? If so, the last shot may control.
