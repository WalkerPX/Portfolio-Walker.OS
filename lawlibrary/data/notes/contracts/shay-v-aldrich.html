Quick reference

Citation: 790 N.W.2d 629 (Mich. 2010) (Knapp's cite); 487 Mich. 648 is outside knowledge · Casebook: Knapp pp. 453-454 (Taylor Note 6, new to the 10th edition) · Topics: Parol Evidence Rule, Interpretation One line: A 4-3 Michigan Supreme Court admitted extrinsic evidence to show that a release of "all other ... persons" was not meant to release officers who weren't party to the settlement. The dissent insisted on plain meaning.

Coverage

Summarized in Knapp's Taylor Note 6, with quotations from both opinions. Not reprinted in full.

Overview

A vivid illustration of the textualist vs. contextualist split. The majority lets the parties' actual intent trump the literal words. The dissent says plain meaning and the duty to read must control.

Parties

  • Plaintiff: Shay. He alleged he was assaulted by a group of police officers.
  • Defendants: three Melvindale police officers (accused of actual physical assault). They relied on a release in the plaintiff's settlement with two other officers.
  • Settling parties: two officers from another municipality (accused only of gross negligence in failing to intervene) and their insurer.

Facts

  • Shay settled his claims against the two non-assaulting officers for $25,000 total.
  • The settlement agreement released the two named defendants "together with all other firms, persons or corporations."
  • His claims against the three Melvindale officers had been independently valued at about $1.5 million.
  • The Melvindale officers argued the "all other persons" language released them too, though there was much evidence this was not the settling parties' intent and none that it was.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The lower courts applied prior Michigan precedent and enforced the release's literal terms. The Michigan Supreme Court reversed, overruling its earlier decision (whose author, Justice Markman, wrote the dissent).

Issue

May a court consider extrinsic evidence to determine whether a release's general "all other persons" language was intended to benefit third parties who were not part of the settlement?

Holding

Yes (4-3). Parol evidence was admissible to show that the settling parties intended to release only the two named officers.

Reasoning

  • Majority (Justice Weaver), quoted in Knapp: without extrinsic evidence, "the settling parties' intent would undoubtedly be perverted." The Melvindale officers would get "a complete windfall ... in exchange for no bargained-for promise and no consideration at all," contrary to "the cardinal rule of contract interpretation: that the contracting parties' intent should control."
  • Dissent (Justice Markman, for three): a party has a duty to read and understand what he signs. Enforcing unambiguous terms as written is the "bedrock of contract law," promoting predictability and respecting freedom of contract.
  • [Outside knowledge]: the majority analyzed it partly as a third-party beneficiary question. The Melvindale officers could enforce the release only if the parties intended to benefit them.

Rule

(Contextualist) Extrinsic evidence may be used to show that general release language was not intended to benefit unnamed third parties, so that the parties' actual intent controls over literal wording. (Textualist dissent) Unambiguous language must be enforced as written.

Why It Matters

  • Key takeaway (slides): the debate between textualism and contextualism is not settled. It remains very much alive across American jurisdictions, so know which approach your jurisdiction follows.
  • It pairs with Taylor v. State Farm. Both involve releases that literally cover more than the parties likely intended.

Other Key Points

  • Slides: the dissent ran 28 pages, arguing "plain meaning" should trump intent.
  • Exam use: for a release or broad boilerplate, argue both textualist (duty to read, certainty) and contextualist (intent, windfall, consideration) positions.
