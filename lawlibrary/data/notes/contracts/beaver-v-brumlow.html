The buyers took possession and spent about $85,000 on improvements in reliance.

Overview

The course case on the part performance exception to the land-contract provision of the statute of frauds. The court rejects a narrow reading of the "unequivocally referable" test.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs (buyers) / appellees: Michael and Karen Brumlow. Michael worked for the sellers for about ten years. They wanted specific performance of the oral land sale.
  • Defendants (sellers) / appellants: Warren and Betty Beaver. Owners of a race-horse transportation business and 24 acres in Ruidoso Downs. They reneged after Michael left to work for a competitor.

Facts

  • The Beavers bought 24 acres in October 2000. In mid-2001, Michael asked Warren to sell him some land for a home. Warren agreed orally, and they walked the boundaries of the parcel.
  • With the sellers' consent, the buyers took possession. Karen cashed in her IRA and 401(k) at a substantial penalty. The buyers moved a double-wide home onto the land, poured footers and a foundation, built a deck and stairs, ran electricity and water, installed septic and propane systems, added a shed and landscaped. They spent about $85,000.
  • Warren signed the village's applications for placing the home and for the septic system.
  • In 2004, Michael left the Beavers' employment for a competitor. The sellers then refused to convey.

Procedural History

  • The trial court ordered specific performance of the oral agreement. The sellers did not challenge the findings of fact, only the statute of frauds.
  • Court of Appeals: affirmed.

Issue

Does the buyers' part performance take the oral land-sale agreement out of the statute of frauds, so it can be specifically enforced?

Holding

Yes. The buyers' possession and substantial improvements in reliance on the agreement satisfy the part performance exception, and specific performance was proper.

Reasoning

  • Part performance doctrine rests on equity: (1) the acts provide evidence of the contract, and (2) refusing enforcement after reliance would be a fraud on the buyer.
  • The sellers relied on the "unequivocally referable" test (from Cardozo's opinion in Burns v. McCormick, Knapp p. 365): the acts must point unmistakably to a contract for the land.
  • The court rejected a narrow reading. The test is satisfied if the acts would lead a reasonable outsider to "naturally and reasonably" conclude that a contract existed. Possession plus valuable, permanent improvements made with the owner's knowledge and help do that.
  • Remedy: land is unique, so damages are presumed inadequate and specific performance is available without special proof.

Rule

An oral contract for the sale of land may be enforced despite the statute of frauds where the buyer, in reliance on it and with the seller's consent, has taken possession and made valuable improvements (or paid part of the price), such that the acts are referable to the contract and denying enforcement would be unjust.

Why It Matters

  • It is the typical part performance pattern (slides): possession plus valuable improvements.
  • Restatement (Second) § 129 contrast (slides): the Restatement drops the "unequivocally referable" test and asks whether the party reasonably relied and whether justice requires enforcement.
  • It shows specific performance as the usual remedy for land (see Specific Performance).

Other Key Points

  • Compare Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice: promissory estoppel, rather than part performance, as a way around the statute.
  • Exam pattern: for an oral land deal, check (1) whether it is within the statute (a land interest), (2) for a writing, and if none, (3) part performance (possession, improvements, payment) and (4) reliance or estoppel (§ 139).
