Citation: 140 Cal. App. 4th 1238, 45 Cal. Rptr. 3d 293 (2006) · Casebook: Knapp p. 676 · Topic: Unconscionability › Arbitration clauses and the FAA One line: An arbitration clause buried in an Extreme Makeover: Home Edition participant agreement, signed by orphaned siblings (some minors) without explanation, was unconscionable and unenforceable.

Overview

The course case applying unconscionability to a mandatory arbitration clause in an adhesion contract. The FAA favors arbitration, but generally applicable contract defenses such as unconscionability still apply.

Parties

  • Petitioners (plaintiffs below): the five Higgins siblings, orphans whose parents both died within months of each other. Some were minors when they signed. They wanted to sue in court.
  • Real parties in interest (defendants below): Disney/ABC and the producers of Extreme Makeover: Home Edition, plus others. They moved to compel arbitration. (The Leomiti family, a church family that took the children in, was also sued.)
  • Respondent: Superior Court of Los Angeles County, which had ordered arbitration.

Facts

  • The Leomitis took in the five Higgins orphans. The show rebuilt the Leomiti house as a showpiece in an episode featuring the children.
  • The children signed a lengthy participation agreement, presented with no expectation that they would read it, on a take-it-or-leave-it basis. It contained an arbitration clause. [Outside knowledge: the arbitration provision was on page 9 or so of a long form, and no one explained it.]
  • The children later alleged the Leomitis verbally abused and exploited them and forced them out of the house. They sued the Leomitis and the producers (for fraud, emotional distress and so on).

Procedural History

  • The trial court granted the producers' motion to compel arbitration.
  • The Court of Appeal granted the children's writ petition and directed the trial court to deny the motion. [Outside knowledge: the exact writ disposition.]

Issue

Was the arbitration clause in the participation agreement unconscionable and therefore unenforceable?

Holding

Yes. The clause was both procedurally and substantively unconscionable, so arbitration cannot be compelled.

Reasoning

  • FAA background (Knapp p. 673, slides): the FAA generally preempts state laws that single out arbitration clauses for invalidation. But general contract defenses, including unconscionability, still apply if they apply equally to all contracts. That opening permits Higgins's analysis.
  • Procedural: an adhesion contract, presented take-it-or-leave-it, with no expectation the children (young, recently orphaned, unrepresented) would read it. The clause was not highlighted or explained.
  • Substantive: troubling features include the cost of arbitration, lack of bilaterality (the clause bound the participants but effectively preserved the producers' court remedies, such as injunctions), and procedural constraints in the arbitration itself.
  • On California's sliding scale, the strong procedural showing offset a lesser substantive one.

Rule

An arbitration clause in a contract of adhesion is unenforceable if it is procedurally unconscionable (oppression or surprise) and substantively unconscionable (one-sided, lacking bilaterality, unduly harsh), assessed on a sliding scale. The FAA does not bar this generally applicable defense.

Why It Matters

  • It is a modern consumer and entertainment application of Williams.
  • Restatement § 211(3) (slides): where the drafter has reason to believe the other party would not assent if it knew a writing contained a particular term, that term is not part of the agreement. Higgins Note 2 addresses adhesion.
  • Slides' evaluation: "A good case can be made that the agreement is unconscionable." But one can also argue the clause is not so unbalanced that it "shocks the conscience."

Other Key Points

  • Hypo 2, "The Reality Show Waiver" (slides): a 19-year-old contestant, a 60-day claim limit, a $1,000 damages cap and one-sided arbitration. Apply the sliding scale and bilaterality, and consider federal policy.
  • Related: Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing (arbitration and duress); Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute (forum clauses).
