Big picture

  • UCC § 1-304: every contract or duty within the Act imposes an obligation of good faith in performance and enforcement.
  • Restatement § 205: duty of good faith and fair dealing extends to every contract.
  • Core idea ("fruit of the contract"): neither party may destroy or injure the other's right to receive the fruits of the contract.
  • Good faith constrains how discretionary rights are exercised — it does not create new obligations the contract doesn't impose (Locke limiting principle).
  • Two types of good faith (Locke Note 1): subjective (honesty in fact) and objective (reasonable commercial standards).

Doctrine outline

  • UCC definition: "honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing."
  • Seidenberg three categories where the implied covenant is successfully invoked:
    1. Implication of a term is necessary to protect the parties' reasonable expectations.
    2. Termination or other action by one party appears bad faith or pretextual.
    3. Exercise of discretion granted by a contract should be limited by fair dealing.
  • Implied reasonable efforts (Wood): exclusive dealing implies a duty of reasonable efforts. Codified for goods at UCC § 2-306(2).
  • Implied reasonable notice (Leibel): UCC § 2-309(3) implies reasonable notice before terminating an indefinite-duration contract — a fairness obligation (time to recoup investment, find substitutes).
  • Discretionary rights (Locke): an express right to accept or reject is still subject to the implied duty of good faith. A party may not exercise discretionary rights to deny the other the benefit of the bargain.

Tests and elements

  • Seidenberg checklist (invoke the covenant when):
    • Term implication needed to protect reasonable expectations.
    • Termination/action appears bad faith or pretextual.
    • Contractual discretion should be limited by fair dealing.
  • Wood reasonable-efforts test: exclusive dealing → implied duty of reasonable efforts (common law for services; UCC § 2-306(2) for goods).
  • Leibel notice test: indefinite duration → reasonable notice before termination (UCC § 2-309(3)).
    • Express "no notice" clause enforceable unless unconscionable.
    • Immediate termination on an agreed event (e.g., two consecutive years of declining sales) is effective.
  • Locke good-faith test: express accept/reject right still subject to good faith; no exercise of discretion to deny benefit of the bargain.
    • Subjective honesty: genuine consideration, not rubber-stamped rejection (Locke Note 1).
    • Objective standard may apply where trade practice gives absolute creative discretion (Locke Note 2).

Cases

  • Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon (p. 486): Cardozo — "A promise may be lacking, and yet the whole writing may be 'instinct with an obligation,' imperfectly expressed." Held: exclusive dealing implies a duty of reasonable efforts. UCC § 2-306(2) codifies.
  • Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing Co. (p. 490): UCC § 2-309(3) implies reasonable notice before terminating an indefinite-duration contract. Contrast with Wood: Wood implies efforts to carry out intent; Leibel implies notice as a matter of fairness.
  • Seidenberg v. Summit Bank (p. 498): sophisticated parties; good faith claim survives. Three categories (above). "Bad faith, like beauty, is in the eye of the beholder — or more to the point, of the trier of fact."
  • Locke v. Warner Bros., Inc. (p. 518): express right to accept or reject still subject to implied duty of good faith; may not exercise discretion to deny benefit of the bargain. Remanded on breach of contract and fraud theories.

Exam angles

  • Apply the Seidenberg three categories as a checklist to any good-faith claim.
  • Distinguish Wood (implied efforts) from Leibel (implied notice) — different fairness rationales.
  • Locke: express discretion ≠ unlimited discretion; but good faith doesn't create new obligations.
  • Identify subjective vs. objective good faith; match standard to facts (Locke Note 1 vs. Note 2).
  • Watch for express clauses ("no notice," "sole discretion") — enforceable unless unconscionable or exercised in bad faith.
  • Consider fraud claims surviving independently of good faith (Locke Note 2).
