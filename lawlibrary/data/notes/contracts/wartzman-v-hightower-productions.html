Quick reference

Citation: 53 Md. App. 656, 456 A.2d 82 (1983) · Casebook: Knapp p. 1021 · Topic: Reliance and Restitution Damages › Reliance damages One line: Promoters of a world-record flagpole-sitting stunt, whose venture collapsed because their lawyers failed to advise them about securities laws, recovered reliance damages (their lost investment), since lost profits were too speculative to prove.

Overview

The course case showing reliance damages as an alternative when expectation damages cannot be proved with reasonable certainty. The plaintiff is put back where it would have been had the contract never been made.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs / appellees / cross-appellants: Hightower Productions, Ltd., a promotional venture formed in 1974 by Ira Adler, Frank Billitz and J. Daniel Quinn. They wanted damages for their failed venture.
  • Defendants / appellants: Wartzman and his law firm, which handled the incorporation and fundraising and failed to give timely securities-law advice.

Facts

  • Hightower planned to have a singer-entertainer ("Woody Hightower") live on a specially built mobile flagpole perch from April 1, 1975 to New Year's Eve, breaking the Guinness World Record for flagpole sitting, and to profit from promotions and appearances.
  • The law firm incorporated the venture and helped raise money from investors, but failed to advise early on that the stock offering was covered by Maryland's "Blue Sky" laws and needed registration.
  • When this was finally disclosed, the principals lacked the cash to hire a securities specialist or to proceed. Much of the money raised had to go into escrow. The venture was abandoned.

Procedural History

  • Jury trial (Superior Court of Baltimore City, Judge Karwacki): the jury was allowed to consider reliance damages and awarded the principals substantially all their lost investment. [Outside knowledge: about $170,000.] The court refused prejudgment interest.
  • Court of Special Appeals: affirmed. Costs were assessed to the appellants.

Issue

When lost profits are too speculative to prove, may a plaintiff recover reliance damages (its expenditures) for breach of contract, and what limits apply?

Holding

Yes. Reliance damages were properly submitted. The jury was adequately instructed on causation, foreseeability was met, the plaintiffs were not barred by failure to mitigate, and prejudgment interest was properly denied.

Reasoning

  • Reliance damages put the plaintiff where it would have been had the promise not been made, by reimbursing expenditures made in reliance on the contract.
  • Note 1: full expectation damages, the profits lost because of the bad advice, would have required proving the flagpole venture would have succeeded. That was "almost surely an impossible burden." The promotion may have seemed a good bet, but a jury would hardly regard it as a sure thing.
  • [Outside knowledge]: the breaching party may reduce reliance damages by proving the plaintiff would have lost money even with full performance, but the burden is on the breaching party.

Rule

A non-breaching party may recover reliance damages (expenditures in preparation for or performance of the contract) as an alternative to expectation damages, especially when profits are uncertain, less any loss the breaching party proves the plaintiff would have suffered on full performance (Restatement (Second) § 349). The usual limits (foreseeability, certainty, mitigation, causation) also apply (Note 3).

Why It Matters

  • Reliance purpose (slides): put the promisee where it would have been had the promise not been made. Example: Tony promises $10,000, and Cornelius spends $9,000 relying on it. Reliance damages are $9,000.
  • It shows reliance damages are a contract remedy, not just a promissory estoppel remedy.
