Quick reference

Citation: 156 P. 458 (Cal. 1916) (Knapp's cite; 172 Cal. 289 is [Outside knowledge]) · Casebook: Knapp pp. 770-771 · Topic: Impracticability and Frustration › Impracticability One line: A contractor who agreed to take all its gravel from the plaintiff's land was excused from taking the gravel below water level, because extracting it would cost about ten times the normal cost. This is the origin of commercial impracticability.

Coverage

Summarized in Knapp's introductory text. Details marked [Outside knowledge] are not in your materials.

Overview

The case generally credited with creating impracticability. Performance need not be literally impossible if it has become so much more costly than contemplated that it is, in practical effect, impossible.

Parties

  • Plaintiff: Mineral Park Land Co., the landowner. It wanted damages for gravel the contractor obtained elsewhere.
  • Defendant: Howard (a contractor), who agreed to take from the plaintiff's land all the gravel needed to build a concrete bridge. He took some gravel from another source.

Facts

  • From Knapp: the defendant agreed to buy and extract from the plaintiff's land, at fixed prices, all the gravel required for a concrete bridge. He got some gravel elsewhere and was sued for failing to take all his requirements from the plaintiff.
  • [Outside knowledge, consistent with the slides]: the remaining gravel lay below the water level. Extracting it would have required a steam dredge at about 10 to 12 times the usual cost.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The trial court found largely for the defendant. The California Supreme Court affirmed (in relevant part).

Issue

Is a party excused from performing when performance, though physically possible, would cost vastly more than both parties contemplated?

Holding

Yes. The extreme increase in the cost of extraction justified non-performance. Performance was "impracticable."

Reasoning

  • From Knapp: "Even though performance clearly was not literally impossible (indeed, it was not even 'subjectively' impossible), it was sufficiently different from what the parties had both contemplated at the time of contracting as to be 'impracticable.'"
  • [Outside knowledge]: a thing is impossible in legal contemplation when it is not practicable, and it is impracticable when it can be done only at an excessive and unreasonable cost.

Rule

A party is excused where an event, the non-occurrence of which was a basic assumption of the contract, makes performance impracticable (extreme and unreasonable difficulty or expense), without the party's fault and absent assumption of the risk (Restatement (Second) § 261; UCC § 2-615).

Why It Matters

  • It completes the trilogy: impossibility (Taylor v. Caldwell), frustration (Krell v. Henry), impracticability (Mineral Park).
  • Slides: increased cost or reduced profitability, even if extreme, is generally insufficient. Modern courts rarely excuse for cost increases (see Hemlock Semiconductor v. Solarworld). Mineral Park's facts (a physical condition unknown to both parties that multiplied the cost) are unusual.
