In one paragraph

A misrepresentation is an assertion of fact that is not true. Fraud in the execution (someone is tricked about the nature of the document they sign) makes the contract void (Park 100 Investors v. Kartes). Fraud in the inducement makes it voidable: a knowing or reckless misrepresentation, meant to induce assent, that does induce it, with justified reliance. A nonfraudulent misrepresentation makes a contract voidable if it is material and justifiably relied on. Under the classical view there was no duty to disclose (Laidlaw v. Organ). The modern view (Restatement § 161; Hill v. Jones) requires disclosure of material, latent facts in several situations. Misrepresentation sits where contract and tort meet (Syester v. Banta).

Fraudulent misrepresentation

Fraud in the inducement: three elements (existing note):

  1. A fraudulent misrepresentation of fact:
    • (a) made knowingly or recklessly (conscious ignorance of the truth), and
    • (b) with the intent to induce assent.
  2. It induced the other party to assent.
  3. The other party was justified in relying on it. This is usually where the fight is.
  • Effect: voidable. The victim may rescind and get restitution, or sue in tort for damages (including punitive damages).

Nonfraudulent misrepresentation: voidable if (1) it is a material misrepresentation of fact and (2) there is justifiable reliance. Material means a statement or omission that would make a reasonable person act differently if they knew the whole truth.

Opinions: the old rule that opinions aren't actionable has eroded. An opinion is actionable when the speaker knows it is false, when there is a close relationship, or when it implies underlying facts.

  • Syester v. Banta: Agnes Syester, a lonely, elderly widow, bought nearly $30,000 of lessons from an Arthur Murray dance studio. Staff flattered her, saying she could become a professional dancer and that she did not need a lawyer. She sued, then settled and signed a release. She later sued again to rescind the release and recover in tort. The jury could find "fraudulent overreaching," and the judgment was affirmed. Reliance problem: she had already sued alleging she had been lied to. Undue influence may explain the result better.

  • Park 100 Investors v. Kartes: the landlord's agent called a personal guaranty "lease papers," and the document was headed "Lease Agreement." Mr. Kartes even called his lawyer to confirm the lease papers had been approved. The guaranty was not enforceable. Normally you can't avoid a signed document by saying you didn't read it (Ray v. Eurice Bros: duty to read). But active misrepresentation of the document's character overrides that. Whether reliance was reasonable is a question of fact, and here the Karteses used ordinary care.
  • Contrast Ray v. Eurice Bros: no misrepresentation by the other side, so the signer was bound.
  • The parol evidence rule doesn't bar fraud evidence, but see the narrow view in Sherrodd v. Morrison-Knudsen (Parol Evidence Rule › Exceptions).

Nondisclosure

  • Classical view: caveat emptor. No duty to disclose in an arm's-length deal. Laidlaw v. Organ: a buyer of tobacco who knew the War of 1812 had ended (news that would raise prices) had no duty to tell the seller. But he could not make active misstatements.
  • Modern view (Restatement § 161; Hill v. Jones): nondisclosure is treated as an assertion that the fact does not exist, and is actionable when it amounts to a failure to act in good faith.

A duty to disclose a defect arises when the defect is (1) unknown to the other party and (2) of a kind the other party is unlikely to discover or ask about.

Rescission for nondisclosure (checklist). The undisclosed facts must:

  1. have a material effect on the transaction;
  2. not be readily observable;
  3. not be otherwise known to the uninformed party; and
  4. fall into one of four circumstances: (1) disclosure is required by statute; (2) there was intentional concealment; (3) there is a relationship of trust and confidence; or (4) disclosure is needed to prevent or correct a mistake (§ 161(b)).

Keeton factors (whether a duty is fair to impose): whether the withholding party knew the fact; whether it knew the fact's significance; reliance; and the parties' relationship.

  • Hill v. Jones: buyers of a house learned after contracting that it had a prior termite infestation. The sellers knew and had not disclosed it. Held: the sellers had a duty to disclose a material latent fact. An integration clause did not bar the claim.

Remedies

  • Rescission and restitution (contract), or affirm and sue for damages (tort deceit).
  • [Outside knowledge] In tort, damages may be measured by the "benefit of the bargain" or "out of pocket." Punitive damages are available for fraud, not for breach of contract.

Exam framework

  1. What was said or not said? Fact or opinion? An affirmative statement, concealment, or silence?
  2. Classify: fraud in the execution (void) vs. inducement (voidable) vs. nonfraudulent (voidable if material).
  3. Fraud in the inducement: run the three elements, focusing on justified reliance and the duty to read.
  4. Nondisclosure: classical vs. modern. Run the duty checklist, the four circumstances and the Keeton factors.
  5. Overlap: tort damages vs. contract rescission; a release must be rescinded first (Syester); the PER fraud exception.
  6. Alternatives: undue influence, mistake (Mistake), unconscionability.

Hypos (slides)

  • Hypo 4, "Just Sign the Lease": the agent says "we just need your signature on the lease," but the document is a personal guaranty headed "Commercial Lease Agreement." The CEO skims it and asks "Is this the lease?" The agent says "Yes." Under Park 100, fraud in the execution, so the guaranty is void. The heading and the agent's direct answer make reliance reasonable. The CEO's sophistication cuts the other way. The fix for the landlord: label and explain the guaranty separately.
  • Syester as an illustration: "you could be a professional dancer," "you don't need a lawyer." Was her reliance reasonable, or was she paying for companionship (Note 4)?
