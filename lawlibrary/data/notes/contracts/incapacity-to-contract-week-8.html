Big picture

  • Defenses to enforcement rest on three grounds: (1) status/incapacity, (2) defects in bargaining (duress, undue influence, misrepresentation), (3) substance of the bargain (unconscionability, public policy).
  • Chapter assumes ordinary formation requirements are met; one party wants to avoid obligations.
  • Defenses work as a shield (defend against enforceability) or a sword (rescind + restitution for benefits conferred).
  • Two classes have limited power to contract: minors (age) and mentally infirm persons.

Doctrine outline

Minors

  • Until age of majority, contracts are voidable at the minor's option.
  • Minor may "avoid"/"disaffirm" before or within a reasonable time after reaching majority (generally 18).
  • Doing nothing after 18, or retaining benefits (reasonable grace period), = ratification.
  • Necessities exception: benefit conferred (Dodson Note 3).
  • Prevailing rule (Note 4): minor liable on contract unless disaffirmed within reasonable period after majority — contract is voidable, not void.

Mental incapacity

  • Adjudicated mentally incompetent → contract is void.
  • Without adjudication → contract voidable if unable to (1) reasonably understand nature and consequences (cognitive), OR (2) act reasonably in relation to the transaction AND other side has reason to know of condition (volitional).

Tests and elements

Minority disaffirmance checklist

  • Contract entered before age of majority → voidable at minor's option.
  • Disaffirm before majority or within reasonable time after.
  • If no action after 18 / benefits retained → ratified.
  • Necessities: contract unenforceable but minor may be liable in restitution for reasonable value.
  • Misrepresentation of age or willful destruction of goods: minor may be liable (courts split on whether misrepresentation loses right to disaffirm).

Dodson restitution rule (elements)

  • Seller gets reasonable compensation for loss of value to goods while in minor's hands.
  • Rule applies only when no evidence of overreaching, fraud, or unfair advantage by seller.

Mental incapacity tests

  • Cognitive: lack of understanding of the transaction — some evidence required.
  • Volitional: inability to act in a reasonable manner, apart from understanding — must also show other party had reason to know of the condition.
  • Adjudication → void (not merely voidable).

Minor vs. mental incapacity (Note 2)

  • Minor capacity: based on age, without regard to specific capacity. Mental capacity: depends on particular circumstances of the individual.
  • Mental incapacity: detrimental reliance by other party may protect that party if contract on fair terms and made without knowledge of incapacity (Restatement (Second) § 15(2)). For minors, reliance traditionally disregarded.

Cases

  • Dodson v. Shrader (p. 576) — Minor bought truck for cash, seeks to avoid and recover purchase price in restitution. Issue: must he make restitution for depreciation? Seller's remedy: reasonable compensation for loss of value while in minor's hands; applies only absent overreaching, fraud, or unfair advantage by seller. Tension: minority incapacity assumes poor judgment, but depreciation compensation deprives minors of much of the protection disaffirmance provides.
  • Sparrow v. Demonico (p. 583) — Susan Demonico refused to honor mediation settlement, claiming mental incapacity at the time. Court required medical evidence; lay testimony alone insufficient. Sets out cognitive and volitional tests.
  • Ortelere — named only (leading application of volitional test) — now briefed: Ortelere v. Teachers Retirement Board.

Examples and hypos

Hypo 1 — "The Used Truck"

  • Facts: 17-year-old buys used pickup for $5,000 cash; dealer believed buyer was adult. Six months later truck breaks down from engine defect possibly pre-existing. Minor disaffirms, demands full refund. Truck now worth $2,000.
  • Q1: Apply traditional minority rule — what can minor recover?
  • Q2: How does Dodson modify the traditional rule?
  • Q3: Does possible pre-existing defect change the analysis?

Exam angles

  • Is the contract void or voidable? (Adjudicated incompetence → void; minor/unadjudicated mental incapacity → voidable.)
  • Was there disaffirmance within a reasonable time after majority, or ratification by inaction/retaining benefits?
  • Does Dodson restitution apply — any overreaching, fraud, or unfair advantage by seller?
  • Necessities: contract unenforceable but restitution may lie for reasonable value.
  • Mental incapacity: which test — cognitive or volitional? Volitional requires other party's reason to know.
  • Evidence: Sparrow requires medical evidence; lay testimony insufficient.
  • Shield vs. sword: defending enforcement vs. rescission + restitution.
  • Compare reliance: protects other party for mental incapacity (§ 15(2)) but traditionally disregarded for minors.
