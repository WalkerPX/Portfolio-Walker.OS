Quick reference

Citation: 305 N.Y. 48, 110 N.E.2d 551 (1953) · Casebook: Knapp p. 337 · Topic: Statute of Frauds › The writing requirement One line: A two-year employment contract satisfied the statute of frauds by piecing together signed payroll cards and an unsigned memo that referred to the same transaction. Parol evidence could show the connection.

Overview

The leading case on combining multiple writings to satisfy the statute of frauds. A signed writing and an unsigned writing may be read together if they clearly refer to the same transaction on their face, even without an express cross-reference. Parol evidence is admissible to show the connection and the party's assent to the unsigned writing.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / respondent: Nate Crabtree. He left a secure, well-paying job to become sales manager and insisted on a contract for a definite term. He wanted the promised second-year salary.
  • Defendant / appellant: Elizabeth Arden Sales Corp. A cosmetics manufacturer (president: Miss Elizabeth Arden; executive VP: Robert P. Johns). It argued that a two-year contract not to be performed within a year needed a signed writing.

Facts

  • September 1947: Crabtree asked for a three-year contract at $25,000/year, because he was giving up a secure job for a new field. Miss Arden offered two years: $20,000 for the first six months, $25,000 for the second six months, $30,000 for the second year, plus $5,000/year expenses. Crabtree said it was "interesting."
  • Miss Arden's secretary wrote a memo on a telephone order blank: the date, the salary steps, expenses, and the phrase "2 years to make good." It was unsigned. [Outside knowledge: exact memo wording.]
  • Crabtree accepted by phone and telegram. On starting, a payroll change card signed by Johns showed the first-year salary terms. Six months later, a second payroll card, signed by the comptroller, showed the raise to $25,000.
  • When the second-year raise to $30,000 was refused, Crabtree quit and sued.

Procedural History

  • Trial (non-jury): judgment for Crabtree for the unpaid salary. [Outside knowledge: about $14,000.]
  • The Appellate Division affirmed, two justices dissenting.
  • Court of Appeals: affirmed.

Issue

Can a signed writing and an unsigned writing be read together to satisfy the statute of frauds, and may parol evidence be used to connect them and to explain the term "2 years to make good"?

Holding

Yes. The signed payroll cards and the unsigned memo refer on their face to the same transaction and together contain all the essential terms, including duration. They satisfy the statute. Parol evidence was properly admitted to explain "2 years to make good" as a two-year term.

Reasoning

  • One-year provision: a two-year contract cannot be performed within a year, so it requires a signed memorandum.
  • Multiple writings: a memorandum may consist of several writings, some signed and some unsigned, if they clearly refer to the same subject matter or transaction. An express reference in the signed writing to the unsigned one is not required (this rejects a stricter older rule).
  • Parol evidence is admissible to show the connection between the papers and the assent of the party to be charged to the unsigned writing. It cannot supply missing essential terms.
  • "2 years to make good" is "a cryptic and ambiguous statement," so parol evidence may explain it. Given Crabtree's insistence on security, the trier of fact could find it meant a two-year term.

Rule

The statute of frauds is satisfied by several writings read together if: (1) together they contain all essential terms; (2) at least one is signed by the party to be charged; (3) the unsigned writing on its face refers to the same transaction; (4) evidence shows the party to be charged assented to the unsigned writing. No explicit cross-reference is needed, and parol evidence is admissible to show the connection and assent (slides).

Why It Matters

  • It is the standard rule for piecing together a memorandum (Restatement (Second) § 132).
  • It shows the one-year provision in action: a term longer than one year is within the statute.
  • It shows the interplay with the parol evidence rule: extrinsic evidence can explain ambiguity, but not create missing terms.

Other Key Points

  • Writing elements (slides): the writing must (1) reasonably identify the subject matter, (2) indicate a contract was made, (3) state the essential terms with reasonable certainty, (4) be signed by the party to be charged.
