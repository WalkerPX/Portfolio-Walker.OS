Rep. 145 (Ex. 1854) (9 Ex. 341 is [Outside knowledge]) · Casebook: Knapp p. 923 · Topic: Limits on Damages › Foreseeability (Hadley) One line: A carrier that delayed delivering a broken mill shaft was not liable for the mill's lost profits, because it was not told that the mill would sit idle without the shaft. Consequential damages must be within the parties' contemplation.

Overview

The foundational case on foreseeability of contract damages. It sets out the two-branch rule: damages are recoverable if they (1) arise naturally from the breach in the usual course of things, or (2) were reasonably in the contemplation of both parties at the time of contracting, based on special circumstances communicated.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs: Hadley (and partner), operators of an extensive milling business at Gloucester. They wanted lost profits for the days the mill was stopped.
  • Defendants: Baxendale and others, carriers trading as Pickford & Co. They delayed delivering the shaft and argued the lost profits were too remote.

Facts

  • May 11: the mill stopped when its crankshaft broke. A new one had to be made by the engine manufacturers (Joyce & Co., Greenwich), using the old shaft as a pattern.
  • The plaintiffs' servant took the shaft to the defendants' office. [Disputed in the record: whether the clerk was told the mill was stopped and the shaft had to be sent immediately. The court treated the communicated facts as only that the article was a broken shaft of a mill.]
  • Through the carriers' neglect, delivery was delayed several days, so the mill was idle longer. The plaintiffs claimed lost profits.

Procedural History

  • At trial (Gloucester Assizes, Crompton, J.), the jury was allowed to consider lost profits and returned £25 beyond the amount paid into court. [Outside knowledge: amount.]
  • Court of Exchequer: new trial granted, because the jury should have been instructed not to consider lost profits.

Issue

Is a carrier liable for a mill's lost profits caused by its delay in delivering a broken shaft, when it was not informed that the mill's operation depended on prompt delivery?

Holding

No. Lost profits were not recoverable. In the ordinary course, a miller might have a spare shaft, and the carriers were not told of the special circumstances that made the delay cause the mill to stand idle.

Reasoning

  • The rule (Alderson, B.): damages should be such as may fairly and reasonably be considered either (1) arising naturally, i.e., according to the usual course of things, from the breach, or (2) such as may reasonably be supposed to have been in the contemplation of both parties, at the time they made the contract, as the probable result of the breach.
  • If special circumstances were communicated, damages that would ordinarily follow from breach under those circumstances are recoverable. If unknown to the breaching party, it is liable only for damages arising generally.
  • Here, many circumstances (a spare shaft, other defects) could exist where delay would not stop the mill. The loss of profits did not follow naturally, and no special circumstances were communicated.

Rule

Damages for breach are recoverable only if, at the time of contracting, they were foreseeable as a probable result of breach, either because they follow in the ordinary course (general damages) or because the breaching party had reason to know of special circumstances (consequential damages) (Restatement (Second) § 351).

Why It Matters

  • Restatement § 351 (slides): (1) loss is not recoverable if the breaching party did not have reason to foresee it as a probable result when the contract was made; (2) loss may be foreseeable because it follows (a) in the ordinary course of events, or (b) from special circumstances beyond the ordinary course that the party had reason to know; (3) a court may limit damages for foreseeable loss (e.g., by excluding lost profits) if justice so requires to avoid disproportionate compensation.
  • Hadley governs consequential damages, the most common battleground (see Florafax v. GTE Market Resources, Princess Cruises v. General Electric).

Other Key Points

  • Knapp's Notes discuss Danzig's historical study of Hadley (industrialization of the law). [See Knapp p. 926.]
  • UCC counterpart: § 2-715(2)(a), a buyer's consequential damages "of which the seller at the time of contracting had reason to know." [Outside knowledge: the section cite.]
