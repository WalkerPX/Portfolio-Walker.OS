How to use this outline

This follows the course order (Knapp et al., Problems in Contract Law, 10th ed. 2023). Each rule links to its topic note ([[Topic#Heading]]) and each case to its brief. Most case links go to the brief's Rule or Holding section ([[Case#Rule]]). The exam is December 4: 4 hours, closed book, multiple choice plus essays (final = 90%). Items marked [Outside knowledge] are not from the course materials. For a fuller treatment, see Contracts Study Guide (Long). For the bare rules, see Contracts Essentials (Short). Everything is listed in the Contracts Law Library (Index).

0. Essay attack plan

  1. What law applies? Goods → UCC Article 2; services, land, employment → common law. For mixed deals, use the predominant purpose test (UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation › Which law applies).
  2. Is there a contract? Mutual assent (offer and acceptance), consideration, definiteness. If not: promissory estoppel, restitution.
  3. Is it enforceable? Statute of frauds; defenses (capacity, duress, undue influence, misrepresentation, unconscionability, mistake).
  4. What are its terms? Parol evidence rule, interpretation, implied terms (good faith), UCC gap-fillers, the battle of the forms.
  5. Was performance due, and was there a breach? Conditions, substantial performance, material or total breach, repudiation, excuse (impracticability, frustration).
  6. Remedies: expectation (with limits), reliance, restitution, specific performance, liquidated damages.
  • Argue both sides and in the alternative (contract → promissory estoppel → restitution). Name the rule, apply the facts, and conclude.

I. Foundations

  • Contract = a promise or set of promises the law will enforce (Foundations of Contract Law › What is a contract?).
  • Sources: common law (Restatement (Second)), UCC Article 2, CISG (Foundations of Contract Law › Sources of contract law).
  • Theories: promise and autonomy, reliance, efficiency, fairness (Foundations of Contract Law › Theories of contract law (slides)).
  • Standard forms: Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute (Problem 1-2): a forum-selection clause in a form ticket was held reasonable and enforceable [Outside knowledge: the holding is not in the casebook problem] (Foundations of Contract Law › Problem 1-2: Carnival Cruise (standard forms)).
  • Remedies vocabulary: expectation, reliance, restitution (Foundations of Contract Law › Remedies vocabulary (Week 2 preview)).

II. Formation

A. Mutual assent: the objective theory

  • Assent is judged by outward manifestations, as a reasonable person would understand them, not by secret intent (Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance › Objective theory of contract).
  • Ray v. Eurice Bros: a party who signs is bound by the contract as written, even if it misunderstood it.
  • Lucy v. Zehmer: a secret jest is no defense if the words reasonably appeared serious.
  • Leonard v. Pepsico: an obviously jocular ad (a Harrier jet for Pepsi Points) is not an offer.

B. Offers

  • An offer is a manifestation of willingness to bargain, made so that the offeree is justified in understanding that its assent will conclude the deal (Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance › Offers).
  • Ads are usually invitations to make offers, unless they are clear, definite, explicit and leave nothing open (Izadi v. Machado Ford: a bait-and-switch ad may be treated as an offer).
  • Lonergan v. Scolnick: an ad plus letters were preliminary negotiations ("act fast"), not an offer.

C. Acceptance and termination of the power of acceptance

  • Acceptance must be unequivocal. Under the mirror image rule, a response with different terms is a counteroffer (which rejects the offer) (Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance › Acceptance (bilateral contracts)).
  • Mailbox rule: an acceptance is effective on dispatch. Revocations and rejections are effective on receipt.
  • Termination: rejection or counteroffer, lapse of time, revocation (including indirect revocation when the offeree learns of inconsistent action from a reliable source), death or incapacity (Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance › Termination of the power of acceptance).
  • Normile v. Miller: the seller's counteroffer was revocable without consideration. The buyers learned it had been sold to another and could no longer accept (indirect revocation).

D. Unilateral contracts

  • An offer accepted by performance (Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance › Unilateral contracts).
  • Once performance begins, an option arises and the offer becomes irrevocable (Restatement § 45) (Option Contracts and Firm Offers › Part performance of a unilateral offer (§ 45)).
  • Cook v. Coldwell Banker: a bonus program couldn't be changed after the agent substantially performed.
  • Sateriale v. RJ Reynolds: a rewards program was an offer for a unilateral contract, accepted by buying and saving certificates.

E. Indefiniteness, agreements to agree, letters of intent

  • Terms must be reasonably certain. An agreement to agree on a material term (such as price) is unenforceable (Agreements to Agree and Letters of Intent › Indefiniteness, Agreements to Agree and Letters of Intent › Agreements to agree).
  • Walker v. Keith: a lease renewal "at a rental to be agreed upon" set no price or method, so it was unenforceable.
  • Letters of intent: did the parties intend to be bound before a formal writing? (Agreements to Agree and Letters of Intent › Letters of intent and preliminary agreements). In Quake Construction v. American Airlines, the letter was ambiguous, so extrinsic evidence of intent was needed.
  • UCC § 2-204(3): open terms are OK if the parties intended to contract (UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation › Formation under § 2-204).

F. Consideration

  • Bargained-for exchange (§ 71): sought by the promisor and given in exchange; or benefit/detriment (Consideration › Two formulations).
    • Hamer v. Sidway: giving up a legal right (drinking, smoking) is a detriment.
    • Pennsy Supply v. American Ash Recycling: taking "free" AggRite saved the promisor disposal costs, so it was a bargain.
  • Gift vs. bargain: a conditional gift isn't consideration (Consideration › Gift vs. bargain): Kirksey v. Kirksey, Dougherty v. Salt (a recital or motive is not consideration).
  • Past consideration and moral obligation are not consideration (Consideration › Past consideration and moral obligation): Plowman v. Indian Refining, Mills v. Wyman.
  • Adequacy isn't reviewed (Consideration › Adequacy of consideration): Batsakis v. Demotsis, Harris v. Time.
  • Illusory promises fail. An implied duty (reasonable efforts, good faith) or a conditional promise can save them (Consideration › Illusory promises and mutuality): Wood v. Lucy Lady Duff-Gordon, Marshall Durbin Food v. Baker (continued at-will employment is consideration).

G. Promissory estoppel (Restatement § 90)

  • Elements: (1) a promise; (2) the promisor should reasonably expect it to induce action or forbearance; (3) it does induce such action or forbearance; (4) injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. The remedy may be limited as justice requires (Promissory Estoppel › Elements (slides), Promissory Estoppel › Remedy).
  • Family promises: Kirksey (pre-§ 90) (Promissory Estoppel › Family promises).
  • Charitable subscriptions: § 90(2), no proof of reliance needed. In King v. Trustees of Boston University, Dr. King's letter to BU raised a jury question of consideration or reliance (Promissory Estoppel › Charitable subscriptions).
  • Reliance in negotiations: in Pops Cones v. Resorts International, a "clear and definite" promise is no longer strictly required, and reliance damages are available (Promissory Estoppel › Reliance in negotiations).
  • Remedy: often reliance damages (Walser v. Toyota Motor Sales; Reliance and Restitution Damages › Reliance damages).

H. Option contracts and firm offers

  • An option requires consideration. A nominal recital isn't enough in Berryman v. Kmoch, although § 87(1)(a) enforces a signed writing that recites a purported consideration (Option Contracts and Firm Offers › Option contracts).
  • Firm offers (UCC § 2-205): a merchant's signed written assurance holds an offer open without consideration, up to 3 months (Option Contracts and Firm Offers › Firm offers (UCC § 2-205)).
  • Subcontractor bids (Option Contracts and Firm Offers › Reliance on offers: subcontractor bids): James Baird v. Gimbel Bros (Hand: revocable, no consideration) vs. Drennan v. Star Paving (Traynor: irrevocable once the general contractor relies; § 87(2)).

I. Restitution and promissory restitution

  • Unjust enrichment: (1) a benefit conferred and (2) retaining it would be unjust (Restitution and Promissory Restitution › Unjust enrichment without a promise).
    • Emergency services: Credit Bureau Enterprises v. Pelo (the slides call it "Credit Enterprises v. Pelo").
    • Subcontractor v. owner: Commerce Partnership v. Equity Contracting (exhausted remedies + the owner gave no consideration).
    • Cohabitants: Watts v. Watts; Marvin v. Marvin.
  • Promissory restitution (§ 86): a promise made in recognition of a benefit previously received is binding to the extent necessary to prevent injustice. It doesn't apply to gifts or disproportionate promises (Restitution and Promissory Restitution › Promissory restitution (Restatement 86)). Mills v. Wyman (no) vs. Webb v. McGowin (yes).

J. UCC Article 2 formation and the battle of the forms

  • Scope: goods (§ 2-105); predominant purpose for mixed deals (UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation › Which law applies). Jannusch v. Naffziger (goods; formed by conduct); Princess Cruises v. General Electric (services → common law; GE's terms controlled).
  • § 2-204 formation by conduct vs. EC Styberg Engineering v. Eaton (no intent to be bound).
  • § 2-207: (1) a definite expression of acceptance is an acceptance unless expressly conditional; (2) between merchants, additional terms come in unless the offer limits acceptance, the terms materially alter it, or the offeror objects; different terms → the knockout rule [Outside knowledge]; (3) contract by conduct → the agreed terms plus gap-fillers (UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation › Battle of the forms (UCC 2-207)).

K. Good faith and implied terms

  • Every contract carries a duty of good faith and fair dealing (§ 205; UCC § 1-304) (Good Faith and Fair Dealing › Sources of the duty).
  • Implied reasonable efforts (Wood); implied reasonable notice of termination (Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing) (Good Faith and Fair Dealing › Implied terms).
  • Discretion is limited by good faith: Locke v. Warner Bros; Seidenberg v. Summit Bank (three categories) (Good Faith and Fair Dealing › Bad-faith performance and discretion).

III. Writing requirements and terms

A. Statute of frauds

  • Three steps: within the statute? sufficient writing? exception? (Statute of Frauds › Three-step frame).
  • Categories: suretyship, marriage, land, contracts that can't be performed within one year, goods of $500 or more (Statute of Frauds › Categories within the statute). For the one-year provision, any possibility of full performance within a year takes the contract outside the statute (Statute of Frauds › The one-year provision).
  • The writing: subject matter, that a contract exists, essential terms, signed by the party to be charged. Several documents may be combined (Statute of Frauds › The writing requirement; Crabtree v. Elizabeth Arden).
  • Part performance (land): possession plus improvements. Burns's "unequivocally referable" test vs. Beaver's "naturally and reasonably" test (Statute of Frauds › Part performance (land); Beaver v. Brumlow; Burns v. McCormick).
  • Promissory estoppel (§ 139): Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice. Reliance must be reasonable, including belief in the promisor's authority (Statute of Frauds › Reliance and promissory estoppel).
  • UCC § 2-201: a writing with a quantity term, signed. Exceptions: merchant's confirmation (10 days), specially manufactured goods, admission, payment or acceptance (Statute of Frauds › UCC 2-201; Buffaloe v. Hart).

B. Parol evidence rule

  • Integration (partial vs. full), then contradict vs. supplement. Consistent additional terms are allowed only if the writing is partially integrated (Parol Evidence Rule › The two-step test).
  • Classical (four corners): Thompson v. Libby (Parol Evidence Rule › Classical approach).
  • Modern (Corbin): Taylor v. State Farm; Shay v. Aldrich (Parol Evidence Rule › Modern (Corbin and Restatement) approach).
  • Exceptions: interpretation, fraud, duress, mistake, conditions precedent, later modifications, trade usage. Sherrodd v. Morrison-Knudsen read the fraud exception narrowly (Parol Evidence Rule › Exceptions).
  • UCC § 2-202 (Parol Evidence Rule › UCC 2-202).

C. Interpretation

  • Objective meaning; plain meaning vs. contextual approaches; patent and latent ambiguity; UCC § 1-303 hierarchy; contra proferentem [Outside knowledge] (Interpretation).

IV. Defenses

A. Capacity

  • Minors: voidable; disaffirm within a reasonable time after majority; ratification; necessaries. Dodson v. Shrader (depreciation offset absent overreaching) (Capacity › Minors).
  • Mental incapacity: void if adjudicated; otherwise voidable under the cognitive or volitional test (the volitional test requires that the other party had reason to know). Sparrow v. Demonico (medical evidence required); Ortelere v. Teachers Retirement Board (Capacity › Mental incapacity).

B. Duress and undue influence

  • Economic duress (Totem): wrongful threat + no reasonable alternative + inducement (Duress and Undue Influence › Economic duress; Totem Marine v. Alyeska Pipeline).
  • Undue influence: unfair persuasion + undue susceptibility, weighed with the Odorizzi factors (Duress and Undue Influence › Undue influence; Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District); Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing.

C. Misrepresentation and nondisclosure

  • Fraud in the inducement: knowing misrepresentation + intent + inducement + justified reliance → voidable (Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure › Fraudulent misrepresentation; Syester v. Banta).
  • Fraud in the execution → void. In Park 100 Investors v. Kartes, a guaranty disguised as "lease papers" (Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure › Fraud in the execution vs. inducement).
  • Nondisclosure: classical caveat emptor (Laidlaw v. Organ) vs. modern § 161 (Hill v. Jones, termites) (Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure › Nondisclosure).

D. Unconscionability

  • Procedural + substantive on a sliding scale, decided by the court, judged at formation (Unconscionability › The two prongs). Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture (remanded, not decided).
  • Arbitration and the FAA: Higgins v. Superior Court (Unconscionability › Arbitration clauses and the FAA).
  • Price and interest rates: De La Torre v. CashCall (Unconscionability › Price and interest-rate unconscionability).
  • Remedies: refuse to enforce, sever the term, or limit it. Affirmative relief is a minority view (In re Checking Account Overdraft Litigation) (Unconscionability › Remedies).

E. Mistake

  • Mutual: a basic assumption, material effect, and the party doesn't bear the risk (Mistake › Mutual mistake). Lenawee County Board of Health v. Messerly ("as is" put the risk on the buyers); Sherwood v. Walker; Gartner v. Eikill.
  • Unilateral: the same elements plus unconscionability or that the other party had reason to know (Mistake › Unilateral mistake). BMW Financial Services v. Deloach (no relief); Donovan v. RRL Corp (relief).
  • Risk (§ 154): by agreement, conscious ignorance, or court allocation (Mistake › Allocation of risk (Restatement 154)).

F. Impracticability and frustration

  • Impossibility: Taylor v. Caldwell (Impracticability and Frustration › Impossibility).
  • Impracticability (§ 261 / UCC § 2-615): Mineral Park Land v. Howard; market change is not enough, per Hemlock Semiconductor v. Solarworld (Impracticability and Frustration › Impracticability).
  • Frustration (§ 265): Krell v. Henry; "virtually worthless," per Mel Frank Tool v. Di-Chem; Lloyd v. Murphy (slide mention only) (Impracticability and Frustration › Frustration of purpose).

V. Performance and breach

A. Express conditions

  • Strict compliance; substantial performance doesn't count (Express Conditions › Strict compliance; Oppenheimer v. Oppenheim Appel Dixon; Maxton Builders v. Lo Galbo).
  • Condition vs. promise; § 227 reads doubtful terms against forfeiture (Express Conditions › Conditions vs. promises).
  • Excuse: waiver, prevention, immateriality, forfeiture (Express Conditions › Excuse of conditions). Under JNA Realty v. Cross Bay Chelsea: forfeiture + no prejudice + venial inattention (Express Conditions › Excuse to avoid forfeiture).

B. Substantial performance and material breach

  • Constructive conditions; substantial performance (Material Breach and Substantial Performance › Substantial performance; Jacob and Youngs v. Kent).
  • Partial → material (suspend; allow cure) → total (terminate) (Material Breach and Substantial Performance › Partial, material and total breach; Sackett v. Spindler).

C. Anticipatory repudiation and adequate assurance

  • Repudiation must be definite and unequivocal. Retraction is possible until the other side relies, cancels or treats it as final (Anticipatory Repudiation and Adequate Assurance › Repudiation; Truman L. Flatt and Sons v. Schupf).
  • UCC § 2-609: reasonable grounds for insecurity → a written demand → no adequate assurance within 30 days = repudiation (Anticipatory Repudiation and Adequate Assurance › Adequate assurance (UCC 2-609); Hornell Brewing v. Spry).

VI. Remedies

A. Expectation damages

  • Loss in value + other loss − cost avoided − loss avoided (§ 347) (Expectation Damages › The general formula (Restatement 347)).
  • Land: contract price vs. market value (Expectation Damages › Sale of real estate; Crabbys v. Hamilton).
  • Employment: replacement cost (Expectation Damages › Employment contracts; Handicapped Childrens Education Board v. Lukaszewski).
  • Cost of completion vs. diminution in value (Expectation Damages › Cost of completion vs. diminution in value; Jacob and Youngs).

B. Limits

  • Foreseeability (Limits on Damages › Foreseeability (Hadley); Hadley v. Baxendale; Florafax v. GTE Market Resources).
  • Certainty (net profits; new businesses) (Limits on Damages › Certainty and causation).
  • Mitigation (Limits on Damages › Mitigation (avoidable consequences); Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge). In employment cases, the employer must prove comparable work was available (Limits on Damages › Mitigation in employment cases; Maness v. Collins).
  • Attorney fees follow the American rule (Limits on Damages › Attorney fees (American rule); Zapata Hermanos v. Hearthside Baking).
  • Emotional distress is not recoverable (Limits on Damages › Emotional distress; Erlich v. Menezes).

C. Reliance and restitution

  • Reliance damages when expectation is uncertain; the promissory estoppel remedy (Reliance and Restitution Damages › Reliance damages; Wartzman v. Hightower Productions; Walser v. Toyota Motor Sales).
  • Restitution for the non-breaching party: quantum meruit, even on a losing contract (Reliance and Restitution Damages › Restitution as a remedy for breach; US ex rel Coastal Steel v. Algernon Blair).
  • Breaching party (§ 374): Lancellotti v. Thomas (Reliance and Restitution Damages › Restitution for the breaching party).
  • Where an express contract governs: Shaffer v. George Washington University (Reliance and Restitution Damages › Restitution when the contract doesn't cover the issue).

D. Specific performance

  • Damages inadequate → equitable factors → practical limits (Specific Performance › Three limits; City Stores v. Ammerman).
  • No affirmative specific performance of personal services; negative injunctions are limited (Specific Performance › Personal services contracts; Reier Broadcasting v. Kramer).

E. Liquidated damages

  • Enforceable if a reasonable forecast and damages were hard to estimate; otherwise a penalty. Single look vs. second look (Liquidated Damages › Tests, Liquidated Damages › Single look vs. second look). No slide cases; the principal case is Barrie School v. Patch.

VII. Third parties (not in the syllabus)

  • [Outside knowledge] Intended vs. incidental beneficiaries; vesting; assignment and delegation (Third-Party Beneficiaries and Assignment).
