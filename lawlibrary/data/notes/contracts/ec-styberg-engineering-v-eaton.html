Quick reference

Citation: 492 F.3d 912 (7th Cir. 2007) · Casebook: Knapp p. 140 · Topic: UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation One line: Despite e-mails suggesting a minimum purchase commitment, sophisticated parties never formed a contract for 13,000 brake units. Their past practice of formal purchase orders showed they had not reached agreement.

Overview

The contrast case to Jannusch v. Naffziger. Even under § 2-204's liberal formation rules, there must be intent to contract. The parties' past practice can be the strongest evidence that they did not agree this time.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: E.C. Styberg Engineering Co. A maker of custom components. It wanted damages for Eaton's alleged breach of a contract to buy 13,000 I-brakes.
  • Defendant / appellee: Eaton Corp. A maker of vehicle transmissions. It denied any binding volume commitment.

Facts

  • 1998-2000: Styberg made the "Inertia Brake Assembly" (I-brake, Part No. A-6871) for Eaton's six-speed transmissions. Prototypes and limited quantities came first, while Eaton tested the market.
  • 1999: the parties negotiated a large production agreement. Styberg wanted a minimum purchase commitment to protect its capital spending.
  • May 27, 1999: Eaton engineer Al Davis e-mailed Styberg's John Baker that Eaton was "willing to give Styberg that commitment" and "at the very least ... will guarantee" a minimum. Later communications, quotes and drafts never produced a signed long-term agreement.
  • Eaton placed orders through its usual practice: a specific purchase order, answered by Styberg's acknowledgment form. Eaton later stopped buying. Styberg claimed a contract for 13,000 units.

Procedural History

  • Bench trial: the district court found no contract existed and entered judgment for Eaton.
  • Seventh Circuit: affirmed (clear-error review of the factual finding).

Issue

Did the parties' negotiations and communications form a contract under UCC § 2-204 for Eaton to buy a minimum of 13,000 I-brakes?

Holding

No contract. The district court's finding that the parties never agreed to a 13,000-unit commitment was not clearly erroneous.

Reasoning

  • § 2-204 allows a contract to form by conduct, without identifying the moment of making, and with open terms. But there must be intent to contract on the alleged terms.
  • The evidence was ambiguous. Davis's e-mail expressed willingness ("I believe Eaton will..."), and later negotiations continued over terms, which suggests no final agreement.
  • Past practice: "historically, Eaton would submit a specific purchase order to Styberg and Styberg would send Eaton a purchase order acknowledgment form." The court could find Eaton's orders were placed under the parties' previous arrangement dating to 1998, not a new 13,000-unit contract.
  • On clear-error review, the district court's choice between two reasonable interpretations stands (Anderson v. Bessemer City).

Rule

Under § 2-204, a contract for the sale of goods requires conduct or communications showing mutual intent to be bound. Continued negotiation over material terms, and a course of dealing that required formal documents, can show that no contract was formed.

Why It Matters

  • Contrast with Jannusch (slides): sophisticated parties, extensive written communications, a longer relationship and millions at stake, versus Jannusch's unambiguous buyer conduct.
  • The past practice in concluding prior deals was the strongest evidence of no agreement this time.
  • Note 3, acceptance through conduct: § 2-204(2) allows a binding contract even when the moment of formation cannot be pinned down. That helps a plaintiff only if conduct actually shows agreement.

Other Key Points

  • A § 2-204(2) note at slide 24.
  • Related: course of dealing and course of performance (§ 1-303) as evidence of agreement. See Parol Evidence Rule and Interpretation.
  • Exam angle: when parties are still negotiating a "master agreement," look for objective signs of commitment (signed documents, performance, reliance) versus signs of continued bargaining.
