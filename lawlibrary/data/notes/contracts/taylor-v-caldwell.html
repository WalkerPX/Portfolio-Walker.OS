The contract rested on the hall's continued existence.

Coverage

Summarized in Knapp's introductory text (not reprinted). The slides list it as a foundational case.

Overview

The "fountainhead of the modern law of impossibility" (Farnsworth, quoted in Knapp). It is the first notable exception to the old rule of strict liability for contract promises (Paradine v. Jane).

Parties

  • Plaintiff: Taylor, who rented the hall to give musical performances (concerts and fêtes). He wanted damages for the hall's unavailability.
  • Defendant: Caldwell, the owner of the Surrey Gardens music hall [Outside knowledge: name of venue], who had agreed to let Taylor use it.

Facts

  • From Knapp: Caldwell agreed to rent a music hall to Taylor for several days so Taylor could present musical performances.
  • The hall burned down shortly before the first performance, without either party's fault. Taylor sued for breach.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] Tried before a jury with leave reserved. The court (Blackburn, J.) entered judgment for the defendant.

Issue

Is a party excused from performing when the specific thing essential to performance is destroyed, without fault, before performance is due?

Holding

Yes. Caldwell was excused. Because the hall was "essential" to performance and the parties contracted "on the basis of [its] continued existence," its accidental destruction excused performance.

Reasoning

  • Courts imply a condition that the parties will be excused if performance becomes impossible through the perishing of the person or thing essential to it, before breach and without fault.
  • Contracts for personal services are excused by the death of the performer by analogy.

Rule

Where performance depends on the continued existence of a specific person or thing, and that person dies or the thing is destroyed without fault, the duty to perform is excused (Restatement (Second) §§ 262-263; UCC § 2-613 for identified goods).

Why It Matters

  • It is the start of the trilogy of excuse doctrines: impossibility (Taylor), frustration (Krell v. Henry), impracticability (Mineral Park Land v. Howard).
  • Knapp: the principle extends to death or incapacity of a necessary person, destruction of specific goods (easy with unique goods like a racehorse, harder with fungible goods; compare Bende & Sons, combat boots), and governmental prohibition (Restatement § 264; UCC § 2-615 cmt. 10).
  • The old test required objective ("no one could do it") rather than subjective ("I can't do it") impossibility (Restatement (First) § 455).
