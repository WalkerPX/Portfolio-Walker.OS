The short version

Minors and people whose mental condition keeps them from understanding or controlling a deal can often cancel their contracts. But they may have to give back what they received or pay a fair value for necessities.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Capacity.

What it's about

Contract law assumes both sides are able to look after their own interests. When one side legally can't, the contract is usually voidable: that person may cancel it, but doesn't have to.

Minors (under 18)

  • A minor can disaffirm (cancel) most contracts before turning 18 or within a reasonable time after.
  • The minor must give back whatever they still have. Some courts also let the seller subtract for use and wear, especially if the seller dealt fairly.
  • Minors must still pay a reasonable value for necessaries (food, shelter, basic medical care).

Mental incapacity

Courts use two tests (Restatement § 15):

  • Cognitive test: the person couldn't understand the nature and consequences of the deal.
  • Volitional test: the person understood but, because of mental illness, couldn't act reasonably, and the other side knew or should have known. Fairness matters: if the other side acted in good faith without knowing, and has already performed, a court may limit relief.

Intoxication

Being drunk is a defense only in extreme cases, where the other side had reason to know you couldn't understand or act reasonably.

An everyday example

A 16-year-old buys a used car, drives it for months, then wants their money back. They can usually cancel, but they'll return the car and the seller may be able to deduct for depreciation.

The cases, in plain words

  • Dodson v. Shrader: a 16-year-old returned a damaged truck and got his money back, minus the truck's loss in value and his use of it.
  • Ortelere v. Teachers Retirement Board: a teacher with severe depression made a pension choice that made no sense for her family. The leading case on the volitional test.
  • Sparrow v. Demonico: to prove incapacity, you need medical or expert evidence, not just testimony that you were upset.
  • Credit Bureau Enterprises v. Pelo: even if a hospital form isn't binding, a patient still pays the reasonable value of necessary care.

Words you'll hear

  • Voidable: valid unless the protected party chooses to cancel it.
  • Disaffirm: cancel a contract as a minor.
  • Necessaries: basic needs a minor must pay fair value for.
