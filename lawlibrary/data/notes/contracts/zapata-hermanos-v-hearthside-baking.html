Quick reference

Citation: 313 F.3d 385 (7th Cir. 2002) (Posner, J.) · Casebook: Knapp p. 968 · Topic: Limits on Damages › Attorney fees (American rule) One line: A winning seller could not recover its attorney's fees as "losses" under the CISG. Under the American rule, fees require a contract term, a statute or a court's sanction power, and pre-litigation bad faith doesn't qualify.

Overview

The course case on the American rule that each side bears its own attorney's fees, and its exceptions. It also shows the CISG's growing role in transnational sales.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Zapata Hermanos Sucesores, S.A., a Mexican supplier of cookie tins. It won its breach claim and wanted attorney's fees.
  • Defendant / appellant: Hearthside Baking Co. (doing business as Lenell), a U.S. wholesale cookie baker. It appealed the fee award.

Facts

  • Zapata sued under the CISG for money due on 110 invoices (about $900,000), plus prejudgment interest and attorney's fees, arguing fees are "losses" recoverable under the Convention.
  • Zapata won on the merits. The district judge ordered Lenell to pay $550,000 in attorney's fees.

Procedural History

  • District court: awarded fees on two theories: (1) fees are foreseeable consequential damages ("loss") under CISG Article 74, and the treaty is federal law equivalent to a statute; (2) the court's inherent power to sanction bad faith by the defendant.
  • Seventh Circuit: affirmed in part, reversed in part and remanded. It reversed both fee theories and remanded to consider whether litigation misconduct could support fees as a sanction.

Issue

  1. Are attorney's fees recoverable as "loss" under CISG Article 74?
  2. May a court award fees as a sanction for a party's pre-litigation bad-faith breach?

Holding

  1. No. The CISG addresses substantive contract law, not the procedural question of who pays attorney's fees. Fees are not "loss" under Article 74.
  2. No. The court's sanction power reaches misconduct in litigation, not a bad-faith breach before suit. Remanded to consider litigation misconduct.

Reasoning

  • American rule vs. English rule: in the U.S., each party usually bears its own fees. The English rule and civil-law systems shift fees to the loser. Because the American rule still applies in U.S. courts, a specific, recognizable basis is required to award fees.
  • Three bases for fees in U.S. courts (slides): (1) a contract provision, (2) a statute, (3) judicial power based on court rules or inherent authority.
  • Reading the CISG to shift fees would create an asymmetry (only prevailing plaintiffs could claim fees as "damages") and would override the American rule without clear treaty language.

Rule

Under the American rule, attorney's fees are not recoverable as contract damages unless authorized by contract, statute, or the court's rule-based or inherent power to sanction litigation misconduct. CISG Article 74 does not authorize fee-shifting.

Why It Matters

  • Note 1: Posner makes a compelling case that the CISG is substantive, but recent decisions and commentary show the issue is still open.
  • Note 2: UCC §§ 2-710 and 2-715 (incidental and consequential damages) have consistently been read not to allow attorney's fees, so Zapata matches the UCC outcome.
  • Note 3: court rules (e.g., Rule 11) let courts sanction frivolous litigation conduct, including fees.
  • Note 4: the contract exception. Freedom of contract supports enforcing a prevailing-party fee clause.
  • Note 6, pros and cons of the American rule: Pros: it avoids deterring plaintiffs who fear paying the other side, and avoids clogging courts with tiny claims. Cons: it deters legitimate non-monetary suits, encourages parties to ignore others' rights, and under-compensates prevailing parties.
