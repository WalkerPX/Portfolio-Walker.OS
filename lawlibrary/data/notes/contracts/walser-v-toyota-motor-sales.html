Quick reference

Citation: 43 F.3d 396 (8th Cir. 1994) · Casebook: Knapp p. 1032 · Topics: Reliance and Restitution Damages › Reliance damages, Promissory Estoppel › Remedy One line: Would-be Lexus dealers who relied on a mistaken assurance of approval won on promissory estoppel but were properly limited to out-of-pocket reliance damages ($232,131), not lost dealership profits.

Overview

The course case on remedies for promissory estoppel. Under Restatement § 90, "the remedy granted for breach may be limited as justice requires," so courts may award reliance rather than expectation damages.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs / appellants: Paul Martin Walser and Philip Martin McLaughlin. Prospective Lexus dealers. They wanted lost profits from the dealership, or specific performance.
  • Defendant / appellee: Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc. (the Lexus distributor). It had mistakenly signaled approval.

Facts

  • Walser was mistakenly told he had been approved as a Lexus dealer. In reliance, his father bought property for the new dealership (slides).
  • Toyota informed the plaintiffs of the mistake only a couple of days after it was made. Negotiations were still preliminary, and the deal was "far from a certainty."

Procedural History

  • Jury: found for the plaintiffs on promissory estoppel but, as instructed, limited damages to out-of-pocket expenses: $232,131. (Their contract claim failed.)
  • The plaintiffs appealed, arguing the instruction was wrong, that specific performance should be available, and other errors.
  • Eighth Circuit: affirmed.

Issue

Did the district court err by limiting promissory estoppel damages to out-of-pocket (reliance) expenses rather than lost profits (expectation)?

Holding

No. Limiting recovery to reliance damages was within the court's discretion, given that the deal was far from certain and the plaintiffs did not show they lost another opportunity by relying.

Reasoning

  • Restatement (Second) § 90: the remedy "may be limited as justice requires." The court has flexibility (Note 3).
  • The dealership deal was "far from a certainty." Negotiations were preliminary, and Toyota tried to rectify the error quickly.
  • The plaintiffs failed to show a lost opportunity (e.g., another dealership they gave up) from relying on the promise.
  • Specific performance was not warranted.

Rule

In a promissory estoppel action, the court may limit recovery to reliance damages (out-of-pocket costs) where justice so requires, particularly where the promised deal was uncertain and expectation damages would overcompensate or are speculative.

Why It Matters

  • Note 1: there is a split of authority on the remedy in detrimental-reliance cases.
  • Note 4: scholars debate how often courts award expectation damages in promissory estoppel and whether they should. If expectation can't be proved with reasonable certainty, the plaintiff is limited to provable reliance damages anyway.
  • Compare Pops Cones v. Resorts International (reliance damages sought, expectation arguably provable) and Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice (lost wages awarded).
