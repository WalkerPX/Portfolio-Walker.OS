Citation: 151 Ariz. 81, 725 P.2d 1115 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1986) · Casebook: Knapp p. 644 · Topic: Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure › Nondisclosure One line: Home sellers had a duty to disclose a known past termite infestation that materially affected the property's value and wasn't readily observable to the buyers.

Overview

The course case on nondisclosure. It departs from the classical "no duty to disclose" rule (Laidlaw v. Organ) and holds that a seller of residential property must disclose known facts that materially affect value and aren't readily observable by the buyer.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs / appellants: Warren G. and Gloria R. Hill (buyers). They wanted to rescind the agreement to purchase the house.
  • Defendants / appellees: Ora G. and Barbara R. Jones (sellers). They argued they had no duty to disclose the termite history.

Facts

  • The Hills agreed to buy the Joneses' residence.
  • Before closing, the buyers asked about a ridge in the floor. [Outside knowledge: the seller said it was water damage.] They received a termite inspection report saying there was no visible infestation. [Outside knowledge.]
  • After moving in (or learning before closing), the buyers discovered the house had a history of termite infestation and damage. The sellers knew and had not disclosed it (they had treated the house previously).

Procedural History

  • The trial court dismissed the misrepresentation claim based on the agreement's integration clause. It then granted summary judgment to the sellers on the concealment claim (no duty to disclose), and awarded sellers $1,000 in attorney's fees.
  • Court of Appeals: reversed and remanded.

Issue

Does the seller of a residence have a duty to disclose known facts about past termite infestation and damage to the buyer?

Holding

Yes. Where the seller knows of facts materially affecting the property's value that are not readily observable and not known to the buyer, the seller has a duty to disclose them. Past termite infestation may be such a fact. Summary judgment was improper.

Reasoning

  • Classical view: no duty to disclose in an arm's-length business transaction (caveat emptor; Laidlaw v. Organ).
  • Modern view: nondisclosure is equivalent to misrepresentation in several situations under Restatement (Second) § 161. In broad terms, nondisclosure is actionable if it amounts to a failure to act in good faith and in accordance with reasonable standards of fair dealing.
  • Materiality: a reasonable buyer would attach importance to a termite history in deciding whether to buy and at what price.
  • The buyers' inspection report and ability to look did not necessarily put them on notice, since the damage was not readily observable.

Rule

A seller of real property has a duty to disclose facts that (1) are known to the seller, (2) materially affect the property's value, (3) are not readily observable, and (4) are not known to the buyer.

Why It Matters

  • Note 1: the shift from the classical rule (Laidlaw) to the modern rule (Hill, Restatement § 161).
  • Note 2, Keeton factors for imposing a duty: the withholding party's knowledge of the fact, its awareness of the fact's significance, reliance, and the relationship of the parties.
  • Week 7 Part 2 slides, nondisclosure: a duty arises when the defect is unknown to the other party and is the kind it is unlikely to discover or ask about. Rescission is available if the facts have a material effect, are not readily observable, and are not otherwise known, and one of four circumstances applies: (1) disclosure required by statute, (2) intentional concealment, (3) a relationship of trust and confidence, or (4) disclosure needed to correct a mistake.
  • Mistake overlap (Week 8 slides): Hill is also used to show where mistake and misrepresentation overlap.

Other Key Points

  • Integration (merger) clauses do not bar fraud claims under the majority view. Compare Sherrodd v. Morrison-Knudsen.
