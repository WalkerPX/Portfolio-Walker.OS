In one paragraph

Third-party beneficiaries: a non-party may enforce a contract only if it is an intended beneficiary, meaning the parties meant to give it the benefit of the promised performance (Restatement § 302). Incidental beneficiaries can't sue. An intended beneficiary's rights vest once it relies, sues or assents, and after that the original parties can't modify them away. Assignment transfers contract rights, and delegation transfers duties. Most rights are assignable and most duties delegable, unless the contract forbids it, the change would materially alter the other side's position, or the performance is personal. A delegating party stays liable.

Third-party beneficiaries

  • Intended vs. incidental (Restatement § 302): a beneficiary is intended if recognizing a right in it carries out the parties' intent and either (a) performance will satisfy a debt the promisee owes the beneficiary (the old "creditor beneficiary"), or (b) the promisee intends to give the beneficiary the benefit (the old "donee beneficiary"). Everyone else is incidental and has no rights.
  • Classic cases: Lawrence v. Fox (N.Y. 1859; creditor beneficiary); Seaver v. Ransom (N.Y. 1918; donee beneficiary).
  • Factors: the contract names the beneficiary; performance runs directly to the beneficiary; the promisee's purpose.
  • Government contracts: members of the public are usually incidental beneficiaries unless the contract shows otherwise (§ 313).
  • Vesting (§ 311): the parties may change or cancel the beneficiary's rights until the beneficiary (a) materially changes position in justifiable reliance, (b) sues, or (c) assents at a party's request.
  • Defenses: the promisor may raise against the beneficiary any defense it has against the promisee (§ 309).

Assignment of rights

  • Generally allowed (§ 317) unless it would materially change the obligor's duty, increase its burden or risk, or impair its chance of return performance; or it is forbidden by statute or public policy; or validly precluded by contract.
  • Anti-assignment clauses (§ 322): read narrowly. A ban usually bars only delegation of duties, and assigning the right to payment generally can't be barred (UCC § 9-406 for accounts).
  • Effect: the assignee "stands in the shoes" of the assignor, subject to the obligor's defenses. The obligor may pay the assignor until it gets notice of the assignment.

Delegation of duties

  • Generally allowed (§ 318; UCC § 2-210(1)) unless the obligee has a substantial interest in having the original obligor perform personally (personal services, unique skill or judgment), or the contract forbids it.
  • The delegating party remains liable unless there is a novation (the obligee agrees to release it).
  • Assumption: if the delegate promises to perform, the obligee is an intended beneficiary of that promise and can sue the delegate.
  • UCC § 2-210(5): the other party may treat a delegation as reasonable grounds for insecurity and demand assurances (Anticipatory Repudiation and Adequate Assurance › Adequate assurance (UCC 2-609)).

Exam framework

  1. Third party suing? Intended or incidental? (Look at the parties' intent, who performance runs to, and whether the beneficiary is named.) Have its rights vested? What defenses does the promisor have?
  2. Assignment? Is it permitted (material change, contract ban, public policy)? Was notice given? What defenses travel with it?
  3. Delegation? Is it a personal performance? Is there a contract ban? Is the delegating party still liable (novation)? Did the delegate assume the duty? Does the delegation create insecurity (UCC § 2-210(5))?
