Quick reference

Citation: 650 N.E.2d 347 (Ind. Ct. App. 1995) · Casebook: Knapp p. 657 · Topic: Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure › Fraud in the execution vs. inducement One line: Business owners who signed a personal guaranty that the landlord's agent misdescribed as "lease papers," under time pressure, were not bound. Active misrepresentation of a document's character overrides the duty to read.

Overview

The course case on fraud in the execution (misrepresenting the nature of the document signed). It limits the "duty to read" from Ray v. Eurice Bros when the other party actively misrepresents what is being signed.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Park 100 Investors, Inc. The landlord of an Indianapolis industrial complex. It wanted to enforce a personal guaranty for unpaid rent.
  • Defendants / appellees: James and Nancy Kartes. Part-owners and officers of Kartes Video Communications, Inc. (KVC), the tenant. They argued they never knowingly agreed to guarantee the lease.

Facts

  • 1984: KVC needed larger facilities. Park 100's representative, Robert Scannell, marketed space. James Kartes delegated negotiations to KVC senior VP David Kaplan. The lease form contained no personal guaranty, and a guaranty was never mentioned in negotiations. KVC's lawyer approved the lease, and Kaplan signed and delivered it.
  • Friday, July 27, 1984, 5:00 p.m., the evening before the move-in: Scannell found the Karteses getting into their car. They were late for their daughter's wedding rehearsal. He said he had "lease papers" for them to sign, that it couldn't wait until Monday, and that KVC couldn't move in until they signed.
  • Scannell produced a document headed "Lease Agreement." From the lobby, James phoned Kaplan to ask if the lease had been approved by KVC's lawyer. Scannell remained silent. Scannell opened the papers to the signature page, and both signed. As officers, they didn't think it unusual to sign a lease.
  • The document was actually a personal guaranty. KVC later defaulted.

Procedural History

  • The trial court found the Karteses not liable on the guaranty.
  • Indiana Court of Appeals: affirmed.

Issue

Are signers bound by a personal guaranty they signed without reading, when the other party's agent represented it as "lease papers"?

Holding

No. Park 100's agent actively misrepresented the nature of the document, and the Karteses acted with ordinary care in relying on that characterization. The guaranty is unenforceable against them.

Reasoning

  • One ordinarily cannot avoid a signed agreement by claiming ignorance of its terms (the duty to read). But active misrepresentation may override that principle.
  • Fraud in the execution: the signer is misled about the character of the document itself, as opposed to fraud in the inducement, where the signer knows what she is signing but is misled about facts that induce it.
  • Whether a party may rely on the other side's characterization of a writing is a question of fact. Here, the guaranty was never discussed, the document was headed "Lease Agreement," there was time pressure, James checked with Kaplan about the lawyer's approval, and Scannell kept silent. The defendants acted with ordinary care.

Rule

A party who is induced to sign a document by the other party's misrepresentation of its nature or character is not bound, if her reliance on the misrepresentation was reasonable (exercising ordinary care), notwithstanding the general duty to read.

Why It Matters

  • Contrast with Ray v. Eurice Bros (slides): Kartes involved active fraud by the other party in misdescribing the document. In Ray, the builder simply failed to read what it signed.
  • Fraud in the execution makes the contract void (no assent to the actual document). Fraud in the inducement makes it voidable (slides, Week 7 Part 1).

Other Key Points

  • Hypo 4, "Just Sign the Lease" (slides): the agent says "Yes, this is the lease," but the document is a guaranty headed "Commercial Lease Agreement," and the CEO skims page one. Apply Park 100. Does the CEO's sophistication matter? How should the landlord structure the transaction (a separate, clearly labeled guaranty, explicit disclosure, time to review)?
