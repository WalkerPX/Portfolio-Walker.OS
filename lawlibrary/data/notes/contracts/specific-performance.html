In one paragraph

Specific performance is an equitable order to perform the contract. A related tool is an injunction ordering a party not to act. It is available only when money damages are inadequate. Courts then weigh equitable factors (hardship, delay, benefit received, the plaintiff's reliance) and practical limits (is the contract definite enough, and can the court supervise the decree?). Modern courts are more generous: City Stores v. Ammerman ordered a shopping-center developer to lease space to a department store. Land is presumed unique. For goods, the UCC allows the remedy where goods are unique "or in other proper circumstances." Courts never order a person to perform personal services, and they grant negative injunctions only in limited cases (Reier Broadcasting v. Kramer).

Three limits

  1. Inadequacy of damages (the threshold). If money can make the plaintiff whole, no specific performance.
    • Typical reasons damages are inadequate: the subject is unique (land, heirlooms, art); damages are too uncertain to prove; substitutes are unavailable; the defendant is insolvent [Outside knowledge].
  2. Equitable factors:
    • hardship on the breaching party, disproportionate to the plaintiff's gain;
    • whether the plaintiff pursued the claim promptly (laches);
    • whether the defendant received a benefit from the plaintiff's performance;
    • whether the plaintiff changed position in reliance;
    • [Outside knowledge] unclean hands, unfairness of the bargain, mistake (Restatement § 364).
  3. Practical limits:
    • Is the agreement definite enough to frame an order (Restatement § 362 [Outside knowledge])?
    • Can the court supervise performance? Long-running construction or services are hard to supervise.
    • Can the court shape an enforceable decree?

City Stores and modern liberality

  • City Stores v. Ammerman: the developers promised City Stores (Lansburgh's) a lease in their planned Tysons Corner mall on terms as favorable as those given other major department stores. They used City Stores' letter of support to win zoning approval, then refused. Specific performance ordered.
    • Inadequacy: lost profits many years out would be difficult if not impossible to prove; damages couldn't capture the "almost incalculable future advantages" of expanding into the suburbs (p. 1073).
    • Definiteness: standard lease terms in the trade and the other anchor tenants' leases supplied the missing terms.
    • Supervision: not a serious obstacle.
    • Equities: the developers benefited (zoning approval), and City Stores relied by passing up a comparable mall opportunity.
    • Counterfactual (Notes 4-5): if the developers had already leased all the department-store spaces to others, specific relief would probably be denied. No court would order the mall expanded or existing leases breached.
  • Land: presumed unique. Specific performance is routine for buyers, and often available to sellers as well [Outside knowledge]. See also Beaver v. Brumlow (part performance → specific performance).

Personal services contracts

  • No affirmative specific performance of personal services. It is hard to compel good performance, courts would have to supervise, and it resembles involuntary servitude (Thirteenth Amendment concerns [Outside knowledge]).
  • Negative injunctions: a court may sometimes forbid the employee from working for a competitor during the contract term. [Outside knowledge] The classic case is Lumley v. Wagner (an opera singer barred from singing elsewhere). Usually this requires unique or extraordinary services and an express or implied negative covenant (exclusivity), and must not leave the employee without a means of living.
  • Reier Broadcasting v. Kramer: Montana State's football coach Kramer had an exclusive contract (through Nov. 2004) to appear on Reier's "Cat Chat" radio show. When MSU gave its broadcast rights to a competitor, Kramer was to perform for the new station. Held: no injunction. Reier's remedy was damages. [Outside knowledge] a Montana statute bars injunctions against breaching a contract whose performance couldn't be specifically enforced. Since Kramer's services couldn't be compelled, a negative injunction that would indirectly compel them was also unavailable, and damages were adequate.
  • Employer's damages when an employee quits: Expectation Damages › Employment contracts (Handicapped Childrens Education Board v. Lukaszewski).

UCC (goods)

  • § 2-716 [Outside knowledge: section number]: specific performance where goods are unique or "in other proper circumstances," for example when finding a replacement would be very burdensome (Note 7).
  • Inadequacy still matters: if goods are readily available on the market, specific performance will almost certainly be denied. The buyer covers and sues for the difference.

Exam framework

  1. Are damages inadequate? Uniqueness, uncertainty (City Stores), no substitutes. Land → yes; market goods → no.
  2. Equitable factors: hardship, delay, benefit to the defendant, reliance by the plaintiff, clean hands.
  3. Practical limits: definiteness (fill gaps from trade practice and comparable deals), supervision, enforceability.
  4. Personal services? No affirmative order. Consider a negative injunction: unique services, an exclusivity covenant, irreparable harm, and the employee can still earn a living (Reier).
  5. Goods? § 2-716, unique or other proper circumstances.
  6. If denied, go back to damages (Expectation Damages, Reliance and Restitution Damages).

Hypos (slides)

  • City Stores counterfactual: all department-store spaces already leased → specific relief probably denied.
  • UCC: a replacement would be very burdensome to find → supports specific performance. Goods readily available → denied.
