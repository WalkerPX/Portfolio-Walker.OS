Quick reference

Citation: 222 N.Y. 88, 118 N.E. 214 (1917) (Cardozo, J.) · Casebook: Knapp p. 486 · Topics: Good Faith and Fair Dealing, Consideration One line: An exclusive agency contract implies a promise by the agent to use reasonable efforts, so the agent's side is not illusory and the contract is enforceable.

Overview

Cardozo's famous implied-promise case. A contract that seems to lack a return promise may be "instinct with an obligation, imperfectly expressed." Granting exclusive rights implies a duty of reasonable efforts, codified for goods in UCC § 2-306(2).

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Otis F. Wood [Outside knowledge: first name]. An advertising and marketing agent with the exclusive right to place Lady Duff-Gordon's endorsements. He wanted damages for her breach of exclusivity.
  • Defendant / respondent: Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon ("Lucile"). A famous fashion designer (slides: she survived the Titanic). She wanted the contract declared void for lack of consideration.

Facts

  • Lady Duff-Gordon gave Wood the exclusive right for one year (renewable) to place her endorsements on others' designs and to market her designs. She would get half of all profits and revenues.
  • Wood's only express duties were to account for revenues and to secure patents and copyrights as needed.
  • She then placed her endorsement on goods (including through Sears) without Wood's involvement and kept the profits.
  • Her defense: Wood never promised to do anything, so there was no consideration and no valid contract.

Procedural History

  • The trial court overruled her demurrer ([Outside knowledge]). The Appellate Division reversed and dismissed the complaint for lack of mutuality.
  • Court of Appeals: the Appellate Division's judgment is reversed, and judgment for the plaintiff on the demurrer reinstated.

Issue

Is an exclusive agency agreement enforceable when the agent makes no express promise to perform any marketing efforts?

Holding

Yes. A promise by Wood to use reasonable efforts to market her endorsements is implied. That implied promise is consideration for her grant of exclusivity, so the contract is binding.

Reasoning

  • "The law has outgrown its primitive stage of formalism when the precise word was the sovereign talisman." A promise may be lacking, "and yet the whole writing may be 'instinct with an obligation,' imperfectly expressed."
  • Implication from the whole agreement: she gave an exclusive privilege, and her income depended entirely on Wood's efforts. Without an implied promise by Wood, the transaction could not have the "business efficacy" the parties intended.
  • Wood's promise to account for profits and take out patents makes sense only if he was bound to use reasonable efforts to bring profits into existence.

Rule

Where one party grants another exclusive rights and depends on that party's efforts for its return, the law implies a promise to use reasonable efforts, which supplies consideration and makes the contract enforceable. For goods: UCC § 2-306(2) (exclusive dealing imposes "best efforts" duties on both sides).

Why It Matters

  • It is the foundation for implied terms and the implied obligation of good faith. It saves contracts from being illusory.
  • Note 2 (Eisenberg): some deals seek only a chance to perform (speculative), but Lady Duff-Gordon had a well-established business.
  • Note 3: UCC § 2-306(2) codifies Wood for goods. Wood itself involved services, so the common law governs it.
  • Note 4: what does "reasonable efforts" mean? Joint profit maximization (the economic view) or a flexible jury standard?

Other Key Points

  • Hypo 4, "Exclusive Rights" (slides): MegaCorp gives Agent exclusive software licensing for two years at 20%, Agent's only express duty is "maintain reasonable records," and MegaCorp starts licensing directly. Under Wood, Agent is bound to reasonable efforts, so there is a contract and MegaCorp breached exclusivity.
  • Contrast Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing: Wood implies a term to carry out the parties' intent. Leibel implies one as a matter of fairness.
  • Related: Marshall Durbin Food v. Baker (illusory vs. conditional promises).
