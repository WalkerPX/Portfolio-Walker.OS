Quick reference

Citation: 124 N.Y. 538, 27 N.E. 256 (1891) · Casebook: Knapp p. 89 · Topic: Consideration One line: A nephew's giving up drinking, smoking, swearing and gambling until 21, things he had a legal right to do, was consideration for his uncle's promise of $5,000, even though it benefited only the nephew.

Overview

The classic benefit/detriment consideration case. Forbearance from exercising a legal right is a legal detriment, and detriment to the promisee alone is sufficient. The promisor need not receive any benefit.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Louisa W. Hamer. She acquired the nephew's claim "through several mesne assignments." She wanted $5,000 plus interest from February 6, 1875.
  • Defendant / respondent: Franklin Sidway, executor of the estate of William E. Story, Sr. (the uncle). He refused payment, arguing no consideration.
  • The nephew: William E. Story, 2d, who performed.

Facts

  • March 20, 1869: at the golden-wedding celebration of the uncle's parents, "in the presence of the family and invited guests," the uncle promised the nephew $5,000 if he would refrain from drinking liquor, using tobacco, swearing, and playing cards or billiards for money until he turned 21. The nephew agreed.
  • He fully performed. On turning 21 (1875) he wrote to his uncle. The uncle replied that he had the $5,000 set aside and would hold it with interest until the nephew was capable of using it wisely. The nephew agreed.
  • The uncle died in 1887 without paying. The executor refused the claim.

Procedural History

  • Trial at Special Term: judgment for the plaintiff.
  • The General Term (Fourth Department) reversed and granted a new trial (July 1, 1890).
  • Court of Appeals: the General Term's order is reversed and the Special Term's judgment for the plaintiff is affirmed.

Issue

Is a promisee's forbearance from lawful conduct, which benefits the promisee and not the promisor, sufficient consideration for the promise?

Holding

Yes. The nephew's forbearance was valid consideration. The promise was enforceable and the estate had to pay.

Reasoning

  • The court quotes the Exchequer Chamber definition: consideration may consist "either in some right, interest, profit or benefit accruing to the one party, or some forbearance, detriment, loss or responsibility given, suffered or undertaken by the other."
  • Detriment does not require actual harm. It means giving up a legal right. The nephew had a legal right to drink and smoke, and he restricted his lawful freedom of action on the strength of the promise.
  • It is irrelevant whether the forbearance actually benefited the promisor, or even benefited the promisee. Courts will not ask whether the thing was good for him.
  • The later letter created a trust-like holding of the money, but the core point is that the original contract was supported by consideration.

Rule

Consideration exists when the promisee gives up a legal right, or does or refrains from doing something it was not otherwise obligated to do, in exchange for the promise. Legal detriment to the promisee is sufficient, without benefit to the promisor.

Why It Matters

  • It is the foundation of the traditional benefit/detriment test (slides: "detriment theory").
  • Variation (a), objective theory (slides): the uncle's subjective motive does not matter if the words, read objectively, sought the forbearance.
  • Variation (b), revocation during performance: under the classical rule, the uncle could revoke before the nephew finished. Under Restatement § 45, beginning performance creates an option (see Cook v. Coldwell Banker).
  • Compare the modern bargain theory in Pennsy Supply v. American Ash Recycling: here, the forbearance was also clearly bargained for, since the uncle wanted it.

Other Key Points

  • Contrast Dougherty v. Salt (affection and "good behavior" are motive, not bargained-for consideration) and Kirksey v. Kirksey (a "mere gratuity").
  • Cited in Marshall Durbin Food v. Baker Note 2 (legal detriment by continuing to work).
  • Hypo (slides): Aunt Phyllis and Ryan, $25,000 not to drink, smoke or gamble until 21. Ryan didn't enjoy those anyway. Is there consideration? Under Hamer, yes. Giving up a legal right is enough, whether or not it hurts.
