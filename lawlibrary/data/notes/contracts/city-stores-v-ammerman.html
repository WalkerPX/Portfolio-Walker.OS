Quick reference

Citation: 266 F. Supp. 766 (D.D.C. 1967), aff'd, 394 F.2d 950 (D.C. Cir. 1968) · Casebook: Knapp p. 1068 · Topic: Specific Performance One line: Shopping center developers who promised a department store a lease in exchange for its support of their rezoning were ordered to specifically perform by giving it a lease on terms as favorable as other major stores got.

Overview

The course illustration of the increased willingness of modern courts to grant specific performance. The court rejects objections based on indefiniteness, supervision and hardship, and considers all three types of limits on specific performance.

Parties

  • Plaintiff: City Stores Co. (owner of the Lansburgh's department store, [Outside knowledge]). It wanted a lease as a major tenant in the Tysons Corner shopping center.
  • Defendants: Ammerman and associates, developers of the Tysons Corner shopping center in Fairfax County, Virginia. They had promised City Stores a lease if they got rezoning, then leased to others.

Facts

  • The developers sought zoning approval for a regional shopping center at Tysons Corner. To support their application, they got a letter of support from City Stores.
  • In exchange, the developers promised City Stores the opportunity to lease space as a major tenant on terms at least equal to those offered to other major department stores in the center.
  • The developers got the rezoning (using City Stores' letter). They then leased to other department stores (e.g., Hecht's) and refused City Stores. City Stores had forgone a comparable opportunity in another shopping center by supporting Tysons.
  • The court granted a preliminary injunction to stop the developers from leasing the last available department-store site.

Procedural History

  • Federal district court (Judge Gasch, [Outside knowledge]): specific performance granted. City Stores had properly exercised its option and was entitled to a lease on terms equal to the Hecht lease.
  • D.C. Circuit: affirmed.

Issue

Should the court order specific performance of a promise to lease department store space, despite objections that the terms were indefinite, that the decree would need supervision, and that it would cause hardship?

Holding

Yes. The agreement was sufficiently definite, damages were inadequate, and the practical and equitable objections did not bar specific performance.

Reasoning

  • Definiteness (Note 1): such leases contain many standard provisions known in the trade, and the other major stores' leases (e.g., Hecht's) supply almost all material terms.
  • Inadequacy of damages (Note 2): proving lost profits for many years into the future would be difficult if not impossible. Damages couldn't compensate for the "almost incalculable future advantages" of expanding into the suburbs (p. 1073).
  • Equitable factors (Notes 1, 4, 5): the defendants benefited from City Stores' letter (restitution interest), and City Stores relied by forgoing another opportunity (reliance interest). Its expectation interest was also affected.
  • Supervision: the court can resolve disputes over terms as they arise. That is not a serious burden here.

Rule

Specific performance may be granted where money damages are inadequate, the contract terms are sufficiently definite (or can be made so by reference to comparable transactions), equitable factors favor relief (hardship, timeliness, clean hands), and the decree is practically enforceable.

Why It Matters

  • Three limits on specific performance (slides): (1) inadequacy of money damages (the fundamental limit); (2) equitable factors (hardship to the breaching party, timely pursuit); (3) practical difficulties in shaping and enforcing a decree.
  • Notes 4-5: if the developers had already signed binding leases with independent parties for all the department-store spaces, specific relief probably would not have been granted. Courts won't order a mall expanded or a breach of obligations to others.
  • Note 7, UCC § 2-716: specific performance for goods that are unique or "in other proper circumstances" (e.g., replacement would be very burdensome). If goods are readily available in the market, it will almost certainly be denied.
