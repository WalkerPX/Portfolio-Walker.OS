In one paragraph

A liquidated damages clause fixes the damages for breach in advance. Courts enforce clauses aimed at compensation and strike penalties meant to punish or coerce performance. The traditional test has three parts: (1) damages were uncertain or hard to prove, (2) the parties intended to liquidate, not penalize, and (3) the sum is a reasonable forecast of just compensation. Restatement § 356 asks whether the amount is reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual loss and the difficulty of proof. Most courts take a "single look" at formation. A growing minority also considers actual harm at breach. A valid clause replaces court-assessed damages, so there is no proof of actual loss and no mitigation inquiry (Barrie School v. Patch). Doubtful clauses are read as penalties, and the challenger bears the burden.

The rule against penalties

  • Settlements after breach, and stipulations fixing damages while litigating only liability, are enforced absent fraud, mistake or duress (p. 1089).
  • Agreed remedies in the original contract get judicial scrutiny: compensation → enforceable; penalty → not (p. 1090).
  • If in doubt, it's a penalty (p. 1095). The challenger has the burden of proving the clause unenforceable (p. 1093).
  • Effect of a penalty [Outside knowledge]: the clause is unenforceable, and the plaintiff gets ordinary court-assessed damages (Expectation Damages).

Tests

Traditional three-part test (Note 1, p. 1100):

  1. Anticipated damages are uncertain in amount or difficult to prove;
  2. the parties intended to liquidate damages rather than penalize;
  3. the amount is a reasonable forecast of just compensation.

Restatement (Second) § 356 (Note 1): enforceable if the amount is reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual loss and the difficulties of proof. The harder the loss is to prove, the easier it is to show the amount is reasonable. A term fixing an unreasonably large amount is unenforceable as a penalty.

Maryland formulations (in Barrie School):

  • Heister (Md. 2006), three essential elements (p. 1094): (1) clear and unambiguous terms for a certain sum; (2) a reasonable estimate of anticipated damages; (3) binding before the fact, not adjustable afterward to match actual damages.
  • Baltimore Bridge (Md. 1915): the named sum is liquidated damages unless "grossly excessive and out of all proportion" to the damages reasonably expected (p. 1093).
  • The parties' intention is decisive, gleaned from the subject matter, the contract language and the circumstances at signing (p. 1094).

UCC § 2-718(1) [Outside knowledge: text]: liquidated damages for sales of goods must be reasonable in light of anticipated or actual harm, difficulty of proof, and the inconvenience of other remedies. An unreasonably large amount is void as a penalty.

Single look vs. second look

ApproachWhen reasonableness is judgedAuthorities (Note 2)
"Single look" (majority, traditional)At formation onlyBarrie School majority; Kelly v. Marx (Mass. 1999); Frick Co. v. Rubel; Wallace Real Estate v. Groves
"Second look" / hindsight (modern trend)At formation and at breach. A sum grossly disproportionate to actual harm may be a penaltyBarrie School dissent; Wasserman's v. Middletown (N.J. 1994); Wassenaar v. Panos (Wis. 1983)
DisjunctiveValid if reasonable in light of anticipated or actual harmCal. & Hawaiian Sugar v. Sun Ship (9th Cir. 1986): $4M upheld despite about $400K in actual harm; sophisticated parties
Penalty rule an "anachronism" between businessesEnforce if reasonable in light of actual harmXCO Int'l v. Pacific Scientific (7th Cir. 2004)
  • No actual loss at all (Note 3): some courts invalidate the clause as a penalty (Colonial at Lynnfield v. Sloan; Arcese v. Daniel Schmitt & Co.). Under the traditional approach, actual harm is irrelevant.

Mitigation and actual harm

  • A valid clause substitutes the parties' assessment for the court's. So the non-breaching party has no duty to mitigate, and a "no actual harm" defense fails (pp. 1095-1097).
  • Lake River Corp. v. Carborundum Co. (7th Cir. 1985, Posner): the clause was a penalty, but the opinion explains that mitigation belongs to court-assessed damages, not party-assessed ones (p. 1096).
  • Compare mitigation generally: Limits on Damages › Mitigation (avoidable consequences).

Barrie School v. Patch (Md. 2007)

Full brief: Barrie School v. Patch.

  • Facts: the parents signed a re-enrollment agreement. Section 3 made them liable for the full year's tuition ($13,490 balance + $1,000 deposit) unless written cancellation arrived before May 31, 2004. They cancelled on July 14, 2004 (p. 1090).
  • Held: a valid liquidated damages clause, not a penalty. One year's tuition was a reasonable forecast because the effect of losing one student was "next to impossible" to estimate. No duty to mitigate. The no-actual-harm defense was rejected. Not an adhesion contract, and an adhesion contract is in any case unenforceable only if unconscionable (p. 1098; Unconscionability).
  • Dissent: review the clause after breach too. The school met its enrollment target ("no empty desk"), so full tuition was grossly disproportionate. The dissent would require proof of actual damages and mitigation (pp. 1098-1100).

Related agreed-remedy terms [Outside knowledge]

  • Limitations of remedy / caps (for example, excluding consequential damages) are generally enforceable unless unconscionable or they fail of their essential purpose (UCC § 2-719). Compare Princess Cruises v. General Electric.
  • Deposits and forfeitures: a breaching buyer may recover a deposit exceeding the seller's damages or a statutory amount (UCC § 2-718(2)-(3)); see Reliance and Restitution Damages › Restitution for the breaching party.
  • Fee-shifting clauses are enforced (Limits on Damages › Attorney fees (American rule)).

Exam framework

  1. Is it a liquidated damages clause (a fixed or determinable sum for breach)? Or a limitation, a deposit, or an alternative performance?
  2. Burden: on the challenger. Doubt: resolve toward penalty.
  3. Traditional test: difficulty of estimating, intent to compensate, reasonable forecast.
  4. § 356: reasonable in light of anticipated or actual loss; difficulty of proof.
  5. Timing: single look at formation vs. second look at breach. Argue both, and use the Barrie School majority and dissent.
  6. Consequences: valid → enforce the sum, no mitigation, no proof of loss. Penalty → actual damages.
  7. Adhesion or unconscionability as a separate attack.
