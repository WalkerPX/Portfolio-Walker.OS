In one paragraph

A court may refuse to enforce a contract or term that was unconscionable when made (Restatement § 208; UCC § 2-302). Most courts require both prongs. Procedural unconscionability means a lack of meaningful choice or terms that are hidden or unintelligible. Substantive unconscionability means terms unreasonably favorable to one side that "shock the conscience." The prongs work on a sliding scale (Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture). The court decides it as a matter of law. Arbitration clauses are protected by the FAA, but a generally applicable defense like unconscionability still applies (Higgins v. Superior Court). Unconscionability can police price and interest rates even where the legislature set no cap (De La Torre v. CashCall). Traditionally it is a defense only. A few cases allow affirmative recovery (In re Checking Account Overdraft Litigation).

The two prongs

Procedural (unfairness in formation). Only one form is needed:

  • (a) Lack of meaningful choice: unequal bargaining power, no alternative sellers, take-it-or-leave-it adhesion contracts.
  • (b) Hidden or unintelligible terms: fine print, convoluted language, high-pressure sales tactics, blank forms filled in later.

Substantive (unfairness of the exchange). Evidence:

  • terms inconsistent with trade practice;
  • terms that seriously disadvantage one side without a substantial added advantage to the other;
  • terms whose coercive effect far exceeds any economic justification.

Sliding scale (Note 1): strong procedural unconscionability needs less substantive, and vice versa. Timing: judged when the contract was made. Decision-maker: the court, as a matter of law, after a chance to present evidence of commercial setting, purpose and effect (UCC § 2-302(2); Note 5).

  • Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture (D.C. Cir., Judge Skelly Wright): Williams, on welfare ($218 a month), bought household goods on installment and then a $514 stereo. An add-on (cross-collateral) clause spread each payment across every item, so the store kept title to everything until all was paid. On default, the store tried to repossess it all. Definition: "an absence of meaningful choice on the part of one of the parties together with contract terms which are unreasonably favorable to the other party." Trap: the court did not hold the contract unconscionable. It held unconscionability was an available defense and remanded for a hearing.
  • Paternalism (Leff): should courts protect people from bargains they chose? Response: buried terms and high pressure mean the choice wasn't truly voluntary.

Adhesion contracts and reasonable expectations

  • Restatement § 211(3): a term in a standardized agreement is not part of the contract if the drafter had reason to believe the other party would not have agreed had they known it was there.
  • Some courts treat adhesion as procedural unconscionability; others apply a separate reasonable expectations test (Higgins Note 2).
  • See also Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute (forum-selection clause in a form ticket; Problem 1-2).

Arbitration clauses and the FAA

  • The Federal Arbitration Act strongly favors arbitration and preempts state rules that single out arbitration clauses.
  • But generally applicable contract defenses (fraud, duress, unconscionability) still apply under FAA § 2. [Outside knowledge] the statute's "save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract" clause, and AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion (2011), which limits defenses that disfavor arbitration in practice.
  • Higgins v. Superior Court: five orphaned Higgins siblings were taken in by the Leomiti family, whose house was rebuilt on Extreme Makeover: Home Edition. The children later alleged verbal abuse and exploitation by the Leomiti children and sued. The production company moved to compel arbitration. Procedural: adhesion, and no expectation that the minors would read the agreement. Substantive: lack of bilaterality (only the children's claims were arbitrable), costs and procedural limits. Held unconscionable, so arbitration could not be compelled.
  • Compare Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing (arbitration agreements attacked as duress and undue influence).

Price and interest-rate unconscionability

  • De La Torre v. CashCall (Cal. 2018): California capped rates on consumer loans under $2,500 but not above. CashCall made $2,500+ loans at 96% to 135% APR.
    • Holding: the lack of a statutory cap does not mean the legislature meant to exempt those loans from unconscionability review. A separate Finance Code provision applies unconscionability to all consumer loans. An interest rate can be unconscionable.
    • Courts are not rate-setters. They have equitable discretion over remedies.
    • Factors: the lender's costs and risks; how salient the term is to consumers; the borrower's creditworthiness; and market imperfections.
  • Note 1: states and the federal government regulate payday lending, and lenders restructure loans to avoid it (as CashCall did with the $2,500 threshold).
  • Classical courts rarely strike down a price term alone. Gross price disparity usually needs procedural problems too. [Outside knowledge]

Remedies

Restatement § 208 / UCC § 2-302 options if a contract or term was unconscionable when made:

  1. Refuse to enforce the whole contract;
  2. Enforce the rest without the unconscionable term; or
  3. Limit the term's application to avoid an unconscionable result.
  • Defense, not sword: traditionally used only to resist enforcement.
  • In re Checking Account Overdraft Litigation (De La Torre Note 4): a minority of recent cases allow unconscionability as a basis for affirmative recovery (for example, of bank overdraft fees). This departs from traditional doctrine, and it is unclear whether it will spread.

Exam framework

  1. Identify the term under attack and the time of formation.
  2. Procedural: meaningful choice? Hidden terms? (One is enough.) Facts: setting, sophistication, alternatives, pressure, fine print, language barriers, age.
  3. Substantive: one-sided? Out of line with trade practice? Coercive beyond any legitimate purpose? For price: De La Torre factors.
  4. Sliding scale: weigh the two.
  5. Arbitration? Address FAA preemption, then argue the defense is generally applicable. Lack of bilaterality, cost and procedural limits are substantive factors (Higgins).
  6. Remedy: the three § 208 options. Court decides. Defense vs. affirmative relief.
  7. Williams trap: remand only.

Hypos (slides)

  • Hypo 1, "The Furniture Store": a Westside cross-collateral clause buried on page 3 of a 4-page form; contracts signed at home with details filled in by the salesperson. Maria defaults on the TV, and Westside seeks to repossess everything. Strong procedural and substantive arguments (Williams). At the remand hearing, offer evidence of the setting, her understanding and trade practice.
  • Hypo 2, "The Reality Show Waiver": a 19-year-old with no legal experience; a "standard release — everyone signs"; a 60-day claim deadline; a $1,000 damages cap; and the company exempt from arbitrating its own claims. Lack of bilaterality (Higgins); the FAA allows generally applicable defenses.
  • Hypo 3, "The Payday Loan": $2,600 at 120% APR, the rate disclosure buried. She has paid $4,800 over 18 months and still owes $1,900. De La Torre: no cap does not bar review. Remedy: equitable relief without setting a rate. Compare Williams.
