Big picture

  • Two types: mutual mistake and unilateral mistake.
  • Mutual: both parties mistaken about a basic assumption; contract voidable by adversely affected party unless she bears the risk.
  • Unilateral: one party mistaken; contract voidable only if she doesn't bear the risk AND enforcement is unconscionable OR the other party had reason to know / caused the mistake.
  • Risk allocation (§ 154) is the pivot for both.
  • Lenawee: mutual mistake found, but rescission denied because buyers bore the risk ("in its present condition").
  • BMW Financial: unilateral mistake, but rescission denied — risk on mistaken party, no unconscionability.

Doctrine outline

Mutual mistake (Restatement rule, slide 2)

  • Mistake of both parties at formation.
  • As to a basic assumption on which the contract was made.
  • Material effect on the agreed exchange of performances.
  • Contract voidable by the adversely affected party UNLESS she bears the risk.

Unilateral mistake (Restatement rule, slide 9)

  • Mistake of one party at formation.
  • As to a basic assumption.
  • Material effect on agreed performances adverse to her.
  • Voidable by her unless she bears the risk AND either:
    • (a) enforcement would be unconscionable; OR
    • (b) other party had reason to know of the mistake, or other party's fault caused it.

Risk of mistake (§ 154, slide 4) — party bears risk when:

  • (a) risk allocated by agreement (e.g., "as is" clause); OR
  • (b) "conscious ignorance" — party aware he has only limited knowledge but treats it as sufficient; OR
  • (c) court allocates risk because reasonable in the circumstances.

§ 153(a)/(b) distinction (slide 11)

  • (a) unconscionability: result of enforcement must "shock the conscience" — high bar.
  • (b) palpable mistake: mistake should be obvious to both parties; other party cannot take advantage of a mistake it knows or should know about.
  • Good faith (§ 157): even absent risk-bearing, gross negligence or failure to meet good faith standards bars relief.

Mistake v. misrepresentation (slide 15)

  • Mistake = inadvertent error by the person committing it.
  • Misrepresentation = often willful/intentional, to gain wrongfully.
  • Overlap map:
    • Active misrepresentation to induce assent → misrepresentation (fraud in the inducement).
    • Failure to disclose known defect → possible duty of disclosure (Hill v. Jones).
    • Both parties simply wrong → mutual mistake (Lenawee).
    • Only one party wrong, other didn't know → unilateral mistake (BMW Financial).

Tests and elements

Mutual mistake checklist (slide 3)

  • Both parties, at contract formation
  • Make a mistake
  • About a basic assumption of the contract
  • Material effect on the agreed performances
  • Adversely affected party does NOT bear the risk of the mistake

Unilateral mistake checklist (slide 10)

  • One party, at contract formation
  • Makes a mistake
  • About a basic assumption of the contract
  • Material effect on agreed performances
  • Party does not bear the risk of mistake
  • PLUS either (5) enforcement unconscionable OR (6) other party had reason to know (palpable mistake) or caused the mistake

Risk-bearing checklist (§ 154)

  • (a) allocated by agreement ("as is" clause)?
  • (b) conscious ignorance — limited knowledge treated as sufficient?
  • (c) court allocation reasonable in the circumstances?

BMW Financial four-element test (from Donovan v. RRL Corp. / § 153) (slide 13)

  • (1) Mistake as to basic assumption
  • (2) Material adverse effect
  • (3) Risk of mistake — who bears it
  • (4) Unconscionable enforcement

Cases

Lenawee County Board of Health v. Messerly (p. 744)

  • Facts: buyers (Pickleses) bought apartment building; sellers' predecessor installed defective septic tank without permit, violating health code; tank too small, failed; county enjoined occupancy; neither party knew of defect at sale.
  • Holding: adopting Restatement §§ 152 and 154 — mutual mistake found, but rescission denied when the party seeking relief bears the risk.
  • Risk placed on purchasers because contract recited they accepted property "in its present condition."
  • Note: truly responsible party (predecessor Bloom) is neither buyer nor seller.

BMW Financial Services NA, LLC v. Deloach (p. 754)

  • Facts: Deloach defaulted on lease, tampered with odometer; default judgment $114,000 (incl. treble damages) for BMW Financial. Clerical error sent account to collection agency, which settled for $14,000 without knowing of the judgment. BMW sought rescission for unilateral mistake.
  • Holding: four-element test applied:
    • (1) basic assumption mistake: YES — didn't know judgment existed.
    • (2) material adverse effect: YES — $14,000 instead of $114,000.
    • (3) risk: BMW bears it — mistake entirely its own; Deloach and agency had no fault and no reason to know.
    • (4) unconscionable: NO — not losing out-of-pocket money, mostly treble damages (windfall).
  • Rescission denied.

Examples and hypos

Hypo 1 — "The Flooded Lot" (slide 8)

  • Byers contract to buy land for $100,000 to build residence; later discover it floods every other year, can't support construction.
  • Seller disclosed only one flooding incident in 10 years.
  • Byers' contractor assessed land as suitable for building.
  • No "as is" clause (Byers declined to check the box).
  • Byers seek rescission.
  • Questions: all mutual-mistake elements? Who bears risk under § 154 (all three alternatives)? Does absence of "as is" clause matter vs. Lenawee? Does contractor's assessment defeat the claim?

Hypo 2 — "The Settlement Trap" (slide 16)

  • Software company sues ex-employee for trade secret theft; preliminary injunction obtained.
  • Internal billing error marks litigation account "closed"; accounts payable authorizes mediator to settle.
  • Mediator settles for $30,000; actual exposure $400,000 in lost profits.
  • Company seeks rescission.
  • Questions: apply four unilateral-mistake elements; who bears risk — is own administrative error decisive? Does ex-employee's reliance matter? Compare to BMW Financial — key distinction?

Lenawee "as is" debate (Note 3, slide 7)

  • Classical: written "as is" clause decisive — you assumed the risk, full stop.
  • Modern: boilerplate "as is" is one factor in risk-of-loss analysis, not conclusive — especially if not actually negotiated.

Lenawee Note 8 reconciliation (slide 7)

  • Gartner and Lenawee apparently inconsistent.
  • Possible reconciliation: zoning restrictions are public record (buyer at fault in Gartner); hidden septic tank defect not easily discoverable (buyer less at fault in Lenawee).

Exam angles

  • Run the mutual-mistake checklist first; if all elements met, still ask whether the party bears risk under § 154 — that decides Lenawee.
  • For unilateral mistake, don't stop at risk: must also satisfy (5) unconscionability or (6) reason to know / causation.
  • Unconscionability is a high bar — "shocks the conscience." Losing a windfall (treble damages) is not enough (BMW Financial); losing real value far below market is (Donovan).
  • "As is" clause: classical = decisive; modern = one factor, especially if boilerplate/not negotiated. Absence of a clause matters (Hypo 1).
  • Conscious ignorance (§ 154(b)): limited knowledge treated as sufficient = you bear the risk.
  • Negligence alone doesn't bar relief; only GROSS negligence or bad faith does (Note 5).
  • Distinguish mistake from misrepresentation: inadvertent vs. willful; non-disclosure of known defect may trigger duty of disclosure (Hill v. Jones).
  • Compare/contrast Donovan and BMW Financial: both administrative errors; key distinctions are degree of fault and whether loss is windfall vs. actual value.
  • Watch for the "truly responsible party" point (Lenawee/Bloom): neither contracting party caused the defect.
