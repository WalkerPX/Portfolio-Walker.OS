Quick reference

Citation: 191 Cal. App. 3d 449, 237 Cal. Rptr. 584 (1987) · Casebook: Knapp p. 116 · Topic: Consideration One line: Opening an envelope was technically consideration for Time's "free calculator watch" promise, but the claim was dismissed because "the law disregards trifles."

Overview

A case about adequacy of consideration. Courts do not weigh the value exchanged, so even a trivial act (opening junk mail) can be consideration. The court nonetheless affirmed dismissal under the maxim de minimis non curat lex.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs / appellants: Mark Harris, Joshua Gnaizda (age 3, son of a prominent Bay Area public-interest lawyer) and Richard Baker, suing for a class. They wanted the watch, and the class action sought $15,000,000 in damages (slides).
  • Defendant / respondent: Time, Inc. The magazine publisher that sent the mailer, seeking subscriptions. It wanted dismissal.

Facts

  • Time sent bulk-rate mail. Through a see-through window, the envelope read: "JOSHUA A GNAIZDA, I'LL GIVE YOU THIS VERSATILE NEW CALCULATOR WATCH FREE Just for Opening this Envelope Before Feb. 15, 1985."
  • Hidden below the window: "AND MAILING THIS CERTIFICATE TODAY!" Inside, the watch came only with a magazine subscription.
  • Joshua's mother opened the envelope and realized it was a ploy.

Procedural History

  • The trial court sustained Time's demurrer on the breach of contract count and granted summary judgment on the unfair-advertising counts.
  • Affirmed by the Court of Appeal (on a different rationale for the contract count).

Issue

Did Time's envelope create an enforceable unilateral contract, accepted by opening the envelope, and if so, should the court enforce it?

Holding

Technically yes, a contract was formed: the outside of the envelope was an offer, and opening it was acceptance by performance and sufficient consideration. But the action was properly dismissed under de minimis non curat lex.

Reasoning

  • Offer: the envelope promised the watch for an act (opening it). Read alone, that is an offer for a unilateral contract.
  • Consideration: the act was bargained for, since Time wanted people to open the envelope and read its pitch. The value or inadequacy of the act is irrelevant.
  • De minimis: the plaintiffs suffered no real damage beyond "the few seconds it takes to open an envelope." Courts "are too heavily overburdened to be used as a vehicle to punish" a trifling technical wrong. Remedies for deceptive junk mail belong to the legislature or Congress.
  • A judgment correct on any theory will be affirmed.

Rule

Courts generally will not inquire into the adequacy of consideration. Any bargained-for act, however trivial, suffices. But courts may decline to adjudicate trivial claims under de minimis non curat lex.

Why It Matters

  • Core takeaway (slides): courts generally will not weigh relative values or police bargains for fairness. A genuine bargain is enough, even where the exchange looks trivial.
  • It shows how advertising can create a unilateral contract (compare Sateriale v. RJ Reynolds).

Other Key Points

  • Note 1: inadequacy alone does not make an agreement unenforceable.
  • Note 2: Batsakis v. Demotsis, the traditional "gross inadequacy" case. Distinguish it from Harris: in Batsakis the inadequacy was large but the bargain was real and serious. In Harris the bargain was real but trivial.
  • Note 3: extreme imbalance can invalidate a contract, usually only with other red flags (fraud, duress, incapacity, undue influence). See Unconscionability.
  • Note 4: the de minimis principle.
  • Priya scratch-off hypo (slides): odds of 1 in 50,000 disclosed in fine print. Consideration? De minimis? Deception?
