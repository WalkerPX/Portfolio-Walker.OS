Big picture

  • One theme: legal problems do not fit neatly into one doctrine or one area of law.
  • Many problems require thinking in terms of several causes of action — contractual, tort, property.
  • This section contrasts the contract and tort consequences of misrepresentation.
  • Misrepresentation = assertion of fact that is not true.
  • Two categories: fraudulent (intentional lie) and nonfraudulent (person believes it is true but it is not).
  • Fraudulent splits into fraud in the execution (contract VOID) and fraud in the inducement (contract VOIDABLE).
  • Nondisclosure is a separate route: duty to disclose + rescission.

Doctrine outline

Fraudulent misrepresentation

  • Fraud in the Execution (Factum): party tricks another into signing a contract they believe is something else. Contract is VOID.
  • Fraud in the Inducement: misrepresentation made to induce assent; adversely affected party justified in relying. Contract is VOIDABLE.

Nonfraudulent misrepresentation

  • Contract voidable by the adversely affected party if (1) material misrepresentation of fact and (2) justifiable reliance.
  • Material misrepresentation of fact = a promise, false statement, or omission of facts that would cause another party to act differently if the whole truth were known.

Nondisclosure

  • Duty to disclose a defect or harmful condition arises when:
    • the defect/condition is unknown to the other party; AND
    • it is of a kind the other party would be unlikely to discover on its own or ask about.
  • Uninformed party may seek rescission if non-disclosed facts:
    • have a material effect on the transaction;
    • are not readily observable;
    • are not otherwise known to the uninformed party; AND
    • one of four circumstances applies: (1) disclosure required by statute; (2) intentional concealment; (3) relationship of trust and confidence; or (4) to prevent or correct a mistake.
  • Modern view (Hill): duty of disclosure of material information imposed in several situations under Restatement (Second) § 161. Broadly: nondisclosure actionable if it amounts to a failure to act in good faith.
  • Classical view: no duty to disclose in a business transaction (compare Laidlaw v. Organ — buyer knew war was over, seller did not).

Tests and elements

CHECKLIST — 3 Elements of Fraud in the Inducement

  1. Fraudulent misrepresentation of fact:
    • (a) made knowingly or recklessly (willful/conscious ignorance of truth); AND
    • (b) with the intent to induce assent.
  2. Induced the adversely affected party to manifest assent; AND
  3. The adversely affected party was justified in relying on the misrepresentation.

CHECKLIST — Nonfraudulent misrepresentation (voidability)

  1. Material misrepresentation of fact.
  2. Justifiable reliance.

CHECKLIST — Duty to disclose (defect/harmful condition)

  1. Unknown to the other party; AND
  2. Of a kind the other party would be unlikely to discover on its own or ask about.

CHECKLIST — Rescission for nondisclosure

  1. Material effect on the transaction;
  2. Not readily observable;
  3. Not otherwise known to the uninformed party; AND
  4. One of four circumstances:
    • (1) disclosure required by statute;
    • (2) intentional concealment;
    • (3) relationship of trust and confidence;
    • (4) to prevent or correct a mistake.

CHECKLIST — Keeton factors (equity of imposing a duty to disclose)

  • Knowledge of the fact by the withholding party.
  • Awareness of the significance of the fact.
  • Reliance.
  • Relationship of parties.

NOTE — Statements of opinion

  • Modern law has substantially eroded the rule that statements of opinion are not actionable.
  • May be actionable when: the speaker knows the statement is false; there is a close relationship; or the opinion implies underlying facts.

Cases

  • Syester v. Banta (p. 634) — Basically a tort action; plaintiff seeks actual and punitive damages for misrepresentation in connection with contracts for dancing instruction. To recover in tort, plaintiff must first rescind certain releases she signed — contract law becomes relevant. P (Agnes Syester), a lonely and elderly widow, sued Arthur Murray Dance Studio alleging fraud and misrepresentation for nearly $30,000 worth of dance instruction; sought damages and rescission of a prior release dismissing an earlier lawsuit. Jury could find "such a concerted effort, lacking in propriety, to obtain the releases as to constitute fraudulent overreaching" — "a predatory play on the vanity and credulity of an old lady." Open questions flagged: Did Mr. Carey make misrepresentations (said she could be a professional dancer; said she did not need an attorney)? Could she reasonably rely, having already sued alleging she had been lied to? Note: undue influence (Odorizzi framework) may be the best doctrinal explanation for the result.
  • Hill v. Jones (p. 644) — When is nondisclosure (vs. actual misrepresentation) actionable, in rescission or tort damages? Hills agreed to buy Defendants' home; sought rescission after learning of a prior termite infestation not disclosed. Result: duty to disclose applies — sellers had an obligation to disclose the prior termite infestation.
  • Park 100 Investors, Inc. v. Kartes (p. 657) — Raises fraud in the execution. Plaintiff seeks to enforce a personal guarantee against principals of a corporation that leased property. Defendants (Mr. and Mrs. Kartes) prevailed below: they signed a personal guarantee not knowing its true nature, believing it was simply "lease papers." Plaintiff's agent actively misrepresented the paper as "lease papers"; document was headed "Lease Agreement." Mr. Kartes called his attorney to confirm the "lease papers" had been approved. Even if one cannot ordinarily avoid a signed agreement by claiming ignorance of its terms, active misrepresentation may override that principle. Whether one can rely on the other party's characterization of the writing is a question of fact; here defendants acted with ordinary care.

Examples and hypos

Hypo 4 — "Just Sign the Lease"

  • Facts: commercial landlord's leasing agent tells Tenant's CEO "We just need your signature on the lease to get you started." Document is actually a personal guarantee; top says "Commercial Lease Agreement." CEO skims first page, asks "Is this the lease?" Agent says "Yes." CEO signs. Company defaults; landlord seeks to enforce the personal guarantee against the CEO personally.
  • Discussion questions:
    • Apply Park 100. Is the CEO personally liable?
    • What is the significance of the document being labeled "Commercial Lease Agreement"?
    • The CEO is a sophisticated businessperson. Does that change the analysis?
    • How would you advise the landlord's agent to structure this transaction differently?

Syester as illustration

  • Statements: she could be a professional dancer; she did not need an attorney.
  • Reliance question: she had already sued alleging she was lied to — how could she claim she "believed and relied upon" statements made to induce her to drop that suit?
  • Note 4: was her reliance reasonable, or was she bargaining for companionship the lessons provided?

Exam angles

  • Classify the misrepresentation first: fraudulent (execution vs. inducement) or nonfraudulent. Remedy differs: VOID vs. VOIDABLE.
  • Run the 3 elements of fraud in the inducement as a checklist; justify reliance is the usual battleground.
  • For nondisclosure, run the duty checklist, then the four circumstances; only one circumstance is needed.
  • Watch the contract/tort overlap: Syester must rescind the release before recovering in tort.
  • Active misrepresentation by the other party can override the rule that you cannot avoid a signed agreement by claiming ignorance of its terms (Park 100).
  • Reliance on the other party's characterization of a writing is a question of fact; ordinary care matters (Park 100).
  • Statements of opinion are not automatically off the table — check knowledge of falsity, close relationship, implied underlying facts.
  • Consider undue influence as an alternative doctrinal frame (Syester note).
