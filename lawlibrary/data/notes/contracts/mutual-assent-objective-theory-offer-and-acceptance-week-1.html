Big picture

  • Traditional contract formation = "a bargain in which there is a manifestation of mutual assent to the exchange and a consideration."
  • Mutual assent is the traditional and probably most important way a contract forms — but not the only way.
  • Two basic requirements for an enforceable contract: mutual assent and consideration.
  • Mutual assent is OBJECTIVE, not subjective: parties need not actually agree in their own minds.
  • Each party must act so as to lead the other to reasonably believe agreement has been reached.
  • Most contracts are bilateral — an exchange of promises.

Doctrine outline

  • Objective Theory of Contracts: a party's intent is deemed to be what a reasonable person in the other party's position would think the first party's objective manifestation of intent meant.
    • If a reasonable person in the offeree's position would believe the offeror was serious, there is an enforceable offer — even if the offeror was subjectively joking.
    • If a reasonable person in the offeree's position would have understood it was a joke (bantering tone, wildly below market value), no contract.
    • If the offeror proves the offeree actually knew it was a joke, no mutual assent — even if it would not have been unreasonable for the offeree to think it serious.
    • Offeree is charged with both actual knowledge and what he reasonably should have known.
  • Bilateral contracts: exchange of promises; each party promises to do something for the other.
  • Offer (Restatement): "the manifestation of willingness to enter into a bargain, so made as to justify another person in understanding that his assent to that bargain is invited and will conclude the bargain."
    • Look for: material terms, specificity, unequivocal language.
    • Restatement (Second) § 26: an offer requires a communication expressing an intent to be bound without further assent from the offeror.
  • Advertisements: general rule — ads are not normally treated as offers, but can be depending on their language.
    • "Bait and switch" tactics (Izadi v. Machado (Gus) Ford, Inc.) may support liability under unfair trade practices statutes.
  • Offers made in jest: an offer the offeree knows or should know is made in jest is not valid; purported "acceptance" creates no contract.
  • Classical offer-and-acceptance rules (Normile):
    • Buyer can't accept a counteroffer after receiving notice of its revocation.
    • An offer must be accepted entirely — a conditional acceptance is a counteroffer.
  • Policy (Lonergan Note 1): better to require more formality and clarity before treating a communication as an offer — otherwise parties must exercise undue caution in preliminary talks.

Tests and elements

  • Objective theory test: Would a reasonable person in the other party's position think the first party's objective manifestation of intent meant serious or joking?
  • Enforceable contract — two requirements: (1) mutual assent; (2) consideration.
  • Offer — Restatement elements: manifestation of willingness to enter a bargain; justifies offeree in understanding assent is invited and will conclude the bargain; intent to be bound without further assent (§ 26).
  • Offer — look-for checklist: material terms; specificity; unequivocal language.
  • Jest checklist: offeree knew or should have known it was a joke → no valid offer → no contract.
  • Advertisement checklist: general rule = not an offer; exception depends on the ad's language; bait-and-switch may trigger unfair trade practices liability.
  • Normile checklist: counteroffer revocable; notice of revocation kills power to accept; conditional acceptance = counteroffer, not acceptance.

Experienced contractor; not reasonable to assume its own specifications governed instead of the signed contract. Unilateral mistake, not mutual; D didn't show diligence in reviewing. Examples and hypos

  • Slide 33 example: A says to B, "I'll buy your contracts casebook for $3,000." Would a reasonable person in B's position believe A was serious or joking? If serious, enforceable offer even if A subjectively joking.
  • Slide 39–40 hypo: A offers to sell casebook for $100; B accepts; book worth $500; A refuses. If B shows A's tone or known lack of business acumen led B to reasonably believe A was serious → contract. If reasonable person would have understood A was joking (bantering tone, wildly below market value) → no contract. If A proves B actually knew A was joking → no mutual assent.
  • Problem 1-2 (Carnival Cruise):
    • Two tangled disputes: substantively, CCL's tort liability; procedurally/contractually, whether litigation is in Washington or Florida.
    • Forum-selection clause could decide the procedural question outright — and that could decide whether the tort claim is ever heard (can the Shutes afford to litigate in Florida?).
    • Note the overlap between contracts, civil procedure, and torts.
    • Shutes already took the cruise — arguing "no contract at all" would be self-serving and implausible. Real argument: the forum-selection clause specifically shouldn't be enforceable.
    • Shutes' facts: T&C's location on back of ticket, small font, clause buried at ¶8 of 25.
    • CCL's response: Shutes did receive tickets; ticket face expressly instructed reader to read the contract on the back; instruction was in bold, all-caps type.
    • Should there be a contract here? Efficiency theorist: clauses cut litigation costs, promote predictability; savings flow through to lower prices. Relational scholar: no ongoing relationship, so relationship-preserving norms carry less weight. Critical scholar: legal rules aren't neutral — powerful repeat players (banks, insurers, large sellers) use forum-selection and arbitration clauses to keep less powerful counterparties out of court.
    • Practical advice — clearer assent: bigger font for T&C; plain, easy-to-read language; place to initial next to specific term(s) or at bottom of each page; separate signature line affirming signer read and understood terms — possibly repeated at multiple points.
  • Slide 48 hypo (Maya/app): free photo editing app; dialogue box links to 47-page ToS; checkbox "I agree to the Terms and Conditions"; Maya clicks without reading; six months later company sells her usage data. Q: Is there a contract? Does it matter she didn't read? Compare to Shutes. Fact twist: checkbox pre-checked, must uncheck to decline — does that change analysis?
  • Slide 49 hypo (Professor Walsh): after bad faculty meeting, tells RA "I would give anything, literally anything, to get out of teaching next semester." RA: "Done. I'll cover all your classes for $500." Walsh laughs, says she was venting. Q: Apply objective theory — would reasonable person in RA's position think serious offer? Facts cutting toward/against?   • Slide 50 hypo (Marcus/Barbri): posts in group chat with 15 classmates: "Selling my old Barbri set. $200 takes it." Two respond — "I'll take it" and "Sold, I'll Venmo you now." Marcus responds to second and completes sale; first is upset. Q: Offer or invitation to receive offers? Does 15 recipients matter? Compare to Lonergan form letter. What if sent to only one classmate?

Wednesday, Tenant hears from friend Landlord already rented to someone else. Thursday morning, Tenant texts: "Fine, $1,600, I'll take it." Exam angles

  • Apply objective theory to any "joke"/"venting"/"I'd give anything" fact pattern: reasonable-person-in-offeree's-position test; then actual-knowledge override.
  • Distinguish offer vs. invitation to receive offers: form letters/mass communications (Lonergan), ads (general rule), group chats (Marcus hypo).
  • Check for material terms, specificity, unequivocal language.
  • Jest: known or should-have-known → no valid offer → no contract (Leonard).
  • Bait-and-switch ads → unfair trade practices liability (Izadi).
  • Counteroffer sequence: conditional acceptance = counteroffer; counteroffer revocable; notice of revocation (formal or informal — see Normile) kills power to accept.
  • Unilateral vs. mutual mistake: Ray — unilateral mistake + no diligence = bound.
  • Policy arguments (efficiency / relational / critical) for boilerplate assent terms — Carnival framework.
  • Practical fixes for assent clarity (font, plain language, initials, signature lines) — Carnival slide 30.
