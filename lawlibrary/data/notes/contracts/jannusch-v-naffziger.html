Quick reference

Citation: 379 Ill. App. 3d 381, 318 Ill. Dec. 480, 883 N.E.2d 711 (2008) · Casebook: Knapp p. 135 · Topic: UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation One line: The oral sale of a food-truck business for $150,000 was mainly a sale of goods, and the buyers' conduct showed a contract under UCC § 2-204 even though some details were left open.

Overview

A course case on two UCC threshold questions: (1) scope, where the predominant purpose test applies to a mixed sale of goods and intangibles; (2) formation, where § 2-204's liberal rules let a contract be formed by conduct, without pinpointing the moment of agreement and despite open terms.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs / appellants: Gene and Martha Jannusch. Owners of Festival Foods, a concession business that worked festivals in Illinois and Indiana. They wanted the $140,000 balance of the price.
  • Defendants / appellees: Lindsey Naffziger and her mother, Louann Naffziger. Buyers who ran the business for a season, then returned it. They argued no contract was formed.

Facts

  • The business's assets included a truck, a servicing trailer and equipment (refrigerators, freezers, roasters, signs and so on), plus the opportunity to work events Gene had secured.
  • August 13, 2005 (Gene's testimony): an oral agreement to sell for $150,000. The buyers paid $10,000 immediately, with the balance due when their bank loan came through.
  • The next day the buyers took possession and ran the business for the rest of the 2005 season. They replaced equipment, paid employees, arranged financing, and asked a lawyer to draft the contract (Note 2, slides). Insurance and titles stayed in Gene's name pending payment.
  • Two days after the season ended, the buyers returned Festival Foods to storage, partly because income was lower than expected. Gene could not resell it.

Procedural History

  • Bench trial. The trial court held the UCC governed (rejecting the buyers' argument that no sale of goods was involved), and found a contract was formed but that there was insufficient evidence of a meeting of the minds on its terms: "If this is an agreement to reach an agreement, ... the action for the price must fail." Judgment for defendants.
  • Appellate Court: reversed and remanded with directions.

Issue

  1. Does Article 2 govern the sale of a business consisting of goods plus intangibles (goodwill, event opportunities)?
  2. Did the parties form an enforceable contract under § 2-204 despite unresolved details?

Holding

  1. Yes. The predominant purpose of the transaction was the sale of goods (the truck, trailer and equipment), so Article 2 applies.
  2. Yes. There was an agreement to sell Festival Foods for $150,000, and the buyers breached it. Returning the goods was not a rejection of an offer; it was a breach.

Reasoning

  • Predominant purpose test: in a mixed contract, ask whether the predominant thrust is the sale of goods or something else. The tangible assets were the core of the bargain.
  • § 2-204(1): a contract may be made in any manner sufficient to show agreement, including conduct recognizing a contract.
  • § 2-204(2): an agreement may be found "even though the moment of its making is undetermined." Louann admitted an agreement to buy for $150,000 but could not recall a specific date. That does not matter.
  • § 2-204(3): a contract does not fail for indefiniteness if the parties intended to contract and there is a reasonably certain basis for a remedy. The essential terms (price, assets) were agreed. Other terms "could have been fleshed out."
  • The buyers' conduct was unequivocal: possession, operation, payment and financing.

Rule

  • Scope: for mixed goods and non-goods transactions, Article 2 applies if the predominant purpose is the sale of goods.
  • Formation (§ 2-204): a sale-of-goods contract can be formed by conduct, without identifying when it was made, and despite open terms, if the parties intended to contract and a court can fashion a remedy.

Why It Matters

  • It shows the UCC's relaxed formation rules compared with classical offer and acceptance (Walker v. Keith).
  • It contrasts with EC Styberg Engineering v. Eaton, where sophisticated parties' past practice showed no agreement.
  • Note 3: a sale of goods of $500 or more generally needs a writing under § 2-201. This is covered later in Statute of Frauds. Here, part payment and receipt and acceptance of the goods would likely satisfy the § 2-201(3)(c) exception.

Other Key Points

  • The trial court's "agreement to agree" framing is exactly what § 2-204(3) is designed to reject when intent to contract is clear.
  • Exam checklist: (1) Is it goods (§ 2-105)? (2) Mixed? Apply predominant purpose. (3) Formed under § 2-204? (4) Statute of frauds under § 2-201?
