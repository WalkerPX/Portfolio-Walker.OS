The short version

The law generally enforces a promise only if it was part of a trade: each side gives or promises something in exchange for what the other side gives. A promise to give a gift usually isn't enforceable.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Consideration.

What it's about

Consideration is the "what's in it for each side" requirement. A promise is enforceable as a contract when it's part of a bargained-for exchange: I promise X because you give or promise Y, and you give Y because of my promise.

It doesn't have to be money. Doing something you didn't have to do, or not doing something you had a right to do, counts.

What does NOT count

  • Gift promises. "I'll give you $1,000 for your birthday" is a promise with nothing asked in return.
  • Past favors. Something already done before the promise wasn't given in exchange for it.
  • Empty promises. "I'll buy as many as I feel like" commits you to nothing, so it's "illusory."
  • Pretend exchanges. Writing "for value received" on a gift doesn't turn it into a trade.

Courts don't judge whether it was a good deal

If there was a real exchange, courts generally won't ask whether it was fair or equal. Overpaying is your own business. (Truly shocking unfairness is handled by other doctrines such as unconscionability.)

An everyday example

Your aunt says, "If you don't drink until you're 21, I'll pay you $5,000." You stay sober. That's consideration: you gave up something you had a legal right to do, because she asked. If she had just said "I'll give you $5,000 when you turn 21," that's a gift promise.

The cases, in plain words

  • Hamer v. Sidway: the nephew gave up drinking, smoking and gambling until 21 for his uncle's promise. Giving up a legal right is consideration.
  • Dougherty v. Salt: an aunt's note to her young nephew, written out of affection, was a gift promise. "Value received" on the paper didn't change that.
  • Batsakis v. Demotsis: someone borrowed wartime money worth about $25 and promised to repay $2,000. Courts don't weigh adequacy, so the promise was enforced.
  • Pennsy Supply v. American Ash Recycling: "free" material wasn't a gift, because taking it saved the supplier disposal costs. That was a bargain.
  • Harris v. Time: opening an envelope was technically consideration, but the claim was too trivial to bother with.
  • Plowman v. Indian Refining and Mills v. Wyman: promises to pay for past service or out of moral duty lacked consideration.
  • Kirksey v. Kirksey: an offer of a home to a widowed sister-in-law was a gift. Today a court might enforce it through reliance.
  • Wood v. Lucy Lady Duff-Gordon and Marshall Durbin Food v. Baker: a promise that looks empty can be saved by reading in a duty of reasonable effort, or by recognizing a real condition.

Words you'll hear

  • Bargained-for exchange: each side's promise or act is the price of the other's.
  • Illusory promise: a promise that doesn't actually commit to anything.
  • Past consideration: something done before the promise, which doesn't count.
