Quick reference

Citation: 895 A.2d 595 (Pa. Super. Ct. 2006) · Casebook: Knapp p. 93 · Topic: Consideration One line: "Free" AggRite was not a gift. Taking it off the supplier's hands saved the supplier disposal costs, so the exchange was a bargain supported by consideration.

Overview

The course's main bargained-for exchange case. It distinguishes a conditional gift from a bargain. The question is whether the promisor sought the promisee's detriment as the "price" of its promise, the Holmesian relation of "reciprocal conventional inducement."

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Pennsy Supply, Inc. The paving subcontractor that took and used about 11,000 tons of AggRite, then had to tear it out. It wanted to recover its costs (over $385,000) for breach of contract, breach of UCC warranties and promissory estoppel.
  • Defendant / appellee: American Ash Recycling Corp. of Pennsylvania. The supplier of AggRite (treated ash aggregate). It argued the material was a free gift, so there was no contract.
  • Others: Northern York County School District (owner); Lobar, Inc. (general contractor).

Facts

  • The school project's specifications allowed substituting AggRite for base aggregate. A "notice to bidders" said AggRite was available at no cost from American Ash, and an American Ash letter confirmed free AggRite on a "first come, first served" basis.
  • Pennsy told American Ash it needed about 11,000 tons, picked it up, and used it for paving (finished December 2001).
  • The pavement developed extensive cracking (February 2002). Pennsy had to remove and dispose of the AggRite, which was classified as hazardous waste, at a cost of over $385,000. American Ash refused to pay.
  • Pennsy alleged that American Ash offered the material free because it would otherwise have to pay to dispose of it.

Procedural History

  • The trial court sustained American Ash's preliminary objections (demurrer) and dismissed the complaint, finding no consideration (a conditional gift).
  • Reversed and remanded by the Superior Court, which also reinstated the UCC warranty and promissory estoppel counts.

Issue

Does a supplier's offer of "free" material, accepted by a contractor who takes it away, involve consideration, or is it merely a conditional gift?

Holding

Consideration was adequately alleged. Pennsy's taking of the AggRite was a bargained-for detriment that conferred a benefit on American Ash (avoiding disposal costs). The complaint stated a claim for breach of contract.

Reasoning

  • Restatement (Second) § 71: a performance or return promise is "bargained for" if the promisor seeks it in exchange for its promise and the promisee gives it in exchange for that promise.
  • Conditional gift vs. bargain: taking the material to get the gift is a condition, not consideration, unless the promisor wanted the taking. The classic illustration is Williston's tramp: "If you go around the corner to the clothing shop, you may purchase an overcoat on my credit." Walking around the corner is a condition of the gift, not the price of the promise.
  • Here, the complaint alleged American Ash actively promoted AggRite to avoid the substantial cost of disposal. Its promise induced Pennsy's detriment and the detriment induced the promise: quid pro quo.
  • Holmes: the promise and consideration must stand in "the relation of reciprocal conventional inducement, each for the other."

Rule

A promise is supported by consideration when the promisee's performance (even taking something "free") is sought by the promisor in exchange for its promise and given in exchange for it. If the promisor would benefit from the performance and offered the promise to induce it, the exchange is a bargain, not a conditional gift.

Why It Matters

  • It is the leading illustration of the modern bargain theory and the gift vs. bargain distinction.
  • Practical point: free giveaways that relieve the giver of a burden may create contracts, with warranty exposure under UCC Article 2.
  • Exam question to ask: does the condition benefit the promisor? If so, it is likely bargained for (Pennsy). If not, it is likely a gift (the tramp, Plowman's check pickup).

Other Key Points

  • IU fill dirt hypo (slides): IU would pay $400,000 to remove soil and posts "FREE CLEAN FILL DIRT." ABC takes 2,000 tons, which turn out contaminated, causing $150,000 in cleanup. IU says there was no contract. Under Pennsy, IU benefited (avoided removal), so there is likely consideration.
  • Five-part hypo (slides), Dwight and the beet harvester: "come pick it up" alone is probably a gift condition. "Haul this away by Friday" when Dwight would pay $1,000 to dispose of it is probably a bargain.
