Overview

A modern case on substantive unconscionability of price terms (interest rates). It holds that a legislative decision not to cap rates does not immunize the rates from the unconscionability doctrine.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs: Eduardo De La Torre and a class of borrowers. They took CashCall loans of $2,500 or more at 96-135% APR and sought relief under California's Unfair Competition Law, predicated on unconscionability.
  • Defendant: CashCall, Inc. A consumer lender. It argued the Legislature's choice not to cap rates on loans of $2,500 or more meant those rates could not be unconscionable.

Facts

  • California's Finance Code set maximum rates for consumer loans under $2,500, but no cap for loans of $2,500 or more.
  • CashCall made loans of $2,500 or more at 96% to 135% APR, targeting the uncapped category (Note 1).

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] In federal class litigation, the Ninth Circuit certified a question to the California Supreme Court: can the interest rate on loans of $2,500 or more be unconscionable under the Finance Code? The California Supreme Court answered yes.

Issue

Can an interest rate on a consumer loan of $2,500 or more be found unconscionable, despite the absence of a statutory rate cap for such loans?

Holding

Yes. An interest rate on such a loan may be deemed unconscionable under California law.

Reasoning

  • Statutory argument rejected: a separate Finance Code provision expressly subjects consumer loans to unconscionability review without limitation. Removing rate caps is not the same as declaring that no rate can be unconscionable.
  • "Rate-setting" argument rejected: CashCall said applying unconscionability would make courts set rates, which they lack the expertise to do. But trial courts have wide equitable discretion to fashion remedies without setting rates (e.g., refusing to enforce or limiting the term).
  • Standard: unconscionability requires oppression or surprise (procedural) plus overly harsh or one-sided results (substantive), on a sliding scale.
  • Relevant factors for price terms: the lender's costs and risks, the salience of the term to consumers, the borrower's creditworthiness, and the existence of market imperfections.

Rule

A price or interest term may be substantively unconscionable even where a statute does not cap it. Courts assess it in context, considering costs, salience, the borrower's risk profile and market failures, together with procedural unconscionability.

Why It Matters

  • It shows unconscionability can reach price (the core economic term), not just ancillary terms.
  • Note 1: payday lending regulation. Lenders restructure loans to avoid regulation, as CashCall did by targeting the $2,500+ category. (The CFPB rulemaking history is on the slides.)
  • Note 4: most courts limit unconscionability to a defense (a shield). A few allow affirmative recovery (a sword), notably In re Checking Account Overdraft Litigation.
