Citation: 35 F.2d 301 (4th Cir. 1929) (Parker, J.) · Casebook: Knapp p. 942 · Topic: Limits on Damages › Mitigation (avoidable consequences) One line: After the county repudiated a bridge contract, the builder could not keep building and charge for everything. It could recover only its costs up to the repudiation plus its expected profit.

Overview

The classic avoidable consequences (mitigation) case. A party may not recover damages it could have avoided by reasonable effort after learning of the breach (Restatement (Second) § 350(1)).

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Luten Bridge Co. A contractor building a bridge over the Dan River. It wanted the full contract price for work performed after the repudiation.
  • Defendant / appellant: Rockingham County, North Carolina. It repudiated (cancelled) the contract shortly after work began and argued it owed only damages as of the repudiation.

Facts

  • The county contracted with Luten to build a bridge over the Dan River.
  • Shortly after work began, amid political controversy over the bridge, the county board repudiated and notified Luten to stop. [Outside knowledge: a newly constituted board majority opposed the project, and there was a dispute over whether the board's action was valid.]
  • Luten continued building anyway, completed the bridge (a "bridge to nowhere," since no connecting road was built), and sued for the entire bill (about $18,000).

Procedural History

  • The trial court instructed in Luten's favor and entered judgment for the full amount. [Outside knowledge: about $18,301.07.]
  • Fourth Circuit: reversed and remanded for a new trial.

Issue

After a party repudiates a construction contract, may the contractor continue performing and recover the full contract price for work done after the repudiation?

Holding

No. Upon notice of repudiation, the contractor must stop and not pile up damages. It may recover its costs incurred up to the repudiation plus its anticipated profit (slides: about $1,900), not the cost of work done afterward.

Reasoning

  • A repudiation, though wrongful, cannot be ignored. The non-breaching party has a duty (really a limitation on recovery) to avoid increasing the damages.
  • "It is likewise true that [the contractor] ... was not justified in thus increasing the damages" by building a useless bridge after notice.
  • Expectation measure: Luten gets the benefit of its bargain (its profit) plus reimbursement of reliance costs to the breach. That puts it where full performance would have, without waste.

Rule

A party may not recover damages for loss that it could have avoided without undue risk, burden or humiliation after it knew or should have known of the breach (Restatement (Second) § 350(1)). After repudiation, a contractor should stop work and recover lost profit plus costs incurred.

Why It Matters

  • Justifications for mitigation (slides): it avoids economic waste, and the non-breaching party is best positioned to avoid further loss.
  • It sets up the employment version in Maness v. Collins.
  • "Duty to mitigate" is shorthand. There is no liability for failing to mitigate; it only limits the recovery.
  • Costs of reasonable mitigation efforts are recoverable even if they fail. [Outside knowledge: Restatement § 350(2).]
