Big picture

  • Three related excuse doctrines. If a party is "discharged," it is NOT liable for breach.
  • Impossibility: unexpected events make performance literally impossible.
  • Impracticability: unexpected events make performance highly impractical.
  • Frustration of purpose: unexpected events completely (or almost completely) destroy a party's purpose in contracting.
  • All three: courts are reluctant to grant relief. The risk of changed circumstances is usually allocated to the party seeking excuse.
  • Bottom line from the deck: the doctrine exists in theory but rarely succeeds in practice.

Doctrine outline

Impossibility

  • Performance made literally impossible by unexpected events → contract may be discharged.
  • Examples: destruction of subject matter, death of an essential party, supervening illegality.

Impracticability

  • Performance theoretically possible but commercially senseless — cost so extreme it defeats the basic assumption.
  • Change in profitability or cost, even if extreme, is generally NOT sufficient (NOTE, slide 4).

Frustration of purpose

  • Performance still possible, may even be cheap — but the reason for making the contract has been destroyed.
  • Key distinction from impracticability: frustration affects the VALUE of what is received, not the COST or POSSIBILITY of performance.

Foreseeability (Hemlock Note 3)

  • Foreseeability does NOT necessarily bar relief — otherwise excuse would almost never be available.
  • Restatement (Second) § 261, Comment c: mere possibility a party might have contracted against a contingency is not enough to assign the risk.
  • Practical difficulty of negotiating myriad conceivable terms may excuse failure to deal with improbable contingencies.
  • But: harder to foresee → stronger excuse argument; more foreseeable → harder to show non-occurrence was a basic assumption.

Governmental action vs. market change (Mel Frank Note 1)

  • Governmental action claims tend to fare better than market-change claims — but frustration must still be substantial.
  • Court asks: was non-occurrence of the governmental action a shared basic assumption? Was it foreseeable enough that claimant assumed the risk?

Force majeure (Mel Frank Note 2; slide 15)

  • A force majeure clause will often strengthen the excuse claim.
  • Negotiate a force majeure clause.

Tests and elements

Impracticability — Restatement § 261 / UCC § 2-615 (checklist)

  • (1) Performance has become impracticable.
  • (2) Because of the occurrence of a contingency.
  • (3) Whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption on which the contract was made.
  • (4) Without the fault of the party seeking discharge.
  • AND: party did not assume the risk of the contingency occurring.
  • NOTE: change in profitability or cost, even if extreme, generally NOT sufficient.

Frustration of purpose — Restatement § 265 (checklist)

  • (1) Party's principal purpose is substantially frustrated.
  • (2) By the occurrence of a contingency.
  • (3) Whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption on which the contract was made.
  • (4) Without the fault of the party seeking discharge.
  • AND: party did not assume the risk.

Mel Frank substantiality standard

  • "The contract must be rendered virtually worthless to warrant relief based on the frustration doctrine."
  • Requires near-total frustration, not merely a reduction in value or usefulness.

Cases

  • Taylor v. Caldwell (1863) — music hall burned down before concert series; neither party at fault; performance literally impossible. Classic impossibility.
  • Krell v. Henry (1903) — coronation viewing room; procession cancelled due to illness; purpose frustrated even though paying rent was still possible.
  • Mineral Park Land Co. v. Howard (1916) — excavation; material existed but below water and prohibitively expensive to remove; impracticability though not literally impossible.
  • Hemlock Semiconductor Operations, LLC v. Solarworld Industries Sachsen GmbH (p. 771) — 14-year fixed-price contracts; "take or pay" clause; China subsidies flooded market, price dropped below contract price; Sachsen refused to purchase; Hemlock sued on liquidated damages clause.
    • Impracticability rejected: continuation of market conditions is NOT a basic assumption of a long-term fixed-price contract; fixing prices allocates risk of market fluctuations. Sachsen could have negotiated excusing terms; it did not, so it accepted the risk.
    • Frustration rejected: primary purpose was supply of goods at a fixed price; not frustrated — Hemlock stood ready to deliver.
  • Mel Frank Tool & Supply, Inc. v. Di-Chem Co. (p. 781) — tenant leased warehouse to store hazardous chemicals; post-signing city ordinance prohibited such storage; frustration claim FAILS.
    • Di-Chem still had "serviceable use" — could store non-hazardous materials and conduct other business.
    • "The contract must be rendered virtually worthless to warrant relief based on the frustration doctrine."
    • Compare: if Di-Chem had only one possible use and that exact use was prohibited, result might differ.
    • Di-Chem had a force majeure clause; court correctly held it did not apply to the actual facts.
  • Lloyd v. Murphy — wartime prohibitions on new car sales frustrated a car dealer's lease, but tenant could still use property for other commercial purposes; claim failed.

Examples and hypos

Hypo 1 — "The Solar Contract"

  • GreenPower (US) → SolarTech (Germany), 10-year fixed price $80/kg. Year 2: US breakthrough cuts industry costs to $15/kg. GreenPower produces at $75/kg (older equipment), losing money, wants excuse. SolarTech refuses to renegotiate.
  • Questions: apply impracticability elements — strongest argument for GreenPower? Does US technological breakthrough (vs. foreign government action) matter? No force majeure clause — effect? What if costs rose to $200/kg due to US regulation banning the primary input chemical?

Hypo 2 — "The Flower Shop"

  • Barlow signs 5-year lease next to Good Samaritan Hospital, intending flower shop serving hospital visitors/patients. Hospital announces permanent closure in 6 months. Barlow claims frustration, seeks rescission. Stewart (landlord) may have known of likely closure at signing.
  • Questions: apply frustration elements; is purpose "substantially frustrated" under Mel Frank? Does Stewart's possible knowledge matter — what doctrine does that invoke? What remedy does Barlow seek — rescission, damages, or something else?

Exam angles

  • Run the four-element checklist for whichever doctrine fits; then always address assumption of risk.
  • Distinguish the three: impossibility = literal impossibility; impracticability = cost/possibility; frustration = value/purpose destroyed.
  • Market change alone → weak claim (Hemlock). Governmental action → stronger, but still needs substantial frustration (Mel Frank, Lloyd v. Murphy).
  • Foreseeability is not an automatic bar, but more foreseeable → harder to show basic assumption.
  • Failure to negotiate a force majeure clause cuts against the party seeking excuse (Hemlock; Mel Frank Note 2).
  • COVID-19: courts generally declined relief; purpose often not "virtually" frustrated because some operations possible; pandemic foreseeable as a category; force majeure clauses covering "epidemic"/"government action" fared better.
  • Hemlock Note 1: overlap between impracticability and frustration elements; hard to prevail because (a) establishing the shared basic assumption is problematic, (b) showing impracticability or sufficient frustration is very hard.
