In one paragraph

When parties leave terms open or plan a later formal writing, ask two questions. (1) Did they intend to be bound now? (2) Are the terms definite enough for a court to determine breach and give a remedy? The classical view refuses to fill gaps (Walker v. Keith). The modern view, and the UCC, focus on intent and supply reasonable terms. Letters of intent may be binding, non-binding, or create a duty to negotiate in good faith (Quake Construction v. American Airlines).

Indefiniteness

  • Classical rule: a contract must be reasonably certain as to material terms. If the parties left a material term (price, rent, quantity) to future agreement without an objective standard or mechanism, there is no contract. Courts won't make the contract for them (Walker v. Keith: renewal rent "to be agreed upon" with a vague comparative standard).
  • Modern and Restatement view (§ 33) [Outside knowledge: section number]: terms are reasonably certain if they provide a basis for determining breach and an appropriate remedy. Open terms may show a lack of intent to be bound, but courts can supply reasonable terms.
  • UCC § 2-204(3): a contract for sale does not fail for indefiniteness if the parties intended to contract and there is a reasonably certain basis for a remedy. Gap-fillers (§ 2-305 open price, § 2-309 time, etc.). See Jannusch v. Naffziger.
  • Reliance: courts are more willing to supply a term (e.g., reasonable rent) where a party reasonably and materially relied on an apparent commitment (Walker v. Keith Note 3).

Agreements to agree

  • An "agreement to agree" on a material term is generally unenforceable unless it includes a method for fixing the term: a formula, an appraisal or arbitration mechanism, or an objective external standard (market price, an index).
  • Policy debate (Walker Notes 1-2): "judicial paternalism" and the cost of fact-finding vs. reading the clause out of the contract and defeating reasonable expectations.

Letters of intent and preliminary agreements

  • Test: intent to be bound (Quake Construction v. American Airlines). First look at the document. If ambiguous, look at extrinsic evidence.
  • Factors: whether the type of agreement is usually in writing; whether it needs a formal writing for full expression; whether it has few or many details; the amount involved; whether the contract is common or unusual; standard forms; partial performance or reliance; and language such as "subject to," cancellation clauses, or "formal contract to follow."
  • Three categories:
    1. Binding preliminary agreement: the formal writing is just a memorial.
    2. Non-binding until the formal contract is executed.
    3. Binding duty to negotiate in good faith toward a final agreement (Quake concurrence; Pops Cones v. Resorts International Note 5). [Outside knowledge: the "Type II" agreement in the Tribune line of New York cases.]
  • Remedies: if only a duty to negotiate was breached, damages are generally reliance-based, not expected profits on the deal ([Outside knowledge]).
  • Drafting lesson (slides): state clearly whether a letter is binding. Ambiguity breeds litigation (the Pennzoil-Texaco example in Quake).

Alternative theory: promissory estoppel

  • Even without a contract, reliance on assurances during negotiations may support recovery (Pops Cones v. Resorts International).
