Quick reference

Citation: 88 F. Supp. 2d 116 (S.D.N.Y. 1999), aff'd, 210 F.3d 88 (2d Cir. 2000) · Casebook: Knapp p. 33 (and pp. 59, 64, 92) · Topic: Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance One line: A TV ad showing a Harrier jet "for 7,000,000 Pepsi Points" was not an offer; no reasonable viewer could think it was serious.

Coverage

Not reprinted in Knapp. It appears in Ray Note 6 (p. 33) and is cited in Sateriale (p. 59). [Outside knowledge] marks details not in your materials.

Overview

The modern counterpart to Lucy v. Zehmer. It applies two rules together: (1) advertisements are generally not offers, and (2) under the objective theory, a statement no reasonable person would take seriously is not an offer.

Parties

  • Plaintiff: John Leonard [Outside knowledge: name]. A consumer who tried to "buy" the jet by sending Pepsi Points plus cash, and wanted the jet delivered.
  • Defendant: PepsiCo, Inc. The beverage company that ran the "Pepsi Stuff" promotion. It wanted a declaration that it had made no offer and owed no jet.

Facts

  • From Knapp: Pepsi's ad showed a Harrier jet (worth about $23 million) as available for 7 million Pepsi Points. Points could be bought for 10 cents each, about $700,000 in total.
  • [Outside knowledge]: Leonard collected 15 points and sent a check for about $700,000 to buy the rest, as the catalog rules allowed. Pepsi refused, saying the jet was a joke and not in the catalog.

Procedural History

  • From Knapp: the district court ruled for Pepsi, and the Second Circuit affirmed (210 F.3d 88).
  • [Outside knowledge]: it was decided on Pepsi's motion for summary judgment.

Issue

Did a humorous television commercial showing a military jet as a "Pepsi Stuff" prize create an offer that a consumer could accept by tendering the stated points?

Holding

No. Pepsi was not contractually bound. "No reasonable person would interpret the advertisement as having been seriously intended" (Knapp's summary).

Reasoning

  • Advertisements are invitations to deal, not offers, unless they show clear commitment and leave nothing open for negotiation (Restatement (Second) § 26 cmt. b; Lefkowitz is the exception for "first come, first served" ads). [Outside knowledge: the court relied on this general rule and found the ad, with its reference to the catalog, was at most an invitation.]
  • Objective test applied to jest: the ad's tone was obviously exaggerated. A teenager flying a jet to school, the impracticality of a combat jet and the gap between $700,000 and a $23 million plane showed it was a joke.
  • [Outside knowledge]: the court also found that the statute of frauds would bar enforcement for lack of a signed writing.

Rule

An advertisement is ordinarily not an offer. A statement that a reasonable person would recognize as a joke or puffery creates no power of acceptance, whatever the recipient's subjective belief.

Why It Matters

  • It shows the limit of the objective theory in the promisee's favor: the test is what a reasonable offeree would believe, not what this offeree believed.
  • It is a standard illustration of the "ads are not offers" rule, which the slides question in Sateriale v. RJ Reynolds (Sateriale doubted the rule even exists under California law and cited Izadi).

Other Key Points

  • Compare Lucy v. Zehmer: the joke defense failed there because the context showed seriousness.
  • Compare Lonergan v. Scolnick: a newspaper ad was a "mere request for an offer."
  • Knapp p. 59 cites Leonard for California and New York treatment of ads; p. 64 and p. 92 revisit it in problems.
