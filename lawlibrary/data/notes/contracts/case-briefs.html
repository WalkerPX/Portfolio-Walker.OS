Part 1 — Making a contract

Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance

CaseYearTakeaway
Ray v. Eurice Bros1952A builder who read and signed a contract incorporating the owners' specifications is bound by them, even if it subjectively thought its own specifications applied. The test for assent is objective.
Lonergan v. Scolnick1954Letters answering a buyer's questions about land and urging him to "decide fast" were preliminary negotiations, not an offer, so there was nothing to accept.
Lucy v. Zehmer (note case)1954A seller who claimed he was "only joking" when he signed a deal to sell his farm over drinks was bound, because a reasonable buyer would have taken him seriously.
Normile v. Miller1985"You snooze, you lose": once the offeree learns the property has been sold to someone else, the offer is revoked and can no longer be accepted.
Izadi v. Machado Ford (note case)1989A car dealer's misleading "bait and switch" newspaper ad could be treated as a binding offer.
Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute (problem case)1991A forum-selection clause buried in fine print on a cruise ticket was enforced, so the injured passengers had to sue in Florida.
Cook v. Coldwell Banker1998Once an agent had substantially performed under her broker's bonus offer, the broker could not change the terms to require her to stay until a March banquet.
Leonard v. Pepsico (note case)1999A TV ad showing a Harrier jet "for 7,000,000 Pepsi Points" was not an offer; no reasonable viewer could think it was serious.
Sateriale v. RJ Reynolds2012A cigarette company's "Camel Cash" rewards program could be an offer for a unilateral contract, accepted by buying, saving and redeeming certificates. The breach-of-contract claim survived dismissal.

Agreements to Agree and Letters of Intent

CaseYearTakeaway
Walker v. Keith1964A lease renewal option leaving rent to be "agreed upon" on a vague comparative basis was too indefinite to enforce, and courts will not set the rent themselves.
Quake Construction v. American Airlines1990Whether a letter of intent is binding depends on the parties' intent. Because this letter was ambiguous (it awarded the contract but had a cancellation clause and contemplated a formal contract), intent was a question of fact for remand.

Option Contracts and Firm Offers

CaseYearTakeaway
James Baird v. Gimbel Bros1933A supplier's mistaken bid could be withdrawn before acceptance even though the general contractor had already used it. No unilateral contract, no promissory estoppel, no option. This is the minority view.
Drennan v. Star Paving1958A subcontractor's bid, reasonably relied on by a general contractor in its own winning bid, becomes irrevocable through promissory estoppel. The sub must pay the difference to the next-lowest bidder.
Berryman v. Kmoch1977A land option reciting "$10 and other valuable consideration" was not binding because the $10 was never paid. The option was a revocable offer, revoked when the optionee learned the land had been sold.

UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation

CaseYearTakeaway
Princess Cruises v. General Electric1998A ship repair contract was predominantly for services, so the common law (not UCC § 2-207) applied. GE's final quotation, accepted by Princess's conduct, governed, and its limitation of liability cut a $4.58 million verdict to $231,925.
EC Styberg Engineering v. Eaton2007Despite e-mails suggesting a minimum purchase commitment, sophisticated parties never formed a contract for 13,000 brake units. Their past practice of formal purchase orders showed they had not reached agreement.
Jannusch v. Naffziger2008The oral sale of a food-truck business for $150,000 was mainly a sale of goods, and the buyers' conduct showed a contract under UCC § 2-204 even though some details were left open.

Interpretation

CaseYearTakeaway
Taylor v. State Farm1993Arizona adopts the Corbin approach. The judge considers extrinsic evidence first to decide whether contract language is reasonably susceptible to the proffered meaning, rejecting the "plain meaning" rule. Evidence about a release was properly admitted.
Shay v. Aldrich (note case)2010A 4-3 Michigan Supreme Court admitted extrinsic evidence to show that a release of "all other ... persons" was not meant to release officers who weren't party to the settlement. The dissent insisted on plain meaning.

Part 2 — Which promises the law enforces

Consideration

CaseYearTakeaway
Hamer v. Sidway1891A nephew's giving up drinking, smoking, swearing and gambling until 21, things he had a legal right to do, was consideration for his uncle's promise of $5,000, even though it benefited only the nephew.
Wood v. Lucy Lady Duff-Gordon1917An exclusive agency contract implies a promise by the agent to use reasonable efforts, so the agent's side is not illusory and the contract is enforceable.
Dougherty v. Salt1919An aunt's $3,000 note to her eight-year-old nephew, given out of affection and marked "value received," was an unenforceable promise to make a gift.
Plowman v. Indian Refining1937A company's promise of lifetime half-pay to long-serving older employees was unenforceable. Past service, moral obligation and picking up the checks were not consideration.
Batsakis v. Demotsis (note case)1949A wartime promise to repay $2,000 plus 8% for 500,000 drachmae (worth about $25) was enforced. Courts do not inquire into the adequacy of consideration.
Harris v. Time1987Opening an envelope was technically consideration for Time's "free calculator watch" promise, but the claim was dismissed because "the law disregards trifles."
Marshall Durbin Food v. Baker2005An at-will executive's continued service through a crisis was consideration for the company's promise of five years of post-employment pay if certain triggering events occurred. The promise was conditional, not illusory.
Pennsy Supply v. American Ash Recycling2006"Free" AggRite was not a gift. Taking it off the supplier's hands saved the supplier disposal costs, so the exchange was a bargain supported by consideration.

Promissory Estoppel

CaseYearTakeaway
Kirksey v. Kirksey1845A brother-in-law's promise of "a place to raise your family" to a widow who moved 60 miles in reliance was a "mere gratuity" and unenforceable. Modern courts would likely enforce it under § 90.
King v. Trustees of Boston University1995A jury could find that Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr.'s 1964 letter promised his papers to Boston University as an enforceable charitable pledge, supported by consideration or reliance.
Pops Cones v. Resorts International1998A casino's repeated assurances that a lease deal was "done" could support promissory estoppel for a tenant who gave up its old lease in reliance. New Jersey no longer requires a "clear and definite" promise.

Restitution and Promissory Restitution

CaseYearTakeaway
Mills v. Wyman1825A father's promise, made after the fact, to pay a stranger for caring for his sick adult son was unenforceable. A moral obligation and past services are not consideration.
Webb v. McGowin1935A man who was saved from death or serious injury by a worker who was crippled in the act, and who then promised the worker lifetime payments, was bound by that promise because he received a material benefit.
Marvin v. Marvin (note case)1976Courts should enforce express contracts between unmarried cohabitants (except to the extent based on sexual services) and may use implied contract and equitable remedies to protect their expectations.
Watts v. Watts1987A woman who cohabited with a man for 12 years and helped build his business stated claims for express or implied-in-fact contract and for unjust enrichment, though not under the divorce statute.
Commerce Partnership v. Equity Contracting1997An unpaid subcontractor can recover from the owner in unjust enrichment only if the owner has not paid anyone for the work. Equity failed to prove that, so it lost.
Credit Bureau Enterprises v. Pelo2000A man involuntarily hospitalized under a court order must pay the reasonable value of the necessary services in restitution (contract implied in law), whatever the validity of the form he signed.

Good Faith and Fair Dealing

CaseYearTakeaway
Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing1978A manufacturer that ends an indefinite-duration oral distributorship for goods must give reasonable notice under UCC § 2-309(3).
Locke v. Warner Bros1997Warner's express discretion to accept or reject Sondra Locke's film proposals was still subject to the implied covenant of good faith. Evidence that it never intended to consider them raised a triable issue.
Seidenberg v. Summit Bank2002Sellers of an insurance brokerage who became employees of the buyer bank could pursue a good-faith claim that the bank pretextually ended their employment and frustrated their earn-out. Sophistication does not bar the claim.

Part 3 — Writings

Statute of Frauds

CaseYearTakeaway
Burns v. McCormick (note case)1922The source of the strict "unequivocally referable" test: part performance takes an oral land contract out of the statute only if the acts point unmistakably to the contract.
Crabtree v. Elizabeth Arden1953A two-year employment contract satisfied the statute of frauds by piecing together signed payroll cards and an unsigned memo that referred to the same transaction. Parol evidence could show the connection.
Buffaloe v. Hart1994An oral $20,000 sale of five tobacco barns was enforceable under UCC § 2-201(3)(c). The buyer's check, though never cashed and later returned torn, was "payment accepted," and the buyer had "received and accepted" the barns.
Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice1997A promised two-year job, which would otherwise be barred by the one-year provision, could be enforced through promissory estoppel (Restatement § 139) because the employee relied by moving to Alaska.
Beaver v. Brumlow2010An oral agreement to sell a home site was specifically enforced under the part-performance exception. The buyers took possession and spent about $85,000 on improvements in reliance.

Parol Evidence Rule

CaseYearTakeaway
Thompson v. Libby1885A short written log-sale contract that looked complete on its face was a full integration, so evidence of an oral warranty of quality was inadmissible.
Sherrodd v. Morrison-Knudsen1991A subcontractor that signed a fixed-price ($97,500) earthwork contract could not introduce evidence of oral assurances about the quantity, because that evidence directly contradicted the writing. A strong dissent objected.

Part 4 — Defenses and excuses

Capacity

CaseYearTakeaway
Ortelere v. Teachers Retirement Board (note case)1969The leading case adopting the volitional test: a contract (here, a pension election) is voidable when mental illness prevents a party from acting reasonably in relation to the transaction and the other party knew or should have known of the condition.
Dodson v. Shrader1992A 16-year-old who disaffirmed his purchase of a used truck could recover the price, but the seller may set off the truck's depreciation and use, absent overreaching or fraud by the seller.
Sparrow v. Demonico2012A party claiming she lacked capacity to agree to a mediated settlement must offer medical or expert evidence that her condition affected her understanding or her ability to act reasonably. Lay testimony about an emotional breakdown was not enough.

Duress and Undue Influence

CaseYearTakeaway
Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District1966A teacher pressured to resign immediately, while exhausted after his arrest, by officials who came to his home, stated a claim to rescind the resignation for undue influence, though not for duress, fraud, mistake or incapacity.
Totem Marine v. Alyeska Pipeline1978A small, financially desperate contractor that settled a $260,000-$300,000 claim for $97,500 stated a claim for economic duress. Alyeska allegedly withheld payment knowing Totem faced bankruptcy.
Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing2022A divided Ninth Circuit held that an H-2A farm worker who signed arbitration agreements at an end-of-day orientation did not prove economic duress or undue influence under California law. The dissent stressed the employer's "coercive atmosphere."

Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure

CaseYearTakeaway
Laidlaw v. Organ (note case)1817A tobacco buyer who knew, before the seller, that the War of 1812 had ended (which would raise prices) had no duty to disclose it. This is the classic statement of the classical no-duty rule.
Syester v. Banta1965An elderly widow sold nearly $30,000 of Arthur Murray dance lessons through flattery could set aside her release of an earlier suit and recover actual and punitive damages for fraud.
Hill v. Jones1986Home sellers had a duty to disclose a known past termite infestation that materially affected the property's value and wasn't readily observable to the buyers.
Park 100 Investors v. Kartes1995Business owners who signed a personal guaranty that the landlord's agent misdescribed as "lease papers," under time pressure, were not bound. Active misrepresentation of a document's character overrides the duty to read.

Unconscionability

CaseYearTakeaway
Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture1965Courts may refuse to enforce unconscionable contracts. Unconscionability means "an absence of meaningful choice ... together with contract terms which are unreasonably favorable to the other party." Remanded for findings.
Higgins v. Superior Court2006An arbitration clause buried in an Extreme Makeover: Home Edition participant agreement, signed by orphaned siblings (some minors) without explanation, was unconscionable and unenforceable.
In re Checking Account Overdraft Litigation (note case)2010Bank customers could use unconscionability affirmatively to recover excessive overdraft fees the banks had already taken. Unconscionability can be a sword, not just a shield.
De La Torre v. CashCall2018The interest rate on a consumer loan of $2,500 or more, for which California sets no rate cap, can still be unconscionable. Here the rates were 96-135%.

Mistake

CaseYearTakeaway
Sherwood v. Walker (note case)1887The famous "barren cow" case. A cow sold as barren turned out to be pregnant, and the seller could rescind because the mistake went to "the very nature of the thing," not merely its quality.
Gartner v. Eikill (note case)1982A buyer could rescind a land purchase for mutual mistake when an unknown zoning restriction made the land unusable for his intended purpose, a result that sits uneasily with Lenawee.
Lenawee County Board of Health v. Messerly1982Both sides were mistaken about a hidden, illegal septic system, but the buyers could not rescind because the "in its present condition" clause allocated that risk to them.
Donovan v. RRL Corp (note case)2001A car dealer's newspaper ad listing a used Jaguar at a price thousands below the intended price was an offer, but the dealer could rescind for unilateral mistake because enforcing it would be unconscionable.
BMW Financial Services v. Deloach2017A creditor that mistakenly sent a $114,000 judgment to a collection agency, which settled for $14,000, could not rescind for unilateral mistake. It bore the risk of its own error, and losing a treble-damages windfall was not unconscionable.

Impracticability and Frustration

CaseYearTakeaway
Taylor v. Caldwell (note case)1863When a music hall essential to the contract burned down before the performances, the owner was excused. The contract rested on the hall's continued existence.
Krell v. Henry (note case)1903A man who rented a room to watch King Edward VII's coronation procession was excused from paying when the coronation was postponed. This is the origin of frustration of purpose.
Mineral Park Land v. Howard (note case)1916A contractor who agreed to take all its gravel from the plaintiff's land was excused from taking the gravel below water level, because extracting it would cost about ten times the normal cost. This is the origin of commercial impracticability.
Lloyd v. Murphy (slides only)1944A lessee who leased property to sell new cars was not excused when wartime restrictions limited new car sales. The premises still had other uses and the risk was foreseeable.
Mel Frank Tool v. Di-Chem1998A chemical distributor that could no longer store hazardous chemicals in its leased warehouse after a new ordinance was not excused from the lease. It still had serviceable uses, so the lease was not "virtually worthless."
Hemlock Semiconductor v. Solarworld2017A German buyer under long-term, fixed-price take-or-pay polysilicon contracts was not excused when Chinese subsidies crashed market prices. Market stability was not a basic assumption, and the buyer assumed that risk.

Part 5 — Performance and breach

Express Conditions

CaseYearTakeaway
JNA Realty v. Cross Bay Chelsea1977A restaurant tenant who gave notice of renewal four months late could be relieved of the notice condition in equity, to avoid forfeiting its valuable improvements and goodwill, if the delay was mere "venial inattention" and the landlord was not prejudiced.
Maxton Builders v. Lo Galbo (note case)1986Written notice of cancellation that arrived a few days late did not satisfy an express condition, even though the other party already knew the buyers intended to cancel.
Oppenheimer v. Oppenheim Appel Dixon1995An express condition (written notice of the landlord's consent by February 25) must be strictly satisfied. Substantial performance (telephone notice) does not count, so a $1.2 million verdict was overturned.

Material Breach and Substantial Performance

CaseYearTakeaway
Jacob and Youngs v. Kent1921A builder that installed non-Reading pipe of equal quality substantially performed, so it can recover the balance of the price, minus damages measured by the (nominal) difference in value, not the cost of tearing out and replacing the pipe.
Sackett v. Spindler1967A buyer who repeatedly failed to pay the balance for newspaper stock (with a bounced check and broken promises) committed a total breach, so the seller could terminate, resell and recover damages.

Anticipatory Repudiation and Adequate Assurance

CaseYearTakeaway
Truman L. Flatt and Sons v. Schupf1995A buyer's letter asking for a lower land price was not an anticipatory repudiation because it was not "definite and unequivocal." In any event the buyer retracted it before the sellers relied or declared the deal over.
Hornell Brewing v. Spry1997A beverage maker with reasonable grounds for insecurity about its Canadian distributor's finances properly demanded adequate assurance. The distributor's failure to respond was a repudiation that justified ending the relationship.

Part 6 — Remedies

Expectation Damages

CaseYearTakeaway
Handicapped Childrens Education Board v. Lukaszewski1983When a therapist quit mid-contract, the employer recovered the extra cost of a replacement, even though the replacement was more experienced, because no equally qualified person was available at the contract salary.
Crabbys v. Hamilton2008When buyers breached a contract to buy commercial real estate, the seller recovered the contract price minus market value at the time of breach. A resale about a year later by highly motivated sellers was substantial evidence of that market value.

Limits on Damages

CaseYearTakeaway
Hadley v. Baxendale1854A carrier that delayed delivering a broken mill shaft was not liable for the mill's lost profits, because it was not told that the mill would sit idle without the shaft. Consequential damages must be within the parties' contemplation.
Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge1929After the county repudiated a bridge contract, the builder could not keep building and charge for everything. It could recover only its costs up to the repudiation plus its expected profit.
Florafax v. GTE Market Resources1997A telemarketing vendor whose understaffing cost its client a lucrative third-party contract was liable for two years of the client's lost net profits on that contract. They were foreseeable, proved with reasonable certainty, and caused by the breach.
Erlich v. Menezes1999Homeowners whose "dreamhouse" leaked everywhere because of a contractor's negligence could recover repair costs but not emotional distress damages, under either a tort or a contract theory.
Zapata Hermanos v. Hearthside Baking2002A winning seller could not recover its attorney's fees as "losses" under the CISG. Under the American rule, fees require a contract term, a statute or a court's sanction power, and pre-litigation bad faith doesn't qualify.
Maness v. Collins2010A wrongfully terminated employee who built himself a house instead of job-hunting still recovered full damages, because the employer never proved that comparable jobs were available.

Reliance and Restitution Damages

CaseYearTakeaway
US ex rel Coastal Steel v. Algernon Blair1973A subcontractor that justifiably quit after the prime contractor's material breach could recover the reasonable value of its work in quantum meruit, even though it would have lost money on the whole contract.
Wartzman v. Hightower Productions1983Promoters of a world-record flagpole-sitting stunt, whose venture collapsed because their lawyers failed to advise them about securities laws, recovered reliance damages (their lost investment), since lost profits were too speculative to prove.
Lancellotti v. Thomas1985A buyer who defaulted on the purchase of a hoagie business may recover his payments to the extent they exceed the harm his breach caused. Pennsylvania adopts Restatement § 374 and rejects the old forfeiture rule.
Walser v. Toyota Motor Sales1994Would-be Lexus dealers who relied on a mistaken assurance of approval won on promissory estoppel but were properly limited to out-of-pocket reliance damages ($232,131), not lost dealership profits.
Shaffer v. George Washington University2022Students who paid for in-person education before COVID-19 forced classes online stated claims for breach of an implied-in-fact contract and for restitution (unjust enrichment), but not for breach of an express contract.

Specific Performance

CaseYearTakeaway
City Stores v. Ammerman1967Shopping center developers who promised a department store a lease in exchange for its support of their rezoning were ordered to specifically perform by giving it a lease on terms as favorable as other major stores got.
Reier Broadcasting v. Kramer2003A radio station could not get an injunction to stop Montana State's football coach from broadcasting for a competitor in breach of his exclusivity clause, because equity will not enforce personal service contracts, even negatively, in these circumstances.

Liquidated Damages

CaseYearTakeaway
Barrie School v. Patch2007Parents who pulled their child out of a private school after the withdrawal deadline owed the full year's tuition under a valid liquidated damages clause. A party protected by a valid liquidated damages clause has no duty to mitigate.
