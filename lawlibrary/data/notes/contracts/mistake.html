In one paragraph

A mistake is a belief not in accord with the facts existing when the contract is made. A mistake about the future doesn't count; that belongs to Impracticability and Frustration. Mutual mistake (§ 152): both parties are wrong about a basic assumption, with a material effect on the exchange. The adversely affected party may avoid the contract unless it bears the risk (§ 154). Unilateral mistake (§ 153) requires the same elements plus either unconscionable enforcement or that the other party had reason to know of or caused the mistake. Risk allocation is the pivot. In Lenawee County Board of Health v. Messerly, there was a mutual mistake but the buyers bore the risk under an "as is" clause. In BMW Financial Services v. Deloach, BMW bore the risk of its own clerical error, and enforcement was not unconscionable.

Mutual mistake

Checklist (Restatement § 152):

  1. Both parties, at the time of contracting,
  2. were mistaken
  3. about a basic assumption on which the contract was made,
  4. with a material effect on the agreed exchange, and
  5. the adversely affected party does not bear the risk (§ 154).
  • Lenawee County Board of Health v. Messerly: the Pickleses bought an apartment building. A prior owner (Bloom) had installed a defective septic system without a permit. It failed, and the county barred occupancy. Neither party knew. Mutual mistake about a basic assumption, but rescission denied: the contract said the buyers took the property "in its present condition," so they bore the risk. The truly responsible party (Bloom) was neither buyer nor seller.
  • Sherwood v. Walker (note case): a cow, Rose 2d of Aberlone, sold as barren for about $80, turned out to be pregnant and worth far more. Rescission was allowed because the mistake went to the "very nature" of the thing, not just its quality. Lenawee rejected the nature/quality distinction as unworkable and replaced it with the Restatement basic-assumption and risk analysis. [Outside knowledge] price and name details are from general knowledge; see the brief.
  • Gartner v. Eikill (note case, Lenawee Note 8): buyers mistaken about zoning were denied relief. Possible reconciliation with Lenawee: zoning is public record, so the buyer was at fault, while the septic defect was hidden.

"As is" clauses (Lenawee Note 3):

  • Classical: decisive. You assumed the risk.
  • Modern: one factor, especially weak if it is boilerplate and wasn't negotiated.

Unilateral mistake

Checklist (Restatement § 153):

  1. One party, at the time of contracting, was mistaken
  2. about a basic assumption,
  3. with a material effect adverse to that party,
  4. that party does not bear the risk, and
  5. either (a) enforcement would be unconscionable, or (b) the other party had reason to know of the mistake (a "palpable" mistake) or its fault caused it.
  • § 153(a), unconscionability: a high bar. The result of enforcement must "shock the conscience."
  • § 153(b), palpable mistake: you can't snap up an offer you know or should know is a mistake.
  • BMW Financial Services v. Deloach: BMW had a $114,000 default judgment (including treble damages) against Deloach, who had tampered with an odometer. A clerical error sent the account to a collection agency, which settled for $14,000 without knowing of the judgment. (1) A basic-assumption mistake: yes. (2) Material adverse effect: yes. (3) Risk: BMW bears it, because the error was entirely its own and the other side had no fault or reason to know. (4) Unconscionable: no, because BMW lost mostly a treble-damages windfall, not money out of pocket. Rescission denied.
  • Donovan v. RRL Corp (note case): a car dealer's newspaper ad listed a Jaguar far below its intended price because of a clerical error. Rescission granted: the dealer acted in good faith, and enforcing the deal would have been unconscionable. Compare BMW: both were administrative errors, but the parties' fault and whether the loss was real value or a windfall differ. See also Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance (advertisements as offers).

Allocation of risk (Restatement 154)

A party bears the risk of a mistake when:

  • (a) the agreement allocates it (for example, "as is");
  • (b) conscious ignorance: the party knew its knowledge was limited but treated it as enough; or
  • (c) the court allocates it because that is reasonable in the circumstances.
  • § 157: a mistaken party's negligence does not bar relief unless it amounts to a failure to act in good faith and by reasonable standards of fair dealing (gross negligence) (Note 5).

Mistake vs. misrepresentation

SituationDoctrine
Active misstatement to induce assentFraud in the inducement (Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure › Fraudulent misrepresentation)
Failure to disclose a known defectDuty to disclose (Hill v. Jones; Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure › Nondisclosure)
Both parties simply wrongMutual mistake (Lenawee County Board of Health v. Messerly)
One party wrong, the other unawareUnilateral mistake (BMW Financial Services v. Deloach)
  • Mistake in expression (a typo in the writing) → reformation. [Outside knowledge] (Restatement § 155).
  • Objective theory: a party who signs without understanding is usually bound (Ray v. Eurice Bros).

Exam framework

  1. Existing fact at formation? (Not a prediction; future events go to impracticability or frustration.)
  2. Who was mistaken? Both (mutual) or one (unilateral)?
  3. Basic assumption? Material effect?
  4. Risk (§ 154): check the agreement ("as is": classical vs. modern view), conscious ignorance and court allocation. This is usually decisive.
  5. Unilateral: add unconscionability (shock the conscience; windfall vs. real loss) or reason to know / caused.
  6. Negligence: ordinary negligence doesn't bar relief; gross negligence or bad faith does (§ 157).
  7. Remedy: rescission and restitution; reformation for errors in expression.

Hypos (slides)

  • Hypo 1, "The Flooded Lot": a $100,000 lot that floods every other year and can't support construction. The seller disclosed only one flood in 10 years, the buyers' contractor said the land was suitable, and the buyers declined an "as is" box. Mutual mistake elements are likely met. Risk under § 154: no "as is" clause (unlike Lenawee). Did relying on the contractor amount to conscious ignorance? Also consider nondisclosure by the seller.
  • Hypo 2, "The Settlement Trap": a billing error marks the litigation "closed," and the mediator settles for $30,000 against $400,000 in actual exposure. Under BMW, the company bears the risk of its own administrative error. But the lost value is real (lost profits), not a windfall, so the unconscionability argument is stronger. Did the ex-employee have reason to know? Did it rely?
