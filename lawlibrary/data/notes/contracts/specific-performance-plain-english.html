The short version

Usually a court awards money for a breach. In special cases, especially land and truly unique items, the court can order the breaching side to actually do what it promised. Courts won't force anyone to personally work for someone.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Specific Performance.

What it's about

Specific performance is a court order to perform the contract instead of paying money. It's an "equitable" remedy, so it's discretionary and used only when money isn't adequate.

When courts grant it

  • Land: every piece of land is treated as unique, so buyers can usually get specific performance.
  • Unique goods: heirlooms, art, custom items, or goods you can't buy elsewhere (UCC § 2-716).
  • When damages are too hard to measure, for example long-term supply deals in short markets.

When courts refuse it

  • Personal services: courts won't force someone to work (that would be close to involuntary servitude). Sometimes they'll stop the person from working for a competitor, but not always.
  • Too vague: the terms must be clear enough for a court order.
  • Too hard to supervise: ongoing, complex performance a court can't monitor.
  • Unfair or harsh: equity looks at fairness, delay and the conduct of the party asking.

An everyday example

You contract to buy a specific lakefront house, and the seller backs out because they got a better offer. A court can order the seller to complete the sale. No amount of money gets you that house.

The cases, in plain words

  • City Stores v. Ammerman: developers promised a department store a lease, and the court ordered them to give it one on terms as good as other major stores got.
  • Beaver v. Brumlow: an oral sale of a home site was specifically enforced because the buyers had moved in and improved the land.
  • Reier Broadcasting v. Kramer: a radio station couldn't get an order stopping a football coach from broadcasting for a competitor. Courts won't enforce personal service contracts this way.
  • Handicapped Childrens Education Board v. Lukaszewski: an employer that couldn't force a therapist to keep working recovered money damages instead.

Words you'll hear

  • Equitable remedy: a court order (not money), granted at the judge's discretion.
  • Injunction: an order to do, or not do, something.
  • Adequate remedy at law: money damages that are good enough.
