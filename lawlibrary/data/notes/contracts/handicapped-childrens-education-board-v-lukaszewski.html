Quick reference

Citation: 112 Wis. 2d 197, 332 N.W.2d 774 (1983) · Casebook: Knapp p. 910 · Topic: Expectation Damages › Employment contracts One line: When a therapist quit mid-contract, the employer recovered the extra cost of a replacement, even though the replacement was more experienced, because no equally qualified person was available at the contract salary.

Overview

The employer's measure of damages when an employee breaches: cost of replacement minus contract price. Extra value from a better-qualified replacement is not a "benefit" to deduct if it was forced on the employer.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / petitioner: Handicapped Children's Education Board (Ozaukee County, Wisconsin). It wanted the added cost of hiring a replacement.
  • Defendant / respondent: Elaine Lukaszewski. A speech and language therapist who resigned before her contract term ended. She argued health excused her and that the Board suffered no loss.

Facts

  • January 1978: the Board hired Lukaszewski as a speech and language therapist for the spring term. She later signed a contract for the 1978-79 school year. [Outside knowledge: salary about $10,760.]
  • Before or near the start of the year, she was offered a better-paying job closer to home and resigned. She claimed the long commute aggravated her high blood pressure (a health excuse). The Board refused to release her, and she left anyway.
  • The Board searched for a replacement and could find only a more experienced therapist at a higher salary. [Outside knowledge: about $1,026.64 more.]

Procedural History

  • Circuit court: found breach (health not a valid excuse) and awarded the Board the extra cost of the replacement.
  • Court of appeals: affirmed the breach but reversed the damage award (the Board got a more valuable teacher).
  • Supreme Court of Wisconsin: reversed the court of appeals on damages, reinstating the award. A dissent would have found no breach, because her health problems were a valid excuse.

Issue

Is an employer that hires a more experienced, higher-paid replacement after an employee's breach entitled to the difference in salary, or must that be offset by the replacement's greater value?

Holding

The employer recovers the cost of the replacement minus the contract price. Any added value from the more experienced replacement was "imposed upon" the Board, not a benefit, because a teacher of equivalent experience was not available (p. 912).

Reasoning

  • Expectation: put the Board where it would have been had Lukaszewski performed, which means a therapist at her contract salary.
  • The Board did not choose to upgrade. It took the only qualified candidate available. Had an equivalent teacher been available, the Board could not have recovered the cost of a superior one.
  • Specific performance of personal services is not available (Note 1), so damages are the remedy.

Rule

When an employee breaches, the employer may recover the reasonable cost of obtaining a replacement in excess of the contract price, if it acted reasonably and no equivalent replacement was available at the contract rate.

Why It Matters

  • It is the mirror image of employee claims against employers (e.g., Maness v. Collins).
  • Note 1: courts will not grant specific performance of personal services contracts (see Reier Broadcasting v. Kramer).
  • Note 2, health excuse: the dissent may be right that health problems are a legitimate excuse. But when an employee leaves for a better job, courts may be skeptical. Whether performance is excused is a question of fact.
