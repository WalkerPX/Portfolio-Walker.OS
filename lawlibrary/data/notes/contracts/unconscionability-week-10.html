Big picture

  • Doctrine rooted in fairness — protects parties who cannot protect themselves.
  • Assessed at the time the contract is made.
  • Requires both (a) procedural AND (b) substantive unconscionability.
  • Sliding scale (Note 1): high procedural → only modest substantive needed; and vice versa.
  • Decided by the court as a matter of law — not the jury (Note 5 after Williams).
  • Parties get a reasonable opportunity to present evidence on commercial setting, purpose, and effect.

Doctrine outline

Procedural unconscionability — unfairness in formation from unequal bargaining power. Two forms:

  • (a) "Lack of meaningful choice" — disparity in bargaining power, absence of alternative sellers.
  • (b) Terms "hidden or unintelligible" — fine print, convoluted language, high-pressure sales tactics.
  • Courts require a showing of only ONE form.

Substantive unconscionability — excessively disproportionate terms that "shock the conscience." Focus on fairness of the exchange. Evidence:

  • Terms inconsistent with practices in the trade.
  • Terms that seriously disadvantage one party without substantial additional advantage to the other.
  • Terms with coercive effect far exceeding economic justification.

Remedies — Restatement § 208 / UCC § 2-302 (if unconscionable at time made, court may):

  1. Refuse to enforce the contract; OR
  2. Enforce the remainder without the unconscionable term; OR
  3. Limit application of the term to avoid an unconscionable result.

Standardized agreements — Restatement § 211(3):

  • A term is not part of the agreement where the other party has reason to believe the assenting party would not assent if he knew the writing contained that term.
  • Note 2 (Higgins): some courts treat adhesion contracts as part of procedural unconscionability; others use a separate reasonable-expectations analysis.

Arbitration overlay:

  • State and federal statutes strongly favor enforcing arbitration agreements.
  • FAA generally preempts state law that invalidates arbitration clauses specifically.
  • But general contract defenses — including unconscionability — still apply if the defense applies equally to all contracts. This is the opening for Higgins.

Tests and elements

Checklist — unconscionability claim:

  • Procedural prong: lack of meaningful choice OR hidden/unintelligible terms (only one form needed).
  • Substantive prong: terms shock the conscience / disproportionate / no substantial added advantage / coercive beyond economic justification.
  • Apply sliding scale to weigh the two.
  • Confirm timing: at contract formation.
  • Choose remedy: refuse enforcement / enforce remainder / limit the term.

De La Torre formulation:

  • Oppression or surprise (procedural) PLUS overly harsh or one-sided results (substantive).
  • Factors: lender's costs and expenses; salience of the term to consumers; borrower's credit worthiness; existence of market imperfections.

Evidence checklist (slide 9):

  • Procedural: fine print; high-pressure tactics; absence of other sellers.
  • Substantive: term inconsistent with trade practice; severe impact with little added economic value to the other side — coercive effect rather than legitimate protection.

Cases

  • Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co. (p. 662) — leading case on meaning of the doctrine. Williams on welfare ($218/month) bought household goods, then a $514 stereo. "Add-on"/"dragnet" clause: payments credited pro rata to all contracts; company kept title to all items until every purchase paid to zero. On default it sought to replevy all items. Trial and appellate courts held unconscionability could not be a defense; D.C. Circuit (Judge Skelly Wright) reversed. Definition: "an absence of meaningful choice on the part of one of the parties together with contract terms which are unreasonably favorable to the other party." NOTE: court did NOT hold the contract unconscionable — only that the trial court erred in refusing to entertain the claim; remanded for a hearing.
    • Note 1: Procedural — door-to-door sales, buyers signing blank forms, convoluted language; easily satisfies "hidden or unintelligible." Substantive — was the add-on clause the problem, or selling the stereo to someone on public assistance? Paternalism concern (Leff): should a court refuse enforcement because it thinks the contract isn't in one party's best interests? Counter: buried fine print / high-pressure tactics mean the buyer did not truly act voluntarily; refusing enforcement gives effect to what the buyer actually understood.
  • Higgins v. Superior Court (p. 676) — challenge to arbitration clause in an Extreme Makeover: Home Edition production agreement. Five orphaned Higgins children taken in by the Leomitis (church family); episode rebuilt the Leomiti house. Higgins children later alleged verbal abuse and exploitation by Leomiti children; sought to litigate; production company moved to compel arbitration. Procedural: no expectation the Higgins children would read the agreement; adhesion — take-it-or-leave-it. Substantive: troublesome aspects — cost of arbitration, lack of bilaterality, procedural constraints built into the arbitration. A good case can be made the agreement is unconscionable; but also arguable the clause is not unbalanced enough to "shock the conscience." (Holding not stated on slides — now briefed: Higgins v. Superior Court.)
  • De La Torre v. CashCall, Inc. (p. 689) — California Supreme Court (2018), payday loans. State capped rates on consumer loans under $2,500 but left $2,500+ loans unregulated as to rate; CashCall charged 96–135% on $2,500+ loans. CashCall argued the omission of a cap showed the Legislature intended unconscionability not to apply — rejected: a separate Finance Code provision subjects consumer loans to unconscionability without limitation; rate caps ≠ unconscionability. CashCall also argued applying unconscionability would make courts economic rate-setters — rejected: trial courts have wide equitable discretion to fashion remedies without rate-setting.
    • Note 1: many states and the federal government have moved to regulate payday loans; lenders restructure loans to avoid regulation — as CashCall targeted the $2,500+ category. CFPB attempted regulation under Obama; Trump administration reversed those rules.
    • Note 4: most courts limit unconscionability to a defense against enforcement. A handful of recent cases — including the Checking Account Overdraft Litigation — allow it as a basis for affirmative recovery. A departure from traditional doctrine; whether it spreads remains to be seen.
  • Checking Account Overdraft Litigation — named only (Note 4, De La Torre) — now briefed: De La Torre v. CashCall, In re Checking Account Overdraft Litigation.

Examples and hypos

Hypo 1 — "The Furniture Store": Westside Furniture sells on installment in a low-income neighborhood; contracts signed at home, salesperson fills in details. Page 3 of a 4-page form buries a cross-collateralization clause (all goods collateral for any purchase; payments pro rata). Maria buys washer, dryer, then a TV; defaults on the TV; Westside seeks to repossess all items.

  • Identify procedural and substantive arguments.
  • Is one form of procedural unconscionability enough? Does Williams tell us?
  • What evidence would you introduce at the remand hearing for Maria?
  • Is there a paternalism objection? How do you respond?

Hypo 2 — "The Reality Show Waiver": chaotic audition day; coordinator hands over a two-page "standard release — everyone signs." Broad arbitration clause; 60-day claim deadline; damages capped at $1,000; production company exempt from any obligation to arbitrate its own claims. Contestant is 19, small town, no legal experience; later claims the show stole her recipe.

  • Identify procedural and substantive arguments.
  • Apply the sliding scale — what result?
  • Does Higgins's bilaterality problem apply?
  • Does federal policy favoring arbitration affect the analysis?

Hypo 3 — "The Payday Loan": QuickCash lends Renata $2,600 (just above the $2,500 cap threshold) at 120% APR. Home health aide with two jobs and a recent medical emergency; signs a one-page agreement without reading the rate disclosure buried at the bottom. Over 18 months pays $4,800 (nearly 2x principal) and still owes $1,900. Seeks to void the loan.

  • Identify procedural and substantive arguments under De La Torre.
  • Does the legislature's failure to cap rates above $2,500 matter? Apply De La Torre.
  • What remedy without economic rate-setting?
  • Compare De La Torre to Williams — what do they share?

Exam angles

  • Always run both prongs; then apply the sliding scale explicitly.
  • Pin the analysis to time of formation.
  • Procedural: pick ONE form (no meaningful choice OR hidden/unintelligible) — don't stack unnecessarily.
  • Substantive: use the three evidence categories; ask whether the term protects legitimately or only coerces.
  • Remedies: recite the three § 208 options; note court decides as a matter of law.
  • Williams trap: it did not hold the contract unconscionable — remand only.
  • Paternalism (Leff) debate: raise it, then answer with the voluntariness counter.
  • Arbitration: FAA preemption yields to generally applicable defenses — argue unconscionability applies to all contracts.
  • De La Torre: absence of a rate cap ≠ legislative intent to bar unconscionability; courts may craft equitable remedies without rate-setting.
  • Note 4: traditional rule = defense only; affirmative recovery is a minority departure.
