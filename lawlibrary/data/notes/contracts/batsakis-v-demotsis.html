Quick reference

Citation: 226 S.W.2d 673 (Tex. App. 1949) (Knapp's cite) · Casebook: Knapp p. 121 (Note 2 after Harris v. Time) · Topic: Consideration One line: A wartime promise to repay $2,000 plus 8% for 500,000 drachmae (worth about $25) was enforced. Courts do not inquire into the adequacy of consideration.

Coverage

Summarized in Knapp's Harris Note 2. The opinion is not reprinted. Slides name it only as the "gross inadequacy" contrast to Harris.

Overview

The leading example of the rule that mere inadequacy of consideration does not void a contract, absent fraud, duress or similar defects.

Parties

  • Plaintiff: Mr. Batsakis. The lender, who wanted repayment of $2,000 plus 8% interest.
  • Defendant: Ms. Demotsis. The borrower, who argued the drachmae she received were worth only about $25, so she owed at most that.

Facts

  • During World War II (in Greece, [Outside knowledge]), Demotsis borrowed 500,000 drachmae from Batsakis. In exchange she gave her written promise to repay US$2,000 plus 8% interest.
  • She wanted money "for the support of my family during these difficult days and because it is impossible for me to transfer dollars of my own from America."
  • After the war, Batsakis sued in Texas. She claimed the drachmae were worth only about US$25.

Procedural History

From Knapp: the Texas Court of Appeals rejected her pleas. [Outside knowledge: the trial court entered judgment for the plaintiff for $750 plus interest (on the jury's finding), and the appellate court affirmed.]

Issue

Is a promise to repay $2,000 unenforceable (or reducible) because the consideration received, 500,000 drachmae, was worth far less?

Holding

No. The pleas of "want of consideration," "failure of consideration" and "inadequacy of consideration" all failed. She received exactly what she bargained for, and mere inadequacy does not void the contract.

Reasoning

  • Want and failure of consideration did not apply: she got the 500,000 drachmae, "exactly what she bargained for."
  • Inadequacy: the court held that "mere inadequacy of consideration" will not void a contract (Knapp's quotation). Courts leave the parties to set their own terms.
  • She had urgent need and few options, but those facts did not amount to fraud or duress.

Rule

Courts generally do not inquire into the adequacy of consideration. If there is a bargain, a gross disparity in value does not by itself make the promise unenforceable. It may be evidence of fraud, duress, mistake, incapacity or unconscionability.

Why It Matters

  • It is the classical baseline for freedom of contract and the bargain theory (Knapp: "the court's role [is] limited to confirming that a bargain had in fact been struck").
  • It is the contrast case for Harris v. Time (a trivial but real bargain) and a bridge to later policing doctrines such as Unconscionability and Duress and Undue Influence.
  • Exam angle: if facts show a desperate party and a lopsided exchange, note that inadequacy alone fails, then pivot to duress or unconscionability.

Other Key Points

  • [Outside knowledge]: the jury actually awarded much less than $2,000 (recalled as $750 plus interest), which the appellate court did not disturb because the plaintiff did not appeal. Often used to illustrate that juries may find ways to soften harsh bargains.
