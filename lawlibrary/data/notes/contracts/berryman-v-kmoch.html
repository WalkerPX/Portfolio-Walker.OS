Quick reference

Citation: 221 Kan. 304, 559 P.2d 790 (1977) · Casebook: Knapp p. 254 · Topic: Option Contracts and Firm Offers One line: A land option reciting "$10 and other valuable consideration" was not binding because the $10 was never paid. The option was a revocable offer, revoked when the optionee learned the land had been sold.

Overview

The course case on option contracts and consideration. It rejects the Restatement's § 87(1)(a) rule that a signed option reciting a purported consideration is binding. Consideration must actually be given. It also applies indirect revocation and rejects promissory estoppel on these facts.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Wade Berryman. Landowner of 960 acres in Stanton County, Kansas. He wanted a declaration that the option was void.
  • Defendant / appellant: Norbert H. Kmoch. A Colorado real estate broker and the optionee. He counterclaimed for damages for Berryman's failure to convey.

Facts

  • June 19, 1973: Berryman signed an option: "For $10.00 and other valuable consideration, I hereby grant unto you or your assigns an option for 120 days after date to purchase" 960 acres, with the price, crops, water rights and irrigation equipment.
  • The $10 was never paid, and both parties knew it (slides).
  • Kmoch spent time and money seeking investors to join the purchase.
  • July 1973: Berryman told Kmoch by phone he no longer wanted to be bound. August 1973: a Federal Land Bank representative told Kmoch that Berryman had disposed of the land. October: Kmoch tried to exercise the option.

Procedural History

  • Declaratory judgment action. On cross-motions, the trial court granted summary judgment for Berryman: the option lacked consideration, was a revocable offer, and was withdrawn in July 1973 before exercise.
  • Affirmed.

Issue

  1. Is an option supported by consideration when it recites $10 that was never paid?
  2. Is the option enforceable through promissory estoppel based on Kmoch's efforts to find investors?
  3. Was the offer revoked before Kmoch tried to accept?

Holding

  1. No. Without actual consideration, the "option" was only an offer, revocable at will.
  2. No. Promissory estoppel does not apply. Kmoch's reliance could not reasonably have been expected by Berryman and was not shown to be the kind that makes enforcement necessary.
  3. Yes. The offer was revoked. Kmoch's power of acceptance ended when he learned reliable information that Berryman had sold the land (Restatement (Second) § 42/43, indirect revocation), so the October exercise came too late.

Reasoning

  • Option contracts require consideration to make the offer irrevocable. A recital of $10 that both parties knew was not paid does not suffice. The court declined to imply a promise to pay or to follow the Restatement's "purported consideration" rule.
  • Other valuable consideration: Kmoch's efforts to find investors were for his own benefit, not bargained for by Berryman. The slides' critique is that a better analysis says those efforts were not the "price" of the option.
  • Promissory estoppel: the promisor must reasonably expect the reliance, and the reliance must be substantial. Here Berryman had no reason to expect Kmoch's investor search, and refusing enforcement caused no injustice.
  • Revocation: an offeree's power ends when the offeror takes definite action inconsistent with the contract and the offeree acquires reliable information of it.

Rule

An option is irrevocable only if supported by consideration actually given (in Kansas, and under the majority view the court followed). A mere recital of unpaid nominal consideration does not create a binding option, contrary to Restatement (Second) § 87(1)(a). Without consideration, the option is a revocable offer, which can be revoked directly or indirectly.

Why It Matters

  • It frames the nominal consideration debate: courts are generally more lenient about nominal consideration for keeping an offer open than for a substantial exchange. Some courts imply a promise to pay a recited sum. Berryman rejects this (slides).
  • It contrasts with § 87(1)(a): a signed writing reciting a purported consideration, proposing an exchange on fair terms within a reasonable time, is binding as an option.
  • It adds § 87(2) for reliance on an offer (compare Drennan v. Star Paving).

Other Key Points

  • Note 2: nominal consideration works if actually paid. Note 3: it need not be money.
  • Note 5: sealed options may be irrevocable in some states. Merchants' firm offers under UCC § 2-205 need no consideration.
  • Note 6: the mailbox rule generally does not apply to accepting an option. Acceptance is effective on receipt.
  • Notes 7-8: indirect revocation. Compare Normile v. Miller.
  • Hypo 1 (slides): a $1 option never paid, buyer spent $15,000 on plans, seller revokes on day 25, buyer accepts on day 28. Analyze under Berryman, § 87(1)(a) and § 87(2).
