In one paragraph

When expectation damages can't be proved or aren't the right measure, courts award reliance or restitution damages. Reliance damages put the plaintiff where it would have been had the promise never been made: its out-of-pocket losses from reasonable reliance. They are the fallback when expected profits are uncertain (Wartzman v. Hightower Productions) and the usual remedy in promissory estoppel, "limited as justice requires" (Walser v. Toyota Motor Sales). Restitution makes the defendant give back the benefit it received. A non-breaching party may get quantum meruit even on a losing contract (US ex rel Coastal Steel v. Algernon Blair). Even a breaching party may get restitution of benefits conferred minus the harm it caused (§ 374; Lancellotti v. Thomas). But restitution is not available where an express contract governs the issue (Shaffer v. George Washington University).

Reliance damages

  • Goal: the position the plaintiff would have been in had the promise not been made. They compensate expenses of preparation and performance (Restatement § 349 [Outside knowledge: section number]).
  • When used:
    1. Expectation can't be proved with reasonable certainty (a new business, speculative profits).
    2. Promissory estoppel claims (Promissory Estoppel › Remedy).
    3. The plaintiff chooses reliance.
  • Same limits as expectation (Wartzman Note 3): foreseeability, causation, mitigation, certainty (Limits on Damages).
  • Losing-contract cap [Outside knowledge]: under § 349, the defendant may reduce reliance damages by proving the plaintiff would have lost money on full performance.
  • Wartzman v. Hightower Productions: Hightower's promoters planned a publicity stunt (a flagpole sitter trying to set a record [Outside knowledge]) and hired a law firm to incorporate and raise money. The lawyers failed to advise that Maryland blue sky laws required securities registration. When this surfaced, the venture lacked cash to fix it and was abandoned. Held: reliance damages of substantially all the money invested were recoverable, since lost profits were too speculative. The jury was properly instructed on causation, the loss was foreseeable, there was no failure to mitigate, and prejudgment interest was properly denied. [Unverified] dollar amounts in the brief.
  • Walser v. Toyota Motor Sales: Walser was mistakenly told he had been approved for a Lexus dealership, and his father bought land for it. On a promissory estoppel claim, the jury awarded out-of-pocket reliance damages only. Lost profits denied: the deal was "far from a certainty," negotiations were preliminary, Toyota corrected the mistake within days, and Walser showed no lost opportunity. Courts split on the right remedy. The Restatement's flexible "as justice requires" approach is the trend (Notes 1, 3, 4).
  • Compare Pops Cones v. Resorts International (reliance damages for promissory estoppel in negotiations).

Restitution as a remedy for breach

  • Goal: prevent unjust enrichment by making the breaching party give back the value received (Restatement § 373 [Outside knowledge: section number]).
  • Measure: the reasonable value of the services (quantum meruit), or the increase in the defendant's wealth. In commercial cases, use market value.
  • Losing contracts: a non-breaching party may elect restitution even if it would have lost money on the contract. Restitution is not capped by the contract price (the majority view [Outside knowledge]), except where the plaintiff has fully performed and only money is owed.
  • US ex rel Coastal Steel v. Algernon Blair: a subcontractor (Coastal Steel) stopped work after the general contractor (Algernon Blair) refused to pay for crane rental, a material breach. Coastal had completed about 28% of the work. Held: Coastal may recover in quantum meruit the reasonable value of its labor and equipment (about $37,000 owed), even if it would have lost money on full performance.
  • See also the underlying doctrine: Restitution and Promissory Restitution.

Restitution for the breaching party

  • Traditional rule: a party in willful breach can recover nothing, which can mean a forfeiture.
  • Restatement § 374: a breaching party may recover the value of the benefit it conferred that exceeds the harm its breach caused (unless the contract says otherwise, for example a valid liquidated damages clause).
  • Lancellotti v. Thomas: Lancellotti bought a luncheonette business for $25,000 (paid), agreed to build a 20' × 40' addition and lease the premises, then abandoned the business. The sellers counterclaimed about $52,000 ($6,635 in rent plus about $45,335 in damages), and they built the addition themselves for about $11,000. Held: a breaching buyer may recover restitution of payments in excess of the seller's actual damages (§ 374). The seller bears the burden of proving its damages. Remanded.
  • Compare Sackett v. Spindler (breaching buyer's payments offset by the seller's damages) and the UCC rule for buyers' deposits (§ 2-718 [Outside knowledge: content]).
  • Construction cases [Outside knowledge]: a builder who breaches can often recover the value of its work minus the owner's damages. Compare Jacob and Youngs v. Kent (substantial performance).

Restitution when the contract doesn't cover the issue

  • Rule: restitution (unjust enrichment) is not available where an express contract governs the same subject. The contract allocates the risk.
  • Shaffer v. George Washington University: students sued their universities for tuition and fees after the COVID-19 shutdown moved classes online. Held (D.C. Cir.): (1) no express contract term promised in-person instruction; (2) the implied-in-fact contract claim survives (university communications and the tuition differential plausibly showed a promise of in-person instruction, and the reservation-of-rights clauses did not, as a matter of law, put pandemic risk on students); (3) the restitution / unjust enrichment claim survives, because no express term covered the issue. Remanded, including on whether the obligations were discharged by impracticability.
  • Damages illustration (slides): tuition and non-tuition services are discharged by impracticability. The refundable non-tuition share = (days from the closure order to the end of the semester) ÷ (days in the semester).

Exam framework

  1. Which interest fits? Expectation (default), reliance (expectation is uncertain; promissory estoppel), or restitution (benefit conferred; losing contract; breaching party).
  2. Reliance: list the out-of-pocket expenses; apply foreseeability, causation, mitigation and certainty; consider the losing-contract offset.
  3. Restitution for the non-breacher: reasonable value; not capped by the contract price (Algernon Blair) unless only money is owed.
  4. Restitution for the breacher: § 374, benefit minus harm (Lancellotti); burden on the non-breacher to prove damages.
  5. Is there an express contract on point? Then no unjust enrichment (Shaffer).
  6. Promissory estoppel: remedy limited as justice requires (Walser).

Hypos (slides)

  • Tony / Cornelius: Tony promises Cornelius $10,000 for a car. Cornelius relies and spends $9,000, then Tony reneges. Reliance damages = $9,000.
  • Delivery date: a customer plans around a promised delivery date that is missed. Reliance damages restore the pre-reliance position.
  • Employment: a promised salary and benefits; the employee acts in reliance; the employer reneges. Reliance damages (or expectation, if the contract is enforceable).
