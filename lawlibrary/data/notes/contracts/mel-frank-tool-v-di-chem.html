Quick reference

Citation: 580 N.W.2d 802 (Iowa 1998) · Casebook: Knapp p. 781 · Topic: Impracticability and Frustration › Frustration of purpose One line: A chemical distributor that could no longer store hazardous chemicals in its leased warehouse after a new ordinance was not excused from the lease. It still had serviceable uses, so the lease was not "virtually worthless."

Overview

A course case on frustration of purpose caused by governmental action. Frustration requires that the contract be rendered virtually worthless, not merely less useful. It also shows how a force majeure clause is interpreted.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Mel Frank Tool & Supply, Inc., the lessor of warehouse space. It wanted unpaid rent and damages to the premises.
  • Defendant / appellant: Di-Chem Co., a chemical distributor and tenant. It argued the ordinance excused it (impossibility or frustration) and that a lease clause released it.

Facts

  • Di-Chem leased warehouse space, principally to store hazardous chemicals for its distribution business. [Outside knowledge: a three-year lease beginning in 1994.]
  • After signing, a city ordinance prohibited storing hazardous chemicals in that area, and city authorities told Di-Chem it could no longer do so there.
  • Di-Chem vacated. Mel Frank sued for breach (rent) and for damage to the premises.

Procedural History

  • The district court awarded the lessor judgment for unpaid rent and damages to the premises.
  • Iowa Supreme Court: affirmed.

Issue

Did the ordinance excuse Di-Chem's lease obligations under impossibility or frustration of purpose, or under the lease's release language?

Holding

No. Di-Chem's frustration claim fails because it retained a serviceable use of the premises. The lease's force majeure language did not apply.

Reasoning

  • Frustration (Restatement § 265) requires that the principal purpose be substantially frustrated. "The contract must be rendered virtually worthless to warrant relief based on the frustration doctrine."
  • Di-Chem could still store non-hazardous materials and conduct other business there. The lease did not restrict use to hazardous chemicals. The ordinance reduced value but did not destroy it.
  • Impossibility: performance (paying rent and using the premises) was not impossible.
  • Force majeure clause (Note 2): the lease contained a clause, but properly construed it did not cover these facts.

Rule

Frustration of purpose excuses performance only if a supervening event (including government action) substantially frustrates the principal purpose, leaving the contract virtually worthless to the party. A reduction in value or usefulness is not enough where a serviceable use remains.

Why It Matters

  • Note 1: governmental-action claims tend to fare better than market-change claims (compare Hemlock Semiconductor v. Solarworld), but the frustration must still be substantial. Compare Lloyd v. Murphy: wartime limits on new car sales did not excuse a car dealer's lease, because the premises had other commercial uses.
  • Courts also ask whether the non-occurrence of the government action was a basic assumption and whether it was foreseeable enough that the claimant assumed the risk.
  • Note 2, drafting: a force majeure clause could have defined the "principal purpose" and granted relief if government action prevented it.

Other Key Points

  • Comparison (slides): if Di-Chem's only possible use were prohibited, the result might differ.
  • Hypo 2, "Flower Shop" (slides): a 5-year lease beside a hospital that announces closure, and the landlord may have known. Is it frustration? Is the remedy rescission or damages?
  • COVID-19 (slides): courts generally denied relief.
