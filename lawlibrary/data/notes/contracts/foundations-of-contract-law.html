In one paragraph

A contract is "an agreement between two or more persons ... which the law will enforce" (slides). Contract law comes from judicial opinions (common law), statutes (especially UCC Article 2 for sales of goods), and persuasive secondary sources such as the Restatements. Throughout the course, ask both is there a contract? and should the law enforce this promise?, and evaluate answers through competing theories (formalist, realist, economic, relational, critical).

What is a contract?

  • Definition (slides): an agreement between two or more persons that the law will enforce. Some contracts must also be in writing (Statute of Frauds).
  • Traditional formation formula: "a bargain in which there is a manifestation of mutual assent to the exchange and a consideration" (Restatement (Second) § 17(1)). So the two core elements are mutual assent (Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance) and consideration (Consideration).
  • Alternative bases of enforcement: Promissory Estoppel (reliance) and restitution (unjust enrichment). These matter when bargain elements are missing.
  • Futurity: contracts usually involve commitments about future performance.

Choosing the governing law: goods → UCC Article 2 (with common law filling gaps); services, land, employment → common law. Mixed contracts use the predominant purpose test (UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation).

Theories of contract law (slides)

  • Formalists (classical): clear rules, objective assent, freedom of contract, limited judicial role (e.g., Walker v. Keith, Thompson v. Libby).
  • Realists: law as it actually operates. Courts reach fair results and justify them afterward.
  • Economic efficiency: enforce promises to maximize joint welfare. Default rules mimic what parties would have bargained for (e.g., Posner in Credit Bureau Enterprises v. Pelo Note 5 and Zapata Hermanos v. Hearthside Baking).
  • Relational: contracts embedded in ongoing relationships. Norms of cooperation and flexibility (Wood v. Lucy Lady Duff-Gordon, Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing).
  • Critical: legal rules are not neutral. Repeat players (banks, insurers, large sellers) shape rules and forms (Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute, Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture).

Problem 1-1: contract or not? (slides)

The Week 1 problems test intuitions about what promises the law enforces:

  1. Stock sale. 2. Union / employment promise. 3. Dry-cleaner ticket (a standard form). 4. Facebook terms of service (clickwrap). 5. Family dinner (social promise; but note the UCC § 2-314 implied warranty if food is sold). 6. A promise to leave something in a will. 7. A ski trip among friends. That one is a close call: social setting vs. detailed plans and financial commitments.

Factors pulling toward enforcement: a commercial setting, detail and definiteness, a writing, reliance or financial commitment, exchange. Factors pulling away: social or family context, vagueness, no expectation of legal consequences.

Problem 1-2: Carnival Cruise (standard forms)

See Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute. It introduces adhesion contracts, forum-selection clauses, and the overlap of contracts, civil procedure and torts.

Remedies vocabulary (Week 2 preview)

  • Expectation: put the injured party where full performance would have (Expectation Damages).
  • Reliance: put the injured party back where it started, before the promise (Reliance and Restitution Damages).
  • Restitution: return any benefit conferred on the breaching party.

Exam approach

  1. Which law governs (UCC or common law)?
  2. Was a contract formed (assent and consideration)? If not, promissory estoppel or restitution?
  3. Are there defenses (statute of frauds, capacity, duress, misrepresentation, unconscionability, mistake, changed circumstances)?
  4. What are the terms (parol evidence, interpretation, implied terms, good faith)?
  5. Was there breach (conditions, material breach, repudiation)?
  6. What remedies?
