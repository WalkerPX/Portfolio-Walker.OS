Quick reference

Citation: 175 Ariz. 148, 854 P.2d 1134 (1993) · Casebook: Knapp p. 440 · Topics: Parol Evidence Rule › Modern (Corbin and Restatement) approach, Interpretation One line: Arizona adopts the Corbin approach. The judge considers extrinsic evidence first to decide whether contract language is reasonably susceptible to the proffered meaning, rejecting the "plain meaning" rule. Evidence about a release was properly admitted.

Overview

The course case adopting the modern, Corbin approach to the parol evidence rule and interpretation. Courts may consider extrinsic evidence to decide whether a writing is reasonably susceptible to a party's interpretation, rather than first finding ambiguity within the "four corners."

Parties

  • Plaintiff / petitioner: Bobby Sid Taylor. An insured who was hit with excess verdicts (about $2.5 million over his limits) after State Farm failed to settle claims against him. He wanted damages for State Farm's bad faith.
  • Defendant / respondent: State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co. Taylor's insurer. It argued that a general release Taylor signed in 1981 in exchange for $15,000 in uninsured motorist benefits barred the bad faith claim.

Facts

  • A three-vehicle accident injured Ring, her passenger Rivers, and Taylor. Ring and Rivers sued Taylor (and Wistrom). State Farm defended Taylor. Taylor's personal lawyer (Randall) counterclaimed for Taylor.
  • Others settled, leaving Taylor alone exposed. The Rings and Rivers won verdicts of about $2.5 million over Taylor's policy limits.
  • 1981: Taylor signed a release, drafted by his own attorney, in exchange for $15,000 in uninsured motorist benefits from State Farm. Its broad language released State Farm from claims arising from the policy.
  • Taylor later sued State Farm for bad faith failure to settle the Rivers claim within policy limits.

Procedural History

  • Trial court: both summary judgment motions were denied. The release was ambiguous, so parol evidence was admissible. The jury returned $2.1 million in compensatory damages for Taylor, plus $300,000 in attorney fees.
  • Court of appeals: reversed. The release was unambiguous, so parol evidence could not be used to vary it.
  • Arizona Supreme Court: granted review, held the trial court properly admitted extrinsic evidence, and vacated the court of appeals' opinion. [Outside knowledge: it remanded to the court of appeals for other issues.]

Issue

May a court consider extrinsic evidence to interpret a release that appears unambiguous on its face, and did the release bar Taylor's bad faith claim?

Holding

Yes. Under the Corbin approach, the judge first considers the offered evidence. If the language is reasonably susceptible to the proffered interpretation, the evidence is admissible to determine meaning. The trial court properly admitted extrinsic evidence about whether the release was intended to cover the bad faith claim.

Reasoning

  • Rejects "plain meaning": words do not have fixed meanings divorced from context. A judge cannot decide whether language is "clear" without considering the circumstances.
  • The Corbin two-step (slides):
    1. Examine all the evidence to determine the parties' intent and the extent of integration.
    2. Apply the parol evidence rule to exclude evidence that varies or contradicts the written terms (as opposed to interpreting them).
  • Interpretation vs. contradiction: extrinsic evidence may be used to interpret the writing (show what the parties meant) but not to contradict it. If the asserted meaning is one the language cannot reasonably bear, the evidence is excluded.
  • Here, the release could reasonably be read as covering only contract claims under the policy, not a later bad faith tort claim. With a bad faith claim worth up to $2.1 million, it is hard to believe Taylor meant to release it for $15,000.

Rule

In interpreting a contract, the court may consider extrinsic evidence of the circumstances and the parties' intent to determine whether the language is reasonably susceptible to a proffered meaning. If it is, the evidence is admissible to interpret the writing. The parol evidence rule still bars evidence offered to vary or contradict an integrated writing.

Why It Matters

  • It is the leading illustration of contextualism vs. textualism in contract interpretation.
  • Note 1: Thompson involved supplementing. Taylor involves interpreting.
  • Note 2, drafting lesson: a specific exclusion of the bad faith claim would have protected Taylor. A specific inclusion would have protected State Farm.
  • Note 4: "ambiguous" and "reasonably susceptible to different meanings" are essentially the same. Courts that purport to distinguish them create confusion.
  • Note 5: patent ambiguity (apparent on the face) vs. latent ambiguity (revealed by extrinsic evidence). Textualist courts consider only patent ambiguity. Contextualist courts consider both.
  • Note 6: Shay v. Aldrich, showing the continuing textualist and contextualist debate.
