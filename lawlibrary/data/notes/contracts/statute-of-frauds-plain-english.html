The short version

A few kinds of contracts, such as land sales, deals that can't be finished within a year, and sales of goods of $500 or more, must be backed by a signed writing. Courts have several exceptions so the rule isn't used to cheat.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Statute of Frauds.

What it's about

Most contracts can be oral. The statute of frauds is an old rule (from 1677 England) saying certain contracts can't be enforced in court unless there's a writing signed by the person being sued. The idea is to prevent people from inventing deals that never happened.

Which contracts need a writing

A common memory aid is "MY LEGS":

  • Marriage (promises made in exchange for marriage)
  • Year: contracts that cannot possibly be completed within one year
  • Land: sales of land or interests in land
  • Executor promises to pay the dead person's debts personally
  • Goods priced at $500 or more (UCC § 2-201)
  • Suretyship: promises to pay someone else's debt

What counts as "the writing"

It doesn't need to be a formal contract. Notes, letters, emails or several papers pieced together can do it, as long as together they show the essential terms and are signed by the party being held to them.

Ways around it (exceptions)

  • Land, part performance: if the buyer moved in, paid and made improvements in ways that clearly point to a deal, the oral contract can be enforced.
  • Reliance (Restatement § 139): if someone reasonably relied on the promise and injustice can only be avoided by enforcing it.
  • Goods (UCC): the writing rule doesn't block enforcement when goods were paid for and accepted, or received and accepted; when the seller specially manufactured them; when the party admits the deal in court; or when a merchant didn't object within 10 days to the other merchant's written confirmation.

An everyday example

You and a friend shake hands on selling your house for $250,000. With no signed writing, neither of you can force the other to go through with it. If the buyer had moved in, paid part of the price and built a deck with your OK, a court might enforce it anyway.

The cases, in plain words

  • Crabtree v. Elizabeth Arden: payroll cards and a memo, read together, were a good enough writing for a two-year job.
  • Beaver v. Brumlow: buyers who moved onto land and spent about $85,000 improving it could enforce an oral sale.
  • Burns v. McCormick: the strict older test. Acts of part performance must point unmistakably to the oral deal.
  • Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice: a two-year job promise was enforced through reliance because the employee moved to Alaska.
  • Buffaloe v. Hart: a buyer's check for tobacco barns counted as "payment accepted," so the oral sale was enforceable.
