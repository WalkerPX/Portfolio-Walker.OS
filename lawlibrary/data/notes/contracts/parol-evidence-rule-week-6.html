Big picture

  • PER gives certainty to careful, formal written agreements. It also protects against perjury. (slide 36)
  • Core idea: a final writing blocks contradiction by prior or contemporaneous oral/prior agreements. It may still be explained or supplemented in limited ways. (slides 37–39)
  • Two integration levels: partially integrated (supplementable by consistent additional terms) vs. fully integrated ("final, complete and exclusive" — not supplementable). (slides 38–39)
  • Course of performance, course of dealing, and trade usage are admissible for BOTH partially and fully integrated writings unless carefully cancelled. (slides 38–39)
  • Classical (Thompson) vs. modern Corbin (Taylor) split: four corners vs. examine extrinsic evidence first. Debate is unsettled. (slides 46–52)

Doctrine outline

  • § 2-202 rule: terms in a final writing may not be contradicted by prior agreement or contemporaneous oral agreement. They may be explained or supplemented by:
    • (a) course of performance, course of dealing, usage of trade (§ 1-303); and
    • (b) consistent additional terms unless the court finds the writing was intended as a complete and exclusive statement. (slide 37)
  • Partially integrated: no contradiction; consistent additional terms allowed. (slide 38)
  • Fully integrated: no contradiction; no consistent additional terms. (slide 38)
  • All final writings (partial or full): explainable/supplementable by course of performance, course of dealing, trade usage. (slide 38)
  • Exclusion trigger: extrinsic evidence is excluded if it (1) contradicts the final writing AND (2) is in a prior agreement or contemporaneous oral agreement. (slide 39)
  • "Consistent additional term" = one that "might naturally be omitted from the writing." (slide 39)
  • Fraud/duress: PER does not bar evidence to prove fraud or duress, e.g., intentional misrepresentation of an aspect of the deal. (slide 45)
  • Consistent additional terms (Restatement-style):
    • (1) admissible to supplement an integrated agreement unless completely integrated.
    • (2) NOT completely integrated if the writing omits a consistent additional term that is (a) agreed to for separate consideration, OR (b) such a term as in the circumstances might naturally be omitted. (slide 53)
  • Consistency determination: interpret the writing in light of all circumstances, including evidence of the additional term. Meaning includes terms fairly implied as part of the bargain in fact. (slide 54)
  • § 1-303 definitions:
    • Course of performance: sequence of conduct in this transaction with repeated occasions for performance. Most helpful — closest to immediate expectations.
    • Course of dealing: sequence of conduct in previous transactions between the same parties, establishing a common basis for interpretation.
    • Trade usage: practice/method of dealing with such regularity of observance in a trade as to justify an expectation it will be observed. (slide 44)

Tests and elements

PER Steps (slide 41) — checklist:

  • Step 1: Is it a final writing? If so, partially or fully integrated?
  • Step 2: Classify the evidence:
    • Contradicts final terms → INADMISSIBLE.
    • Supplementary or explanatory → ADMISSIBLE unless fully integrated.

Integration factors — multi-factored approach (slide 40) — checklist:

  • Both parties represented by counsel?
  • Sophistication of the transaction?
  • Price of the goods?
  • Time available to comport writing to negotiations?
  • Presence/absence of merger or integration clause?
  • NOTE: merger clause alone is NOT dispositive — one factor, not a bright line.

Classical approach (Thompson) (slides 46–47) — checklist:

  • Writing appears complete on its face → treated as full integration.
  • Oral evidence of additional terms → inadmissible.
  • Full integration if merger clause OR appears complete on face ("four corners").
  • Rests on skepticism of juries and desire for certainty.

Modern / Corbin approach (Taylor) (slides 49–50) — checklist:

  • Step 1: Examine ALL evidence to determine parties' intent and extent of integration.
  • Step 2: Apply PER to exclude extrinsic evidence that varies or contradicts the writing.
  • Key difference: courts examine extrinsic evidence FIRST to determine integration — not just four corners.
  • Merger clause NOT conclusive.

Consistent additional term test (slide 53) — checklist:

  • Agreed to for separate consideration? OR
  • Might naturally be omitted from the writing?

Cases

  • Thompson v. Libby — classical approach. Writing appearing complete on its face = full integration; oral evidence of additional terms inadmissible. Excluded oral evidence about alleged warranty. (slides 46, 56) Holding detail beyond this not stated — now briefed: Thompson v. Libby.
  • Taylor v. State Farm — modern Corbin approach. Issue: whether Taylor's bad faith claim (potentially $2.1 million) is barred by a general release signed for $15,000 in uninsured motorist benefits. Court held trial court properly admitted extrinsic evidence to determine intent when signing the release; rejected the lower court's "plain meaning" approach. (slides 48–49, 57–58)
  • Shay v. Aldrich — named only (Note 6, new to 10th ed.). Dissent (28 pages): "plain meaning" should trump intent (textualist). Majority: intent trumps "plain meaning" (contextualist). Illustrates irreconcilable policy positions. (slide 52) Holding detail beyond this not stated — now briefed: Shay v. Aldrich.
  • Sherrodd, Inc. v. Morrison-Knudsen Co. — restrictive fraud exception. Plaintiff allegedly defrauded into fixed-sum $97,500 earthwork contract based on representations parties would work out a deal for actual work; actual earthwork was twice the contract amount. Court prohibited evidence of fraud that directly contradicts the terms of the writing. Dissent: approach lets a party make explicit misrepresentations yet be shielded if the other side signs a writing stating no representations were made — deeply troubling. (slides 59–60)

Examples and hypos

  • Nancy/Eli (slides 42–43): Eli said delivery within 24 hours during negotiations; May 1 contract states delivery May 10. Nancy sues May 2, seeks to introduce oral 24-hour conversation. Result: writing presumably final and partially integrated; evidence of May 2 delivery CONTRADICTS May 10 term → INADMISSIBLE.
  • A owes B $1,000 (slide 55): Written agreement for Blackacre at $3,000 omits oral agreement that the $1,000 debt is credited against price. Result: writing NOT completely integrated — oral credit agreement admissible to supplement.
  • Services-for-Blackacre (slide 55): Written agreement for Blackacre at $3,000; claimed oral understanding that B could pay by rendering services at $50/hour. Result: oral understanding admissible UNLESS the writing was completely integrated.
  • Hypo 4 — "The Release That Swallowed Everything" (slide 61): Dana gets $20,000 in uninsured motorist benefits, signs general release of "all claims" against SafeGuard. Later discovers bad faith refusal to settle — claim worth $1.5 million. Questions: Does PER bar extrinsic evidence of intent behind "all claims"? Apply classical (Thompson) — result? Apply Corbin (Taylor) — result? How should the release have been drafted to protect Dana? To protect SafeGuard?
  • Hypo 5 — "The Earthwork Contract" (slide 62): BuildCo signs fixed-price $150,000 earthwork contract. Before signing, project manager said: "Don't worry about the fixed price — if there's more work than expected, we'll work it out." Actual cost $280,000. Contract clause: "This agreement represents the entire understanding of the parties. No oral representations have been made." Questions: Does PER bar the oral promise evidence? Does the fraud exception help under Sherrodd? Does the merger clause make it fully integrated? How does the Sherrodd dissent view this, and is it persuasive?

Exam angles

  • Always run the two-step: integration level first, then classify the evidence (contradicts vs. supplements/explains). (slide 41)
  • Contradiction = automatic exclusion, regardless of integration level. (slides 39, 41)
  • Fully integrated blocks consistent additional terms but NEVER blocks course of performance, course of dealing, or trade usage. (slides 38–39)
  • Merger clause: one factor only — not dispositive. (slide 40)
  • Fraud/duress evidence is not barred — but watch Sherrodd's restrictive reading: fraud evidence that directly contradicts the writing is excluded. (slides 45, 60)
  • Distinguish supplementation (Thompson) from interpretation (Taylor) — the rule applies more persuasively to supplementation. (slide 51, Note 1)
  • "Ambiguous" and "reasonably susceptible to different meanings" are essentially the same; distinguishing them creates confusion. (slide 51, Note 4)
  • Patent ambiguity (on face) vs. latent ambiguity (revealed by extrinsic evidence): textualist courts recognize only patent; contextualist courts recognize both. (slide 51, Note 5)
  • Know which approach your jurisdiction follows — textualism vs. contextualism debate is NOT settled. (slide 52)
  • Drafting lesson: specifically exclude (protects releasee) or specifically include (protects releaseor) contested claims. (slide 51, Note 2)
  • Policy tension: excluding extrinsic evidence risks injustice when the writing doesn't reflect actual intent; admitting it risks inconvenience (trial time/expense) and injustice (fabricated evidence). (slide 47, Note 1)
