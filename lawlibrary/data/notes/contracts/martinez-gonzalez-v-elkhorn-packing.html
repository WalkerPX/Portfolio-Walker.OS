Quick reference

Citation: 25 F.4th 613 (9th Cir. 2022) (amended opinion) · Casebook: Knapp p. 615 · Topic: Duress and Undue Influence One line: A divided Ninth Circuit held that an H-2A farm worker who signed arbitration agreements at an end-of-day orientation did not prove economic duress or undue influence under California law. The dissent stressed the employer's "coercive atmosphere."

Overview

A modern application of duress and undue influence to mandatory arbitration agreements imposed on migrant farm workers. It is a "hard case" in which judges reach different conclusions on largely uncontested facts.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Dario Martinez-Gonzalez. A lettuce harvester from Mexicali, Mexico, who worked three seasons on an H-2A visa. He sued for federal and state labor and wage violations and resisted arbitration.
  • Defendants / appellants: Elkhorn Packing Co. (a farm labor contractor) and D'Arrigo Brothers (a California grower) ("Elkhorn"). They moved to compel arbitration.

Facts

  • The Elkhorn job paid up to five times what he earned in Mexico. Elkhorn arranged his H-2A visa and transported him to Monterey County.
  • He signed arbitration agreements at an orientation held after he had started harvesting (slides: at the end of a long workday, in a hotel parking lot, in a language many did not fully understand, without adequate explanation). [The details vary by season; see the opinion.]
  • He later quit mid-season and sued. Elkhorn moved to compel arbitration.

Procedural History

  • The district court refused to enforce the arbitration agreements, holding he signed them under economic duress and undue influence.
  • Ninth Circuit: reversed and remanded (majority). One judge dissented.

Issue

Were the arbitration agreements voidable under California law for economic duress or undue influence?

Holding

No (majority). The elements of economic duress and undue influence under California law were not established. The agreements are enforceable.

Reasoning

  • Economic duress (California): requires a wrongful act that leaves the victim no reasonable alternative. Elkhorn did not create his financial pressures (poverty, the need for the job) by any wrongful act. Presenting an arbitration agreement as a condition of employment is not wrongful.
  • Undue influence: requires undue susceptibility plus excessive pressure. The majority found no excessive pressure. He was not told he would be fired or sent home for refusing, and the circumstances were not sufficiently coercive.
  • Dissent: the majority ignored the "coercive atmosphere" Elkhorn deliberately created: urging tired, hungry workers to sign unexplained documents at the end of a long day, far from home, with their livelihoods at stake.

Rule

Under California law, economic duress requires a wrongful act by the other party that leaves no reasonable alternative, and undue influence requires undue susceptibility combined with excessive pressure. Hard circumstances not caused by the defendant, and ordinary pressure to sign employment paperwork, do not satisfy those elements.

Why It Matters

  • Majority (formalistic): element-by-element analysis. Dissent (contextual): the overall coercive atmosphere (slides).
  • Key question (slides): could Elkhorn have done more before the workers were taken to California, for example a short explanation in Spanish of what they would sign? Yes, and that omission is significant.
  • Note 4: a genuinely "hard case" with strong arguments on both sides.
  • It connects to the arbitration and unconscionability cases (Higgins v. Superior Court) and to the FAA.

Other Key Points

  • Hypo 3, "Sign Here — It's Just Paperwork" (slides): workers bused 1,800 miles arrive at 9 p.m. and are handed "standard onboarding forms" including an arbitration waiver. Apply Totem (duress) and Odorizzi (undue influence), then consider unconscionability.
