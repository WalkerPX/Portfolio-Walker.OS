Quick reference

Citation: 909 So. 2d 1267 (Miss. Ct. App. 2005) · Casebook: Knapp p. 123 · Topic: Consideration One line: An at-will executive's continued service through a crisis was consideration for the company's promise of five years of post-employment pay if certain triggering events occurred. The promise was conditional, not illusory.

Overview

A modern case on illusory promises and mutuality. A conditional promise is not illusory. An at-will employee who keeps working (giving up the right to leave) supplies consideration by performance, under a unilateral-contract analysis.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Bill Baker. He joined the company in 1965 as a management trainee and rose to director, vice president and then president. He wanted the promised five years of compensation equal to his monthly salary.
  • Defendant / appellant: Marshall Durbin Food Corp., a poultry company about 80% owned by Marshall Durbin, Jr. It argued the agreement lacked consideration and mutuality.

Facts

  • Late 1990s: the company was in trouble. Grain prices soared, poultry prices fell, and it lost about $30 million. There was family conflict: Durbin's daughters (who controlled about 18%) were removed as co-presidents.
  • Key employees, including Baker, worried about their futures "if anything ever happened to Mr. Durbin." A company witness described "chaos" and said Baker "was put in the breach to help save the company."
  • November 15, 1999: Durbin and Baker signed an "agreement of termination and/or early retirement." It listed circumstances that would trigger an "effective date," after which Baker would receive specified compensation for five years. Neither side committed to any fixed term of employment.
  • Baker stayed and worked through the crisis, later serving as president. A triggering event occurred in 2001, and the company refused to pay. [The details of the triggering event are not in the excerpt.]

Procedural History

  • The Chancery Court of Wayne County held the contract valid and ordered payment from an effective date of August 14, 2001.
  • Court of Appeals: affirmed on validity; reversed and rendered on the effective date, which it moved to July 10, 2001, when Baker began serving as president.

Issue

Is an employer's promise of post-employment compensation, conditioned on future triggering events and given to an at-will employee who promised nothing in return, supported by consideration, or is it illusory or lacking mutuality?

Holding

Enforceable. The employer's promise was not illusory even though conditional. Baker supplied consideration by performance: continuing to work, which the employer bargained for and received.

Reasoning

  • Unilateral contract analysis: the company promised compensation in exchange for Baker's continued service, not a return promise. Baker accepted by performing.
  • Benefit and detriment: the company "enjoyed the benefits of retaining a valued employee" during a difficult period. Baker gave up his right to leave at any time. That is legal detriment, as in Hamer v. Sidway.
  • Not illusory: an illusory promise leaves performance entirely optional. The company's promise bound it once a trigger occurred.
  • Mutuality: strict mutuality of obligation is not required where consideration is otherwise present (Restatement (Second) § 79(c)).

Rule

A promise conditioned on future events is not illusory if the promisor is bound when the condition occurs. An at-will employee's continued employment, bargained for by the employer, can be consideration for the employer's promise. Strict mutuality of obligation is not required (Restatement (Second) § 79(c)).

Why It Matters

  • It distinguishes conditional promises from illusory ones.
  • It reinforces that performance can be consideration (unilateral contracts), linking Cook v. Coldwell Banker and Sateriale v. RJ Reynolds.
  • It shows how employment-retention promises (bonuses, severance) are analyzed.

Other Key Points

  • Note 2: legal detriment mirrors Hamer v. Sidway. Baker gave up his right to leave.
  • Note 3: an illusory promise "promises nothing." This conditional promise was not illusory.
  • Note 4: Restatement (Second) § 79(c) rejects a general mutuality requirement, but some courts still use mutuality to police illusory promises.
  • Darius / Apex hypo (slides): a $50,000 retention bonus, with a twist that payment is in the employer's "sole discretion." Sole discretion may make the promise illusory, unless an implied duty of good faith limits it (see Wood v. Lucy Lady Duff-Gordon).
