Quick reference

Citation: 201 Md. 115, 93 A.2d 272 (Md. 1952) · Casebook: Knapp p. 25 · Topic: Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance One line: A builder who read and signed a contract incorporating the owners' specifications is bound by them, even if it subjectively thought its own specifications applied. The test for assent is objective.

Overview

The leading course case on the objective theory of contract. The court refuses to let a party escape a signed writing by testifying that it secretly understood the deal differently. Absent fraud, duress or mutual mistake, someone with the capacity to understand a document who reads and signs it (or signs without reading) is bound by it.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs / appellants: Calvin T. Ray and Katherine S. J. Ray. Owners of an unimproved lot on Dance Mill Road, Baltimore County. Mr. Ray was an aeronautical engineer, meticulous about detail. They wanted a house built strictly to their architect's plans and their own specifications, for a fixed price.
  • Defendant / appellee: William G. Eurice & Bros., Inc. A small home-building company run by brothers John (president) and Henry (secretary) Eurice, who had built about twenty houses a year for fifteen years. They wanted to build in their usual way, to their own looser three-page specifications, and ultimately wanted out of the deal.

Facts

  • Late 1950: the Rays asked Eurice for an estimate (about $16,000). Their architect redrew the plans and drafted specifications.
  • January 1951: Mr. Ray and John Eurice went item by item through the seven-page "Memorandum Specifications" dated January 9. Eurice vetoed or changed several items (poured concrete walls, "fir or pine," "or equivalent"). The agreed changes were marked in green ink.
  • February 14: Eurice submitted its own unsigned three-page proposal to build for $16,300, with different, more general specifications. Mr. Ray said his own lawyer would draw the contract. That night the Rays retyped the corrected January specifications as five pages dated February 14.
  • February 22: the contract was signed at Eurice's office. It required construction "strictly in accordance" with the plans and the five-page February 14 specifications, and barred deviations without the owner's approval. Both Eurice brothers admitted reading the contract, including that paragraph. John Eurice later also signed every page of the plans and the five-page specifications at the lender's office.
  • May 8: Henry Eurice threw the plans, specifications and contract across the desk, saying he had never seen the specifications and would not build to them. The Eurices claimed they thought the contract referred to their own three-page proposal.

Procedural History

  • The Rays sued in the Circuit Court for Baltimore County for total breach.
  • Trial judge (Judge Gontrum), sitting without a jury, found an "honest mistake" and no meeting of the minds, and entered judgment for the builder.
  • The Rays appealed to the Maryland Court of Appeals.

Issue

Is a party who reads and signs a written contract bound by its terms when it claims it subjectively understood the contract to refer to different (its own) specifications?

Holding

Yes. Reversed. The builder was bound by the contract as written and had wrongfully breached it. Judgment was entered for the Rays for $5,993.40, the cost of completion above the contract price.

Reasoning

  1. Objective test. Intent is judged by outward manifestations, not secret intent. The court quotes Restatement (First) § 20, Williston, and Learned Hand's "twenty bishops" passage from Hotchkiss v. National City Bank: even if twenty bishops proved a party meant something else, it is held to the usual meaning of its words, absent mutual mistake or the like.
  2. Duty to read and signature rule. "Absent fraud, duress or mutual mistake, ... one having the capacity to understand a written document who reads and signs it, or, without reading it ... signs it, is bound by his signature."
  3. Any mistake was unilateral. The Rays intended their specifications and the contract said so. Any misconception was only in the Eurices' minds, so it did not prevent formation. (Quoting Williston § 1577 and Restatement (First) § 70.)
  4. Credibility. It "strain[s] credulity" that experienced builders read a contract identifying specifications by title, date and page count (five pages, not three) without knowing what they agreed to. John Eurice also signed every page at the lender.
  5. No parol variation. The builder may not vary a clear writing by parol or impose its own interpretation.
  6. Damages. Expectation measure: the cost of having the house built in excess of the contract price.

Rule

A party's assent is measured by the reasonable meaning of its words and conduct (objective theory). One who signs a writing is bound by its terms, whether or not it read or understood them, unless there is fraud, duress, mutual mistake or another recognized defense. A unilateral mistake about the contents of a signed writing does not, by itself, prevent formation.

Why It Matters

  • It is the course's foundation for the objective theory (Holmes, Williston, Hand). Assent is about what you show, not what you think.
  • It introduces the two basic requirements of an enforceable contract: mutual assent and consideration (slides).
  • It previews later doctrines: unilateral mistake (Mistake), the parol evidence rule (Parol Evidence Rule), and the "duty to read," which courts later qualify (see Park 100 Investors v. Kartes).
  • It introduces expectation damages measured by the cost to complete (Expectation Damages).

Other Key Points

  • Note 1, credibility: Judge Hammond plainly did not believe the Eurices. Under the objective test, does their honesty even matter?
  • Note 3, objective theory: Holmes's justifications were efficiency (subjective inquiry would make enforcement harder) and fairness (both sides know words will be read in their ordinary sense). Restatement (Second) § 21 rejects the subjective approach.
  • Note 4, bargaining power: classical law ignored imbalances. The same rule binds weaker parties too (Skrbina v. Fleming Cos., employee bound by a release).
  • Note 5, "duty to read": Professor Knapp argues it should be at most a rebuttable presumption of knowing assent, subject to duress, fraud, mistake and unconscionability.
  • Note 6, jest cases: Lucy v. Zehmer (seller bound despite "joking") and Leonard v. Pepsico (no reasonable person would take the Harrier-jet ad seriously).
  • Later contrast (Week 7 slides): Park 100 Investors v. Kartes lets signers avoid a document when the other party actively misrepresented what it was. Ray had no such fraud.
