The short version

A court can refuse to enforce a contract, or part of one, that is shockingly one-sided, especially when the weaker side had no real choice or didn't understand the terms.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Unconscionability.

What it's about

Unconscionability is the safety valve for deals so unfair that enforcing them would offend the court's conscience. It comes from UCC § 2-302 and Restatement § 208.

Courts usually look for both:

  • Procedural unconscionability, an unfair process: no real choice (take-it-or-leave-it forms), hidden or confusing fine print, a big gap in bargaining power, high pressure.
  • Substantive unconscionability, unfair terms: terms that are unreasonably favorable to the stronger side.

Most courts use a sliding scale: the more unfair the process, the less unfair the terms need to be, and vice versa.

What the court can do

The judge, not a jury, decides. The court may:

  1. refuse to enforce the whole contract,
  2. cut out the unfair clause and enforce the rest, or
  3. limit the clause so it isn't unfair.

An everyday example

A store sells furniture on credit to a low-income customer. In the fine print, every item stays "unpaid" until everything is paid, so missing one payment lets the store repossess all of it, even items paid off long ago. That cross-collateral clause could be unconscionable.

The cases, in plain words

  • Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture: the famous furniture-store case. Courts can refuse to enforce contracts that leave one side with "an absence of meaningful choice" plus unreasonably favorable terms.
  • Higgins v. Superior Court: orphaned siblings on a TV home-makeover show signed an arbitration clause buried in paperwork, and it was struck down.
  • De La Torre v. CashCall: interest rates of 96-135% on consumer loans can be unconscionable even where no rate cap applies.
  • In re Checking Account Overdraft Litigation: bank customers could use unconscionability to get money back, not just to avoid paying.
  • Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute: by contrast, a fine-print forum clause was enforced.
  • Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing: arbitration agreements upheld against farmworker challenges.

Words you'll hear

  • Adhesion contract: a standard form offered take-it-or-leave-it. Not automatically unconscionable.
  • Sliding scale: more of one kind of unfairness means less of the other is needed.
