Quick reference

Citation: 257 Iowa 613, 133 N.W.2d 666 (1965) · Casebook: Knapp p. 634 · Topic: Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure › Fraudulent misrepresentation One line: An elderly widow sold nearly $30,000 of Arthur Murray dance lessons through flattery could set aside her release of an earlier suit and recover actual and punitive damages for fraud.

Overview

A course case on fraudulent misrepresentation, including statements of opinion, and on rescinding a release procured by fraudulent overreaching. Slides suggest undue influence may be the best doctrinal explanation of the result.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Agnes Syester. A "lonely and elderly widow." She wanted damages (actual and punitive) for fraud, which first required rescinding releases she had signed.
  • Defendants / appellants: Banta and the other owners of the Des Moines Arthur Murray Dance Studio, along with instructor and employee Mr. Carey. They relied on the releases and denied fraud.

Facts

  • Through intense flattery and sales pressure, the studio sold Syester lessons worth nearly $30,000 (slides), far more than she could ever use. [Outside knowledge: over 4,000 hours of lessons.]
  • She sued the studio for fraud. The studio then had Mr. Carey, an instructor she was fond of, persuade her to drop the suit and sign releases. He allegedly told her she had the ability to be a professional dancer and that she did not need an attorney. [Outside knowledge: she received a partial refund.]
  • She later sued again, seeking to rescind the releases and recover damages.

Procedural History

  • The jury returned a verdict for the plaintiff "in a substantial amount" (actual and exemplary damages). [Outside knowledge: about $14,000 actual and $40,000 punitive.]
  • Iowa Supreme Court: affirmed.

Issue

Could the jury find that the releases were procured by fraud, so they could be set aside, and that the dance studio's sales were fraudulent?

Holding

Yes. The evidence supported the jury's findings of fraudulent overreaching in obtaining the releases and in the underlying sales. Judgment affirmed.

Reasoning

  • Quoted on slides: "The evidence was such that the jury could find that there was such a concerted effort, lacking in propriety, to obtain the releases as to constitute fraudulent overreaching. The jury obviously concluded that there was a predatory play on the vanity and credulity of an old lady."
  • Misrepresentations? Perhaps. Carey said she could be a professional dancer and didn't need a lawyer.
  • Reasonable reliance? Harder. She had already sued, alleging she had been lied to. How could she now claim she "believed and relied upon" statements plainly meant to make her drop that suit? (slides)

Rule

A release or contract procured by fraudulent misrepresentation, including statements of opinion made by one who knows them false, or made in a relationship of trust, may be rescinded, and the defrauded party may recover damages.

Why It Matters

  • Note 3, statements of opinion: modern law has substantially eroded the rule that opinions are not actionable. They may be actionable when the speaker knows the statement is false, when there is a close relationship, or when the opinion implies underlying facts.
  • Note 4, reasonable reliance: could she really believe she had professional potential, or was she bargaining for the companionship the lessons provided?
  • Slides: undue influence (the Odorizzi framework) may be the best explanation for the result.

Other Key Points

  • Fraud elements (slides, Week 7 Part 1): (1) a misrepresentation of fact, (2) fraudulent (knowing) or material, (3) justifiable reliance that induced assent. Nonfraudulent (innocent) misrepresentation requires materiality.
  • Fraud in the execution (void) vs. fraud in the inducement (voidable). See Park 100 Investors v. Kartes.
