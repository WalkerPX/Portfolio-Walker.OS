Big picture

  • Substantial performance: minor deviations do not excuse the other party's payment.
  • Only a substantial deviation justifies refusal to pay (Jacob & Youngs, Cardozo).
  • Substantial performance = partial breach: damages owed, but contract not discharged (Sackett Note 2).
  • Breach spectrum: partial → material → total.
  • Material breach is necessary but not sufficient for total breach.
  • Constructive conditions are default rules; parties can contract around them.
  • Substantial performance doctrine does not apply to express conditions.

Doctrine outline

  • Substantial performance (Jacob & Youngs)
    • Contractor breached by not using Reading pipe.
    • Breach did not justify Kent withholding the balance.
    • Only substantial deviation justifies refusal to pay.
    • Rationale: "partly of justice and partly of presumable intention"; courts refuse forfeiture for "venial faults."
    • Damages: difference in value between home as promised and home as built — not cost of correction.
  • Constructive conditions (Note 2)
    • Default rules applying absent party agreement.
    • Substantial performance is an aspect of the general doctrine of constructive conditions (Note 4).
  • Express conditions (Note 3)
    • Parties generally free to make any performance obligation an express condition.
    • If express condition: harder to excuse failure; substantial performance doctrine does not apply.
    • Court may still excuse via: interpretation, waiver, or excuse to avoid forfeiture.
  • Constructive conditions comment (p. 860)
    • Parties free to change these rules by agreement; most modern commercial contracts do.
  • Breach categories (Sackett)
    • Partial: not significant (short delay, minor payment deficiency absent other circumstances).
    • Material: failure to perform a significant obligation (e.g., failure to tender balance of purchase price).
    • Total: material nonperformance not cured after a reasonable period.
  • Material → total (Sackett Note 3)
    • Key factors: reasonableness of notice; opportunity to cure.
    • Material breach not total unless further delay in terminating will unduly prejudice the other party.

Tests and elements

  • Substantial performance factors (Note 5) — checklist:
    • Purpose of the provision
    • Reason for nonperformance (especially good faith)
    • Cost of remedying the defect
    • Adequacy of damages to compensate harm
  • Material vs. total breach (Sackett) — checklist:
    • Is the breach significant (material)?
    • Was reasonable notice given?
    • Was there an opportunity to cure?
    • Has a reasonable period to cure expired?
    • Would further delay in terminating unduly prejudice the other party?
  • Withholding performance (Sackett) — checklist:
    • Was the other party's failure at least "material" (vs. "partial")?
  • Excusing an express condition — checklist:
    • Interpretation
    • Waiver
    • Excuse to avoid forfeiture

Cases

  • Jacob & Youngs, Inc. v. Kent (p. 851) — Cardozo; develops substantial performance. Contractor installed non-Reading pipe; breach, but Kent not justified in refusing balance ($3,483.46). Damages = difference in value, not cost of correction.
  • Sackett v. Spindler (p. 863) — frames withholding performance (material vs. partial) and termination/total breach questions. Note 1: court seemingly treats "material" and "total" as synonymous; Restatement (Second) does not.

Examples and hypos

  • Non-Reading pipe installed in interior of home; equivalent quality; replacement expense high → substantial performance, no forfeiture.
  • Short delay or minor payment deficiency, absent other circumstances → partial breach.
  • Failure to tender balance of purchase price → material breach (Sackett).

Exam angles

  • Distinguish substantial performance from material and total breach.
  • Apply Note 5 factors to decide if deviation is substantial.
  • Check whether obligation is an express condition — if so, substantial performance doctrine is unavailable.
  • If express condition, run through interpretation / waiver / forfeiture excuse.
  • Separate the withholding question (material?) from the termination question (total?).
  • Apply notice and cure factors before finding material breach became total.
  • Measure damages: difference in value vs. cost of correction.
