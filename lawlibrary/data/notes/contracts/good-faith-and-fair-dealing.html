In one paragraph

Every contract carries an implied duty of good faith and fair dealing in performance and enforcement (Restatement § 205; UCC § 1-304). Under the UCC, good faith means "honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing." Neither party may act to destroy the other's right to the fruits of the contract. Courts also fill gaps with implied terms: reasonable efforts in exclusive dealings (Wood v. Lucy Lady Duff-Gordon; UCC § 2-306(2)) and reasonable notice before ending a contract of indefinite length (Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing; UCC § 2-309(3)). Good faith limits how discretion is exercised (Locke v. Warner Bros) and can apply even between sophisticated parties (Seidenberg v. Summit Bank). It does not create duties the contract doesn't impose.

Sources of the duty

  • Restatement (Second) § 205: every contract imposes on each party a duty of good faith and fair dealing in its performance and enforcement.
  • UCC § 1-304: every contract or duty within the Code imposes an obligation of good faith in performance and enforcement.
  • Two standards (Locke Note 1): subjective (honesty in fact) and objective (reasonable commercial standards).
  • Limiting principle: good faith governs the exercise of rights the contract gives. It doesn't override express terms or add new obligations.

Implied terms

Reasonable efforts

  • Wood v. Lucy Lady Duff-Gordon (Cardozo): Lucy gave Wood an exclusive right to place her endorsements, and he returned no express promise to do anything. The court held the writing "instinct with an obligation" and implied a promise to use reasonable efforts. That supplies consideration (see Consideration › Illusory promises and mutuality).
  • Codified for goods: UCC § 2-306(2) (exclusive dealing → best efforts by the seller to supply and by the buyer to promote sales).

Reasonable notice of termination

  • Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing: in an oral distributorship with no fixed term, UCC § 2-309(3) requires reasonable notice before termination. The point is to give the other side time to recoup its investment and find a substitute.
  • A "no notice" clause is enforceable unless unconscionable. Termination on an agreed event (for example, two straight years of declining sales) is effective without more notice (existing note).
  • Wood vs. Leibel: Wood implies efforts to carry out what the parties intended. Leibel implies notice as a matter of fairness.

Bad-faith performance and discretion

  • Seidenberg v. Summit Bank: the claim survived even though both parties were sophisticated. The implied covenant is successfully invoked in three categories:
    1. Implying a term is needed to protect the parties' reasonable expectations.
    2. A termination or other act appears to be in bad faith or pretextual.
    3. Discretion the contract grants should be limited by fair dealing.
  • Quote: "Bad faith, like beauty, is in the eye of the beholder," or more to the point, of the trier of fact. So good-faith claims are usually fact questions.
  • Locke v. Warner Bros: Warner had discretion to accept or reject Locke's film projects, and allegedly rejected them all without considering them. That discretion was still subject to good faith. Warner could not use it to deny Locke the benefit of the bargain. Remanded on breach of contract and fraud.
  • Satisfaction clauses: where the clause turns on taste or creative judgment, the test is usually subjective (honest dissatisfaction). Where it turns on commercial or mechanical fitness, the test is objective (would a reasonable person be satisfied). See Locke Note 2. [Outside knowledge] the subjective/objective split for satisfaction clauses is standard Restatement § 228 doctrine.

Exam framework

  1. Is there an express term on point? Good faith can't contradict it.
  2. Is there a gap? Imply reasonable efforts (exclusive dealing) or reasonable notice (indefinite duration).
  3. Does a party have discretion (sole discretion, satisfaction, termination)? Ask whether it was exercised honestly (subjective) and, where appropriate, reasonably (objective).
  4. Run the Seidenberg three categories.
  5. Is the alleged bad faith a fact question (Seidenberg)? Then say what evidence would show pretext.
  6. Connect it to consideration: an implied good-faith duty can save a promise from being illusory (Wood).

Hypos (slides)

  • Hypo 4, "Exclusive Rights": MegaCorp gives Agent a two-year exclusive right to license its software. Agent keeps 20%, and its only express duty is to "maintain reasonable records." MegaCorp then licenses directly. Apply Wood: what efforts obligation is implied, what reasonable efforts require of Agent, and whether MegaCorp's direct dealing breaches.
  • Hypo 5, "We Have Sole Discretion": a publisher with "sole and absolute discretion" rejects a commercially strong manuscript without reviewing it, after its parent company bought a competing book. Under Locke, subjective good faith is required. Look for evidence of pretext and consider remedies.
