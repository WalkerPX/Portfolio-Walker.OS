Ct. 1997) · Casebook: Knapp p. 879 · Topic: Anticipatory Repudiation and Adequate Assurance › Adequate assurance (UCC 2-609) One line: A beverage maker with reasonable grounds for insecurity about its Canadian distributor's finances properly demanded adequate assurance. The distributor's failure to respond was a repudiation that justified ending the relationship.

Overview

The course case on UCC § 2-609: when reasonable grounds for insecurity arise, a party may demand adequate assurance of performance and suspend its own performance. Failure to provide assurance within a reasonable time (not over 30 days) is a repudiation.

Parties

  • Plaintiff: Hornell Brewing Co. A maker of bottled soft drinks and beverages ([Outside knowledge: "Arizona" iced tea]). It sought a declaratory judgment that its distribution relationship with Spry had ended.
  • Defendant: Spry (and his company), a Canadian distributor. He claimed an exclusive distributorship.

Facts

  • 1993: an oral arrangement made Spry the distributor of Hornell's products in Canada.
  • By April 1994, Spry was in arrears and sales were below projections.
  • Hornell offered to resume credit up to $300,000 if Spry kept current, with invoices payable within 14 days.
  • Spry paid nearly $80,000 in arrears and then ordered $390,000+ more, exceeding the credit limit.
  • Hornell then demanded either a credit line plus a personal guarantee backed by a verifiable financial statement, or an irrevocable letter of credit for $300,000. Spry never responded.
  • Reports indicated Spry's company was a shell, with no staff, facilities or trucks.

Procedural History

  • Hornell sued for a declaratory judgment. After trial, the New York trial court ruled for Hornell (slides: "a New York trial court").

Issue

Did Hornell have reasonable grounds for insecurity justifying its demands for assurance, and did Spry's failure to respond amount to a repudiation that ended the relationship?

Holding

Yes. Hornell's demands were justified. Spry's total silence in response was a failure to give adequate assurance, which is a repudiation (material breach) justifying termination.

Reasoning

  • Two demands (slides): the first (pay arrears, stay current) was justified by Spry's arrears. The second (a guarantee or letter of credit) was justified by the order exceeding the credit limit and reports that Spry was a shell with no infrastructure.
  • Reasonable grounds for insecurity are judged by commercial standards (§ 2-609(2)). Here they were plainly present.
  • Adequate assurance is what is commercially reasonable under the circumstances. A letter of credit or guarantee was reasonable given the risk.
  • Spry's failure to provide any response within a reasonable time was a repudiation (§ 2-609(4)).
  • The court also relied on information learned later and Spry's "regrettable lack of credibility."

Rule

Under UCC § 2-609 (and Restatement (Second) § 251), when reasonable grounds for insecurity arise about the other party's performance, a party may demand adequate assurance (in writing under the UCC) and, if commercially reasonable, suspend its own performance. Failure to provide assurance adequate under the circumstances within a reasonable time (at most 30 days under the UCC) is a repudiation.

Why It Matters

  • Elements (slides): (1) reasonable grounds for insecurity, (2) a demand for assurances of performance.
  • Objective change test (slides): insecurity requires an actual or apparent change in condition, or a change in the information known to the other party.
  • Note 1 (slides) and Notes 4-6: the right to demand is permissive, not compulsory. Good practice is to make a demand stating which assurances are acceptable, or be ready to justify not doing so.
  • It is the safe harbor for a party facing uncertainty, compared with guessing at repudiation (Truman L. Flatt and Sons v. Schupf).

Other Key Points

  • Restatement § 251 extends the concept beyond goods to common-law contracts.
