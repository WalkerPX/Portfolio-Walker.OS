Quick reference

Citation: 137 Wis. 2d 506, 405 N.W.2d 303 (1987) · Casebook: Knapp p. 304 · Topic: Restitution and Promissory Restitution One line: A woman who cohabited with a man for 12 years and helped build his business stated claims for express or implied-in-fact contract and for unjust enrichment, though not under the divorce statute.

Overview

Restitution and contract in non-marital relationships. Unmarried cohabitants may sue each other on contract (express or implied in fact) and unjust enrichment, as long as the claim does not rest on sexual services. The leading case in this area is Marvin v. Marvin.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Sue Ann Evans Watts. She lived with the defendant for 12 years, had two children with him and took his surname. She wanted a share of the property accumulated during the relationship.
  • Defendant / respondent: James Watts. He kept all the assets when the relationship ended.

Facts

  • The couple cohabited for 12 years, held themselves out as married, and had two children. [Outside knowledge: they began living together in 1969.]
  • Sue contributed domestic services (homemaking, childcare) and professional services to building James's business. She allegedly gave up her own career opportunities. [Outside knowledge: she quit her job and worked in his landscaping business.]
  • They separated. James kept all the assets.

Procedural History

  • The circuit court for Dane County dismissed the amended complaint for failure to state a claim.
  • The Wisconsin Supreme Court took the appeal on certification and reversed and remanded.

Issue

Can an unmarried cohabitant recover a share of property accumulated during the relationship, under the divorce property-division statute, estoppel, contract, or unjust enrichment?

Holding

The complaint states claims for (1) breach of an express or implied-in-fact contract and (2) unjust enrichment. The claims under the family-law property-division statute and estoppel fail. Reversed and remanded.

Reasoning

Sue tried four theories (slides):

  1. Family law property division: rejected. The statute applies to marriages, and the legislature did not extend it to cohabitants.
  2. Estoppel (that James was estopped to deny a marriage): rejected.
  3. Express or implied-in-fact contract: allowed. Wisconsin does not require a writing, and enforcing it is not against public policy as long as the claim is not based on sexual services. The parties' conduct (pooling efforts and holding themselves out as married) can show an implied agreement to share.
  4. Unjust enrichment: allowed. Sue conferred benefits, James accepted and kept them knowing she expected to share, and retention without payment is unjust.
  • The court rejected arguments that the legislature preempted the field or that public policy against cohabitation bars relief.

Rule

Unmarried cohabitants may recover from each other on express or implied-in-fact contract or unjust enrichment for contributions to property accumulated during the relationship, provided the claim does not rest on sexual services as consideration.

Why It Matters

  • It shows restitution filling gaps where no statute or express contract applies, in intimate relationships.
  • Measure: the increase in the defendant's wealth is more appropriate than the reasonable value of services for intimate relationships (slides). Contrast Commerce Partnership v. Equity Contracting (market value).
  • Note 2: on remand Sue recovered only about 10%. Apparent legal success may bring little practical benefit (the same lesson as Marvin).
  • Note 3: requiring strict standards among family members is unwarranted, since they deal informally on mutual expectations of fairness.
  • Sue could also have asserted promissory estoppel (she gave up a career) and fraud. Watts implicates five bodies of law (slides).

Other Key Points

  • Hypo 8 (slides): a non-marital partner contributes to a business. Choose the theory and the measure.
