Quick reference

Citation: 20 F. Supp. 1 (E.D. Ill. 1937) · Casebook: Knapp p. 107 · Topic: Consideration One line: A company's promise of lifetime half-pay to long-serving older employees was unenforceable. Past service, moral obligation and picking up the checks were not consideration.

Overview

A classic past consideration / moral obligation / conditional gift case. It shows each theory a promisee might use to find consideration for a gratuitous benefit, and why each fails under classical doctrine.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs: 13 former employees and the administrators of 5 deceased employees (18 in all, slides). Long-serving, comparatively aged refinery workers. They wanted the promised lifetime payments to continue.
  • Defendant: Indian Refining Co. The employer. It stopped the payments, arguing they were a gratuitous, temporary arrangement never approved by its board.

Facts

  • July 28, 1930: the vice-president and general manager (Anglin) called long-serving employees into his office one by one and told each he would be paid half his current wages for the rest of his life. The stated reason was the company's desire to provide for the welfare of older employees.
  • Employees stayed on the payroll (their share of group-insurance premiums was still deducted) but did no further work. Their only obligation was to come to the office to pick up their checks on payday.
  • Payments continued for almost a year, then the company ended them.
  • Company's position: the payments were to cushion employees against layoffs, had no fixed duration, were gratuitous, and the board never approved them.

Procedural History

Suit in equity in federal district court. After hearing testimony, the court entered a decree for the defendant dismissing the bill for want of equity.

Issue

Was the employer's promise of lifetime half-pay supported by consideration, given that the employees provided no further services?

Holding

No. Even viewing the testimony most favorably to the plaintiffs, there were no valid contracts. The bill was dismissed.

Reasoning

The court considers and rejects each possible basis for consideration:

  1. Past services: services already rendered were not given in exchange for the promise. Past consideration is no consideration.
  2. Moral obligation: a moral duty to care for aged workers is not legal consideration. The court praises pensions as social policy but says that, absent a statute, the policy enters the employment relationship only by contract. "The court is endowed with no power of legislation; nor may it read into contracts provisions upon which the parties' minds have not met."
  3. Picking up the checks: this was a condition of a gift, not a bargained-for detriment. The company did not want employees to come in; it was just the way to receive the money (Williston's "tramp" analysis).

Rule

A promise made in recognition of past services or out of moral obligation is not supported by consideration. An act the promisee must do to receive a gift (e.g., collecting it) is a condition, not consideration, unless the promisor bargained for that act as a benefit to itself.

Why It Matters

  • It is the clearest course illustration of three non-consideration theories: past consideration, moral obligation, conditional gift.
  • It sets up the modern exceptions: Promissory Estoppel (reliance: did employees retire or forgo other work?) and promissory restitution under Restatement § 86 (Mills v. Wyman, Webb v. McGowin).
  • Compare Pennsy Supply v. American Ash Recycling: there, taking the material benefited the promisor, so it was a bargain.

Other Key Points

  • Note 4, agency: even apart from consideration, Anglin may have lacked authority to bind the company. That is an agency problem, not just a consideration problem (slides).
  • Margaret hypo (slides): a paralegal of 28 years is promised $2,000/month for life on retirement. Is that consideration, promissory estoppel (she retires in reliance), or § 86?
