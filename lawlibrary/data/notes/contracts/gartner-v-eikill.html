Quick reference

Citation: 319 N.W.2d 397 (Minn. 1982) · Casebook: Knapp p. 754 (Lenawee Note 8) · Topic: Mistake › Mutual mistake One line: A buyer could rescind a land purchase for mutual mistake when an unknown zoning restriction made the land unusable for his intended purpose, a result that sits uneasily with Lenawee.

Coverage

Known only from Knapp's Lenawee Note 8 and the slides. The opinion is not reprinted.

Overview

A mutual-mistake case reaching the opposite result from Lenawee on similar facts (an undiscovered legal restriction on land use). It is used to test whether the mistake cases can be reconciled.

Parties

  • Purchaser: Gartner, who sought rescission.
  • Seller: Eikill, who resisted rescission. [Party roles inferred from the caption and Note 8.]

Facts

  • From Knapp: the purchaser sought rescission, claiming that, unknown to him, the land had been subject to a special zoning restriction making it unusable for the purpose for which he bought it.
  • The contract said the seller would convey marketable title subject to, among other things, "Building and zoning laws, ordinances, State and Federal regulations."
  • Neither party knew of the restriction at the time of sale.

Procedural History

From Knapp: the Minnesota court allowed rescission. (Procedural details are not provided.)

Issue

May a buyer rescind a land sale for mutual mistake about a zoning restriction both parties were unaware of, despite a contract clause making title subject to zoning laws?

Holding

Yes. Rescission on the basis of "mutual mistake of fact."

Reasoning

From Knapp: the court relied on prior Minnesota cases and on Sherwood v. Walker.

Rule

A mutual mistake about a fact (here, a land-use restriction) that defeats the purpose of the purchase may justify rescission (in Gartner's view), notwithstanding a general "subject to zoning laws" clause.

Why It Matters

  • Note 8 asks: are Gartner and Lenawee inconsistent? Can they be harmonized? Was one, or both, wrongly decided?
  • Slides' possible reconciliation: zoning restrictions are a matter of public record (arguably the buyer was at fault in Gartner), while a hidden septic defect is not easily discoverable (the buyer was less at fault in Lenawee). Yet the outcomes run the other way, which underscores the inconsistency.
  • Under Restatement § 154, the "subject to ... zoning laws" clause arguably allocated the risk to the buyer, so Gartner looks harder to justify under modern doctrine.
