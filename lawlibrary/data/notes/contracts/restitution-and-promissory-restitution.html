In one paragraph

Restitution protects against unjust enrichment. There are two elements: (1) one person is enriched, and (2) it would be unjust for that person to keep the enrichment. It is a third way to recover beside contract and Promissory Estoppel, so plead all three in the alternative. With no promise at all, the law implies a "quasi-contract" for emergency services (Credit Bureau Enterprises v. Pelo), for unpaid subcontractors in narrow cases (Commerce Partnership v. Equity Contracting) and for cohabitants (Watts v. Watts). If a promise was made after a benefit was received, the classical rule says past consideration does not count (Mills v. Wyman). The modern material benefit rule (Restatement § 86, Webb v. McGowin) enforces such a promise "to the extent necessary to prevent injustice." Restitution is also a remedy for breach. That side is covered in Reliance and Restitution Damages.

  • Implied-in-fact contract = a real contract inferred from conduct. Implied-in-law (quasi-) contract = no agreement, a legal fiction to prevent unjust enrichment. Credit Bureau Enterprises v. Pelo draws this line.

Unjust enrichment without a promise

Elements (existing note):

  1. Enrichment of one person.
  2. Retention of that enrichment is unjust to another.

Officious intermeddlers and volunteers usually can't recover. The exceptions are emergencies and benefits the recipient had a chance to decline. [Outside knowledge] the "officious intermeddler" label comes from the Restatement of Restitution and is standard doctrine.

Emergency and necessary services

  • Restatement (First) of Restitution § 116 (as summarized in Credit Bureau Enterprises v. Pelo): someone who provides necessary services in an emergency to protect another's life or health may recover their reasonable value, even without consent.
  • Credit Bureau Enterprises v. Pelo: the patient was held involuntarily in a hospital and argued he lacked capacity when he signed the admission forms. He was still liable in restitution for the reasonable value of the care. The slides call this case "Credit Enterprises v. Pelo."

Subcontractors and owners

  • Commerce Partnership v. Equity Contracting: an unpaid subcontractor may recover from the owner in unjust enrichment only if (1) the subcontractor exhausted its remedies against the general contractor and is still unpaid, and (2) the owner has not given consideration to anyone for the improvements. If the owner paid the GC, element (2) fails.

Cohabitants and family

  • Watts v. Watts: unmarried cohabitants may recover on implied-in-fact contract or unjust enrichment for contributions made during the relationship. Public policy does not bar the claim just because the relationship was nonmarital. The existing note records a 10% recovery on remand. See also Marvin v. Marvin (note case).
  • Measure: in commercial cases, the reasonable (market) value of services. In intimate relationships, often the increase in the defendant's wealth (existing note; Commerce Partnership Note 2).

Promissory restitution (Restatement 86)

Classical rule: a promise made after a past benefit, motivated by moral obligation, has no consideration. In Mills v. Wyman, a father wrote promising to pay for the care a stranger gave his dying adult son, Levi. The promise was not enforceable, because moral obligation is not consideration.

Material benefit rule: in Webb v. McGowin, Webb was badly injured diverting a falling block away from McGowin. McGowin then promised to pay Webb $15 every two weeks for life and paid from 1925 to 1934. After McGowin died, his estate stopped paying. The promise was enforced because McGowin received a material benefit (his life and body were saved).

Restatement (Second) § 86 (checklist):

  1. The promisor previously received a benefit from the promisee.
  2. The promisor later made a promise in recognition of that benefit.
  3. The promise is binding to the extent necessary to prevent injustice.
  4. Limits: (a) not binding if the promisee conferred the benefit as a gift or the promisor has not been unjustly enriched; (b) not binding to the extent its value is disproportionate to the benefit.
  5. Comment f: § 86 doesn't apply where the benefit was conferred under a pre-existing contract (the contract governs).
  • Only a minority of jurisdictions adopt § 86 expressly. The existing note says "a few"; unverified beyond that.
  • Contrast with Mills: the benefit in Mills went to the adult son, not to the promisor-father. That is one way to reconcile the two cases.
  • Revived debts (promises to pay debts barred by the statute of limitations or discharged in bankruptcy) are the older, recognized exceptions to the past-consideration rule (see Consideration › Past consideration and moral obligation).

Restitution as a remedy for breach (overview)

Covered in full at Reliance and Restitution Damages › Restitution as a remedy for breach:

  • Non-breaching party, losing contract: quantum meruit for the reasonable value of the work, even if the plaintiff would have lost money on the contract (US ex rel Coastal Steel v. Algernon Blair).
  • Breaching party: value of the benefit conferred minus the harm caused by the breach (Restatement § 374; Lancellotti v. Thomas).
  • Express contract covers the issue: no restitution, because the contract allocates the risk. In Shaffer v. George Washington University no express term covered in-person instruction, so restitution claims survived.

Exam framework

  1. Is there an enforceable contract? If yes, contract law governs and restitution is usually displaced (Shaffer; § 86 cmt. f).
  2. If not, is there a promise plus reliance? Then Promissory Estoppel.
  3. Was there a benefit conferred with no promise? Run the two elements of unjust enrichment. Then ask whether the plaintiff was a volunteer or officious intermeddler, whether there was an emergency (Pelo), or whether it is a subcontractor/owner case (Commerce Partnership).
  4. Was there a promise after the benefit? Mills (no) vs. Webb / § 86 (yes, to prevent injustice). Check the gift, disproportion and comment f limits.
  5. Measure: reasonable value vs. increase in wealth. State which one and why.

Hypos (slides and existing note)

  • Hypo 6: emergency care given without consent ($500). Under Pelo / § 116, restitution of the reasonable value.
  • Hypo 7: the GC goes bankrupt. The owner paid $75,000 on an $80,000 contract. The subcontractor sues the owner. Under Commerce Partnership, the owner's payment defeats element (2), at least in part.
  • Hypo 8: a cohabitant provides services. Choose the measure: reasonable value vs. increase in wealth.
  • Hypo 9: a Mills-style written promise to pay for care already given. No consideration. Is § 86 available?
  • Hypo 10: a rescuer sends a $12,000 bill and gets a written promise to pay. Webb / § 86, with the proportionality limit.
