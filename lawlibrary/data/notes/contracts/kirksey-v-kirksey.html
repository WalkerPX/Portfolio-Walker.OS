Quick reference

Citation: 8 Ala. 131 (1845) · Casebook: Knapp p. 215 · Topic: Promissory Estoppel One line: A brother-in-law's promise of "a place to raise your family" to a widow who moved 60 miles in reliance was a "mere gratuity" and unenforceable. Modern courts would likely enforce it under § 90.

Overview

The classic pre-promissory-estoppel case. The widow relied heavily on a gratuitous promise, yet the court refused enforcement because there was no consideration. It shows the gap that Restatement § 90 later fills.

Parties

  • Plaintiff (below) / defendant in error: Antillico Kirksey, the widow of the defendant's brother Henry, with several children. She wanted damages for being evicted after moving in reliance on the promise.
  • Defendant (below) / plaintiff in error: Kirksey, her brother-in-law. He wanted to avoid liability, arguing his promise was gratuitous.

Facts

  • The widow lived on public land under a lease she had held over, was "comfortably settled," and might have secured the land.
  • October 10, 1840: the defendant wrote her a letter. He had heard of his brother's death, said her situation was bad, advised her to sell and leave because the area was unhealthy, and wrote: "If you will come down and see me, I will let you have a place to raise your family, and I have more open land than I can tend; and on the account of your situation, and that of your family, I feel like I want you and the children to do well."
  • Within a month or two she abandoned her possession (without selling it) and moved her family 60 to 70 miles to his place. He put her in comfortable houses and gave her land to cultivate for two years, then moved her to an uncomfortable house in the woods, and finally required her to leave.

Procedural History

  • Action in assumpsit. The jury returned a $200 verdict for the plaintiff.
  • On a case agreed, the facts went to the Alabama Supreme Court: if the facts sustain the action, affirm, otherwise reverse.
  • Reversed.

Issue

Is a promise to provide a home, made to induce a relative to move, supported by consideration where the promisee incurred loss and inconvenience in moving?

Holding

No. The majority held the promise was "a mere gratuity," and no action lies for its breach. Judgment reversed.

Reasoning

  • Ormond, J. (writing for the court, but personally dissenting in reasoning): he thought the loss and inconvenience of breaking up and moving 60 miles was sufficient consideration for the promise.
  • The majority ("my brothers"): the move was merely the condition for receiving a gift. The promise was gratuitous, not bargained for, so it was unenforceable.

Rule

(Classical rule) A gratuitous promise is unenforceable even if the promisee relies on it to her detriment. Acts the promisee must take to receive a gift are conditions, not consideration.

Why It Matters

  • It shows why promissory estoppel developed. Under Restatement (Second) § 90, a promise the promisor should reasonably expect to induce reliance, which does induce it, is binding if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. "Modern courts would likely reach a different result under § 90" (slides).
  • It links to the gift-condition vs. consideration analysis (Pennsy Supply v. American Ash Recycling, Plowman v. Indian Refining). Did the brother-in-law want her to come (company, labor), so that it was a bargain? Knapp Note 1 asks whether she suffered a "legal detriment" (compare Hamer v. Sidway) and whether he got a benefit.

Other Key Points

  • Two possible explanations for the result (slides): (1) judicial reluctance to intervene in family disputes; (2) unwillingness to have property pass from male to female.
  • Hypo 1, "Move Here and I'll Take Care of You" (slides): the niece quits her job and moves states, and the uncle later stops support. Apply § 90's elements. Which is hardest? Usually definiteness of the promise and reasonableness of reliance.
  • Knapp pairs Kirksey with Harvey v. Dow (not on slides), which shows modern use of promissory estoppel in family settings.
