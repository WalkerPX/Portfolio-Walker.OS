The big picture: the life of a contract

Almost every contracts problem walks through the same five questions:

  1. Was there a deal? Did both sides agree, and on what? (Part 1)
  2. Will the law enforce it? Was there an exchange, reliance, or a benefit that would be unfair to keep? Does it need to be in writing? (Parts 2-3)
  3. Can someone get out of it? Was there a lie, a threat, a big mistake, a shockingly unfair term, or an unexpected disaster? (Part 4)
  4. Did someone break it? Was the breach serious enough to walk away? (Part 5)
  5. What does the injured side get? Usually money, sometimes a court order. (Part 6)

Part 1 — Making a contract

  • Foundations of Contract Law: A contract is a promise (or set of promises) that the law will back up.
  • Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance: A contract starts when one side makes an offer and the other accepts it.
  • Agreements to Agree and Letters of Intent: Sometimes people agree on the big picture but leave key details to work out later.
  • Option Contracts and Firm Offers: Normally an offer can be withdrawn any time before it's accepted.
  • UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation: When the deal is for goods (movable things), a special statute, the UCC, applies instead of ordinary contract rules.
  • Interpretation: When parties disagree about what contract words mean, courts look for the meaning a reasonable person would give them.

Part 2 — Which promises the law enforces

  • Consideration: The law generally enforces a promise only if it was part of a trade: each side gives or promises something in exchange for what the other side gives.
  • Promissory Estoppel: Even without a trade, a promise can be enforced if the other person reasonably relied on it and would be treated unfairly if the promise were broken.
  • Restitution and Promissory Restitution: If someone receives a benefit they'd be unfair to keep for free, the law can make them pay for it, even without a contract.
  • Good Faith and Fair Dealing: Every contract carries an unwritten promise that neither side will act dishonestly or unfairly to cheat the other out of what the deal was supposed to give them.

Part 3 — Writings

  • Statute of Frauds: A few kinds of contracts, such as land sales, deals that can't be finished within a year, and sales of goods of $500 or more, must be backed by a signed writing.
  • Parol Evidence Rule: If the parties wrote down their final deal, side agreements made before or while signing generally can't be used to change it.

Part 4 — Defenses and excuses

  • Capacity: Minors and people whose mental condition keeps them from understanding or controlling a deal can often cancel their contracts.
  • Duress and Undue Influence: A deal you agreed to only because of an improper threat, or because someone you trusted or depended on pressured you unfairly, can be cancelled.
  • Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure: If you were talked into a deal by a false statement, whether a lie or an important honest error, you can usually cancel it.
  • Unconscionability: A court can refuse to enforce a contract, or part of one, that is shockingly one-sided, especially when the weaker side had no real choice or didn't understand the terms.
  • Mistake: If both sides were wrong about a basic fact that changes the deal in a big way, the disadvantaged side may be able to cancel, unless it took on that risk.
  • Impracticability and Frustration: If something unexpected happens after the deal, nobody's fault, and it makes performance impossible, far more burdensome, or pointless, a party may be excused.

Part 5 — Performance and breach

  • Express Conditions: A condition is an event that must happen before someone has to perform.
  • Material Breach and Substantial Performance: Not every slip-up lets the other side walk away.
  • Anticipatory Repudiation and Adequate Assurance: If one side clearly says, or shows by its actions, that it won't perform when the time comes, the other side doesn't have to wait.

Part 6 — Remedies

  • Expectation Damages: The usual remedy for breach is money that puts the injured side where it would have been if the contract had been performed: the 'benefit of the bargain.'.
  • Limits on Damages: You can't recover every loss a breach causes.
  • Reliance and Restitution Damages: When the benefit of the bargain can't be measured, or isn't the right fit, courts can instead reimburse what you spent relying on the deal (reliance), or give back the value of what you handed over (restitution).
  • Specific Performance: Usually a court awards money for a breach.
  • Liquidated Damages: Parties can agree in advance on how much will be paid if someone breaches.

Extra — not on the syllabus

  • Third-Party Beneficiaries and Assignment: Sometimes a person who isn't a party to a contract can still enforce it, because the contract was meant to benefit them.

Ten words that unlock most of the course

WordPlain meaning
Offer / acceptanceA proposal that a simple "yes" would turn into a deal, and that "yes."
ConsiderationWhat each side gives or promises in exchange. The law's "was it a trade?" test.
Promissory estoppelEnforcing a promise because someone reasonably relied on it.
RestitutionMaking someone pay for a benefit it would be unfair to keep for free.
VoidableA valid contract that one side is allowed to cancel.
BreachNot doing what the contract requires.
Material breachA breach serious enough to let the other side stop performing.
ConditionAn event that must happen before someone has to perform.
Expectation damagesMoney to put you where you'd be if the deal had been kept.
