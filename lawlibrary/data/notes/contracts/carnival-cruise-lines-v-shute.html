Quick reference

Citation: 499 U.S. 585 (1991) · Casebook: Knapp pp. 18-22 (Problem 1-2 is "based directly on" the case) · Topics: Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance, Foundations of Contract Law One line: A forum-selection clause buried in fine print on a cruise ticket was enforced, so the injured passengers had to sue in Florida.

Coverage

Knapp presents the case as Problem 1-2 and does not reprint the opinion. Facts below come from Knapp and the slides. The Supreme Court's procedural history, holding and reasoning are marked [Outside knowledge].

Overview

The Week 1 vehicle for introducing standard-form (adhesion) contracts and the competing theories of contract law (efficiency, relational, critical). It also shows how a "procedural" contract term can decide whether a substantive claim is ever heard.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs / respondents: Eula and Russel Shute. Washington residents who took a seven-day cruise. Eula was injured on board. They wanted to sue for negligence in Washington, where they live.
  • Defendant / petitioner: Carnival Cruise Lines, Inc. A cruise line headquartered in Miami. It wanted the case in Florida under the ticket's forum-selection clause.

Facts

  • The Shutes booked through a travel agent. Carnival mailed standard-form tickets to them in Washington, and they received them.
  • The ticket face said: "SUBJECT TO CONDITIONS OF CONTRACT ON LAST PAGES — IMPORTANT! PLEASE READ CONTRACT ON LAST PAGES 1, 2, 3" (slides: in bold, all caps).
  • The back had 25 fine-print paragraphs. ¶3(a): accepting the ticket is acceptance of all terms. ¶8: all disputes "shall be litigated, if at all, in and before a Court located in the State of Florida."
  • The ship sailed from Los Angeles to Puerto Vallarta. In international waters off Mexico, Eula slipped on a deck mat during a galley tour and was injured. The Shutes sued in Washington.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The federal district court in Washington granted summary judgment for Carnival (on personal-jurisdiction grounds). The Ninth Circuit reversed and refused to enforce the forum clause because it was not freely bargained for. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed.

Issue

Is a non-negotiated forum-selection clause in a form passenger ticket enforceable against a consumer who received the ticket but did not bargain over the term?

Holding

[Outside knowledge] Yes. The clause was reasonable and enforceable. The Shutes had to litigate in Florida. (Majority by Justice Blackmun; Justice Stevens, joined by Justice Marshall, dissented.)

Reasoning

[Outside knowledge] The Court said a cruise line has a special interest in limiting where it can be sued, since passengers come from many places. The clause avoids confusion about the forum and saves litigation costs, and those savings are presumably passed on in lower fares. Florida was not a remote, inconvenient forum chosen to discourage claims. There was no bad faith or fraud, and the Shutes had notice. The dissent stressed the fine print, that passengers had no real chance to read it before paying, and the non-refundable fare.

Slide analysis of the problem:

  • Is there a contract? "No contract at all" is implausible because the Shutes took the cruise. The real fight is over the one clause: its location, small font, and ¶8 of 25. Carnival answers that the Shutes received the tickets and the face told them, in bold capitals, to read the terms.
  • Should there be a contract? Efficiency view: forum clauses cut litigation costs and promote predictability, and savings flow to consumers. Relational view: there is no ongoing relationship, so relationship norms carry little weight. Critical view: repeat players use forum and arbitration clauses to keep weaker parties out of court.

Rule

A forum-selection clause in a non-negotiated form contract is enforceable if it is reasonable, the party had notice, and it was not imposed in bad faith, by fraud or overreaching, or to discourage legitimate claims. [Outside knowledge: this is the Supreme Court's admiralty rule. Knapp uses the case as a problem, not for its rule.]

Why It Matters

  • It introduces standard-form contracting and assent "by acceptance of the ticket," themes that return in Unconscionability (Higgins, Restatement § 211(3)) and 2-207.
  • It shows contracts, civil procedure and torts overlapping: a contract term can effectively decide whether a tort claim is ever heard.
  • It frames the theories of contract law used all semester (Foundations of Contract Law).

Other Key Points

  • Practical drafting advice (slides): bigger font, plain language, initials next to key terms, a separate signature line affirming the terms were read.
  • Exam use: when a fine-print clause is challenged, run (1) assent and notice, (2) unconscionability (procedural and substantive), and (3) public policy.
