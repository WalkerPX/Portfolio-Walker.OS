In one paragraph

Restatement (Second) § 90(1): "A promise which the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance on the part of the promisee or a third person and which does induce such action or forbearance is binding if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement of the promise. The remedy granted for breach may be limited as justice requires." It is an alternative basis for enforcement, not a substitute for consideration. It applies to family promises, charitable pledges (King v. Trustees of Boston University, § 90(2)), offers (Drennan v. Star Paving, § 87(2)), negotiations (Pops Cones v. Resorts International) and the statute of frauds (Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice, § 139). The remedy is often reliance damages (Walser v. Toyota Motor Sales).

Elements (slides)

  1. A promise.
  2. The promisor should reasonably expect it to induce reliance.
  3. The promise does induce action or forbearance, definite and substantial (classical wording; the Second Restatement drops "definite and substantial" for § 90 but keeps it in § 87(2) [Outside knowledge: section-wording detail]).
  4. Injustice can be avoided only by enforcement.

Key framing (slides)

  • Promissory estoppel is not a substitute for consideration. It is an alternative basis for enforcement.
  • Courts are policing injustice, not enforcing promises wholesale.
  • Reliance must be reasonable. The promisee's subjective belief does not control (including reliance on the promisor's authority: Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice).
  • The remedy may be limited: reliance rather than expectation damages.

History

  • Before § 90: reliance alone didn't make a gratuitous promise binding (Kirksey v. Kirksey: "mere gratuity"). Possible reasons: reluctance to enter family disputes, and reluctance to have property pass from male to female. Modern courts would likely enforce under § 90.
  • Hand's narrow view: promissory estoppel applies to donative promises, not commercial offers (James Baird v. Gimbel Bros), later rejected by Drennan v. Star Paving.

Family promises

  • Kirksey (classical denial). Modern cases (e.g., Harvey v. Dow in Knapp) use promissory estoppel to protect family members who relied.
  • Hypo 1, "Move Here and I'll Take Care of You" (slides): the niece quits her job and moves. Is the promise definite enough? Is reliance reasonable? Is injustice shown?

Charitable subscriptions

  • Courts have long strained to enforce pledges (consideration through the charity's undertakings, or reliance).
  • § 90(2): a charitable subscription (or marriage settlement) is binding under § 90 without proof that it induced action or forbearance.
  • King v. Trustees of Boston University: the jury could find Dr. King's letter an enforceable charitable pledge (consideration or reliance from BU's care of the papers). Note 2: § 90(2) eliminates the need to prove reliance, but courts are unlikely to ignore it entirely. The burden may shift to the defendant to prove its absence.

Reliance on offers (option by reliance)

  • § 87(2): an offer the offeror should reasonably expect to induce substantial action or forbearance before acceptance, which does so, is binding as an option contract to the extent necessary to avoid injustice.
  • Drennan v. Star Paving (majority) vs. James Baird v. Gimbel Bros (minority). See Option Contracts and Firm Offers.

Reliance in negotiations

  • Pops Cones v. Resorts International: New Jersey no longer requires a "clear and definite" promise. Assurances that "the deal is done" plus giving up a lease can support promissory estoppel. Note 1: many jurisdictions still require a clear and definite promise. Note 5: possible duty to negotiate in good faith.
  • Compare Quake Construction v. American Airlines (letters of intent) and Berryman v. Kmoch (reliance not reasonably expected).

Promissory estoppel and other doctrines

  • Statute of frauds (§ 139): Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice. See Statute of Frauds › Reliance and promissory estoppel.
  • Unilateral contracts: § 45 is a reliance-based device (Cook v. Coldwell Banker).
  • Promotions: Sateriale v. RJ Reynolds (promissory estoppel claim reinstated).
  • Employment and pensions: Plowman v. Indian Refining (did the retirees rely?).
  • Cohabitation: Watts v. Watts (could have pleaded promissory estoppel; she gave up a career).

Remedy

  • "The remedy granted for breach may be limited as justice requires" (§ 90(1)).
  • Walser v. Toyota Motor Sales: reliance (out-of-pocket, $232,131) rather than lost dealership profits, since the deal was "far from a certainty." Note 1: there is a split of authority. Note 3: a flexible approach. Note 4: a scholarly debate on how often expectation is awarded.
  • Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice: lost wages and benefits awarded (close to expectation).
  • Pops Cones v. Resorts International: reliance damages sought, though expectation was arguably provable (Note 4).

Exam framework

  1. Is there a bargain? If yes, use contract. If not (or if it is unenforceable), consider § 90.
  2. Find the promise (definiteness? jurisdiction's standard?).
  3. Reasonable expectation of reliance (foreseeability to the promisor).
  4. Actual, reasonable reliance (with authority issues).
  5. Injustice: the size of the reliance, the promisor's benefit, the availability of other remedies (restitution).
  6. Remedy: reliance vs. expectation.
