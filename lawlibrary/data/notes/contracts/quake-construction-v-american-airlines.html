Quick reference

Citation: 141 Ill. 2d 281, 152 Ill. Dec. 308, 565 N.E.2d 990 (1990) · Casebook: Knapp p. 75 · Topic: Agreements to Agree and Letters of Intent One line: Whether a letter of intent is binding depends on the parties' intent. Because this letter was ambiguous (it awarded the contract but had a cancellation clause and contemplated a formal contract), intent was a question of fact for remand.

Overview

The leading modern case on letters of intent. A letter of intent can be enforceable if the parties intended to be bound before signing a formal contract. If the letter is ambiguous on that point, extrinsic evidence comes in and the factfinder decides. The concurrence suggests a third possibility: a binding duty to negotiate in good faith.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Quake Construction, Inc. The bidder chosen as general contractor for the project. It wanted damages (including lost profits) after being terminated.
  • Defendants / appellants: Jones Brothers Construction Corp. (American's agent, which ran the bidding and sent the letter) and American Airlines, Inc. (the owner expanding its O'Hare facilities). They argued the letter was not a binding contract.

Facts

  • February 1985: American hired Jones to prepare bid specifications and award contracts for expanding its O'Hare facilities. Quake bid on the employee facilities and automotive maintenance shop project.
  • Jones orally told Quake it had won. Quake's subcontractors would not release their license numbers without signed subcontracts, so Jones, to induce Quake to sign them up, sent an April 18, 1985 letter of intent: "We have elected to award the contract for the subject project to your firm... A contract agreement outlining the detailed terms and conditions is being prepared and will be available for your signature shortly." The letter described the scope of work, stated a start date and the contract amount ([Outside knowledge: about $1,060,000]), and said Jones "reserve[s] the right to cancel this letter of intent if the parties cannot agree on a fully executed subcontract agreement."
  • Quake lined up subcontractors. Before a formal contract was signed, Jones and American terminated Quake's involvement.

Procedural History

  • Quake sued for breach of contract (and other counts). The trial court dismissed, finding the letter showed no intent to be bound absent a formal contract.
  • The appellate court reversed, finding the letter ambiguous.
  • The Illinois Supreme Court affirmed the appellate court and remanded for trial on intent.

Issue

Is a letter of intent that awards a contract but contemplates a formal written agreement, and contains a cancellation clause, a binding contract?

Holding

The letter of intent is ambiguous as to whether the parties intended to be bound before executing a formal contract. On remand, the trier of fact must consider extrinsic evidence to determine the parties' intent.

Reasoning

  • The test is intent. If the parties intended a formal contract to be only a memorial of an agreement already reached, the letter binds. If they intended no obligation until a formal contract was signed, it does not.
  • Factors courts consider (including from Chicago Investment Corp. and other authorities): whether the type of agreement is usually put in writing, whether it needs a formal writing for full expression, whether it has few or many details, whether the amount is large or small, whether it is a common or unusual contract, whether a standard form is widely used, and whether either party has taken action in reliance.
  • Language cutting both ways: "elected to award the contract," the scope, start date and price suggest intent to be bound. The reference to a contract "being prepared," and especially the cancellation clause ("if the parties cannot agree on a fully executed subcontract agreement"), suggest no intent to be bound before signing. Slides: the cancellation clause "cut against an intent to be bound."
  • Because the letter alone is ambiguous, extrinsic evidence is admissible and the question goes to the factfinder.
  • Concurrence: besides "binding now" and "not binding until signed," letters of intent may create a binding obligation to negotiate in good faith toward the final agreement. Drafters should avoid ambiguity. Counsel receiving such letters should assume they may lack contractual force.

Rule

A letter of intent is enforceable as a contract if the parties intended to be bound by it, even though they contemplated a later formal writing. Whether they did is a question of intent, decided first from the document. If that is ambiguous, from extrinsic evidence and circumstances.

Why It Matters

  • It is the modern counterpoint to Walker v. Keith: courts look to intent and context, not just completeness of terms.
  • It frames the three categories of preliminary agreements: (1) binding despite a contemplated writing; (2) not binding until executed; (3) binding duty to negotiate in good faith (concurrence; compare the Good Faith and Fair Dealing topic).
  • Drafting lesson (slides): state clearly whether the letter is binding.

Other Key Points

  • Hypo (slides): Waystar / Dunder Mifflin $8 million letter. Apply the Quake factors.
  • Remedies: if bound only to negotiate in good faith, damages are usually reliance-based, not lost profits on the deal ([Outside knowledge: the general view in the duty-to-negotiate cases]).
  • Related: Pops Cones v. Resorts International (promissory estoppel for reliance during negotiations).
