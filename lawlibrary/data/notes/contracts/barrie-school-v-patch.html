Quick reference

Citation: 401 Md. 497, 933 A.2d 382 (2007) · Casebook: Knapp p. 1090 · Topic: Liquidated Damages One line: Parents who pulled their child out of a private school after the withdrawal deadline owed the full year's tuition under a valid liquidated damages clause. A party protected by a valid liquidated damages clause has no duty to mitigate.

Added during the vault reorganization

This brief was written from the opinion as excerpted in Knapp pp. 1090-1098. There is no slide deck for this case. The Week 15 class note and the topic outline already discussed it, but it had no brief.

Overview

The course's principal case on liquidated damages. It applies the traditional test for telling a valid liquidated damages clause from a penalty, judges reasonableness at the time of contracting (the "single look"), and holds that the non-breaching party does not have to mitigate when a valid clause fixes the damages.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / petitioner: The Barrie School, a private, non-profit Montessori school in Silver Spring, Maryland. It sued for the rest of the year's tuition.
  • Defendants / respondents: Andrew and Pamela Patch, parents who re-enrolled their daughter for 2004-2005 and then withdrew her late.

Facts

  • The Patches signed a re-enrollment agreement for 2004-2005: a $1,000 non-refundable deposit plus a tuition balance of $13,490 in two installments.
  • § 3 (escape clause and liquidated damages): the parents could cancel without liability by certified letter received before May 31, 2004. After that, they were liable for "the entire year's charges," and withdrawal during the year would not release them.
  • On July 14, 2004 (44 days after the deadline), the Patches faxed a cancellation, demanded their deposit back, refused to pay the balance and enrolled their daughter elsewhere.
  • The school did nothing to fill the seat (no review of old applications, no advertising). Even so, it met its enrollment projections for the year and enrolled more students than expected.

Procedural History

  • District Court: the agreement and the liquidated damages clause were valid. There was no fraud and it was not a contract of adhesion. But the school's failure to mitigate was "fatal," so judgment went to the Patches. Their counterclaim for the deposit was denied.
  • Circuit Court: affirmed. The clause was valid and not a penalty, but a duty to mitigate still applied.
  • Court of Appeals of Maryland: reversed and remanded for judgment consistent with its opinion. Bell, C.J., dissented.

Issue

Does a non-breaching party have a duty to mitigate damages when the contract contains a valid liquidated damages clause?

Holding

No. When the parties agreed to a valid liquidated sum, the non-breaching party has no duty to mitigate. The school could recover under § 3.

Reasoning

  • Validity test (Maryland, three elements): (1) the clause provides "in clear and unambiguous terms for a certain sum"; (2) the sum is reasonably compensation for the damages anticipated by the breach; (3) it is a binding agreement made before the fact that "may not be altered to correspond to actual damages determined after the fact." The decisive question is the parties' intent: penalty, or agreed damages?
  • Damages were hard to estimate when the contract was made. The school budgets and hires around expected enrollment, and the effect of losing one student is very hard to isolate. One year's tuition was a reasonable estimate (testimony suggested it was likely less than the real cost of educating the child).
  • Reasonableness is judged at formation (single look). The court looks at the clause prospectively, from the time of contracting, not in hindsight after the breach.
  • Mitigation belongs to court-assessed damages. Mitigation helps a court measure actual damages. A liquidated damages clause is the remedy the parties chose in place of court-measured damages. Requiring mitigation would undo the certainty the parties bargained for.
  • No-actual-harm defense rejected. The Patches argued the school lost nothing because it over-enrolled. The court rejected this for the same reason as mitigation: it would negate the agreed clause and judge it in hindsight.
  • Not a contract of adhesion. The Patches were not forced to enroll their child. The escape clause was for their benefit, and it was an arm's-length transaction.

Rule

A liquidated damages clause that was a reasonable forecast of hard-to-estimate damages when the contract was made is enforceable even if actual losses later turn out lower, and the non-breaching party need not mitigate to recover the agreed sum.

Why It Matters

  • It is the leading course example of the single look (formation-time) approach, which contrasts with a second look at actual harm.
  • Dissent (Bell, C.J.): contract remedies are compensatory, not punitive (Restatement (Second) § 356 cmt. a). Reasonableness should also consider the actual loss after breach. Under § 356 cmt. b, if it is clear no loss occurred, a substantial fixed sum is unenforceable. Here the school met its enrollment projections, so there was "no empty desk" and a full year's tuition was out of proportion to any harm (Knapp pp. 1098-1100).
  • Restatement (Second) § 356(1) and UCC § 2-718(1) allow a clause that is reasonable "in the light of the anticipated or actual harm," which leaves room for the dissent's second look.

Other Key Points

  • Compare Lancellotti v. Thomas (a breaching party's restitution unless a valid liquidated damages clause controls) and the mitigation cases in Limits on Damages (Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge, Maness v. Collins).
