Big picture

  • Expectation damages are the usual measure for breach of contract. In some situations they are the only measure.
  • Goal: put the plaintiff in the position she would have been in had the contract been performed.
  • Normally this means awarding the profit she would have made had the contract been performed.

Doctrine outline

  • Usual formula: (1) the amount by which the value of defendant's actual performance was less than the value of the promised performance, minus (2) whatever benefits, if any, the plaintiff received from not having to complete his own performance.
  • Book formula: ED = loss in value + other loss − cost avoided − loss avoided.
  • Restatement §347 translation (from Crabby's):
    • Difference between contract price and market value = "loss in value."
    • Consequential or incidental damages = "other loss."
    • Rent the seller collected before resale = example of "loss avoided."
  • Employer suing employee (Lukaszewski): damages = difference between cost of a replacement and the contract price.
    • If a replacement with equivalent experience had been available, the employer could not recover the extra cost of a more experienced teacher.
    • Extra value from a more experienced replacement was "imposed upon" the employer, so it is not a benefit.
  • Specific performance: courts will not grant it for personal services contracts.

Tests and elements

  • Expectation damages checklist (apply in order):
    1. Loss in value = value of promised performance − value of actual performance.
      • Other loss (consequential/incidental).
    3. − Cost avoided.
    4. − Loss avoided.
  • When a nonbreaching seller gets direct damages: only if contract price is above fair market value (as in Crabby's). Otherwise the seller can resell at the same or a higher price.
  • When a nonbreaching buyer gets direct damages: only if contract price is below fair market value (buyer was gaining value from the purchase).
  • Proving market value: generally requires expert testimony — typically a real estate appraiser or someone familiar with comparable property.
  • Consequential/incidental damages for a seller: recoverable, but limited by foreseeability, certainty, and mitigation (covered later).

Cases

  • Crabby's Inc. v. Hamilton (p. 899): Buyers refused to close on a real estate purchase; seller prevailed. Court held a resale price from just under a year after breach, by highly motivated sellers, is substantial evidence of fair market value at breach. Court found the sale was within a reasonable time and not a "distress" sale. Formula: contract price − market value at time of breach.
  • Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski (p. 910): Employer's damages for employee's breach = cost of replacement − contract price. Board hired a more experienced, higher-salaried replacement; court rejected the employee's argument because no equivalent-experience teacher was available. "Any additional value the Board may have received from the replacement's greater experience was imposed upon it and thus cannot be characterized as a benefit." (p. 912)
  • Paragon Ventures, L.L.C.: named only (co-defendant buyer in Crabby's) — no separate brief; see Crabbys v. Hamilton.
  • Restatement §347: referenced as the general formula; slides translate Crabby's into it but do not quote the section text.

Examples and hypos

  • Case 1 (p. 898) — builder:
    • Facts: $200,000 contract; estimated cost $180,000; owner breaches when work partly done; owner paid $70,000; builder spent $95,000; builder resells $10,000 of materials.
    • Loss in value: $200,000 − $70,000 = $130,000.
      • Other loss: none apparent.
    • − Cost avoided: $180,000 − $95,000 = $85,000.
    • − Loss avoided: $10,000.
    • Damages: $35,000.
  • Case 2 (p. 898) — employee:
    • Facts: 2-year contract at $50,000/yr; wrongful discharge after 6 months; 3 months unemployed; $1,000 agency fee; new similar job at $45,000/yr.
    • Loss in value: $100,000 − $25,000 = $75,000.
      • Other loss: $1,000.
    • − Cost avoided: $0.
    • − Loss avoided: $45,000.
    • Damages: $31,000.

Exam angles

  • Run every damages fact pattern through the four-line formula: loss in value, other loss, cost avoided, loss avoided.
  • Watch for the "benefit imposed on plaintiff" issue (Lukaszewski): a replacement that is better than the contract promised does not reduce the breacher's liability if the upgrade was unavoidable.
  • Seller vs. buyer direct damages turn on contract price vs. fair market value — check the direction before computing.
  • Timing and proof of market value: argue whether a later resale is reasonable and non-distress, and whether expert testimony is needed.
  • Flag consequential/incidental damages but note they are limited by foreseeability, certainty, and mitigation.
