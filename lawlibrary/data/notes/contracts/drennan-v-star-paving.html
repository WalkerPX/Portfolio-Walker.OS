Quick reference

Citation: 51 Cal. 2d 409, 333 P.2d 757 (1958) (Traynor, J.) · Casebook: Knapp p. 265 · Topics: Option Contracts and Firm Offers, Promissory Estoppel One line: A subcontractor's bid, reasonably relied on by a general contractor in its own winning bid, becomes irrevocable through promissory estoppel. The sub must pay the difference to the next-lowest bidder.

Overview

The majority rule on reliance on offers, codified in Restatement (Second) § 87(2). Justice Traynor uses § 90 and an implied subsidiary promise (by analogy to § 45) to hold a sub's bid irrevocable once the general contractor relies on it. Some call this "offer estoppel."

Parties

  • Plaintiff / respondent: William A. Drennan. A general contractor who bid on the Lancaster school job using Star's paving bid. He wanted damages for Star's refusal to perform. [Outside knowledge: name and job.]
  • Defendant / appellant: Star Paving Co. The paving subcontractor that submitted the lowest paving bid and then refused to honor it, claiming an error.

Facts

  • On bid day, Star phoned in a paving bid ([Outside knowledge: $7,131.60]), the lowest received. Drennan used it in computing his general bid and listed Star as the paving sub, as required.
  • Drennan won the prime contract. The next day he went to Star, and Star said its bid was a mistake and refused to do the work at that price.
  • Drennan found a replacement paver at a higher price ([Outside knowledge: $10,948.60]) after searching.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The trial court awarded Drennan the difference in cost ($3,817 plus costs). The California Supreme Court affirmed.

Issue

Was Star's bid irrevocable after Drennan relied on it in submitting his general bid, even though Drennan had not yet accepted it?

Holding

Yes. Under promissory estoppel, Star's bid could not be revoked once Drennan reasonably relied on it. Drennan recovers the difference between Star's bid and the next-lowest sub's price.

Reasoning

  1. § 45 by analogy: contract law already protects reliance on unilateral offers. An offer may carry an implied subsidiary promise not to revoke once the offeree has begun the invited performance or relied.
  2. Reasonable expectation of reliance (§ 90): Star had reason to expect its bid might be the lowest and that Drennan would rely on it in his own bid, which would bind him. Drennan's reliance was reasonable and substantial.
  3. Star's interest: Star had its own business interest in Drennan's reliance, since it wanted the job. This was not an idle offer.
  4. The mistake defense fails: Drennan had no reason to know of Star's error. As between the two, the party who made the mistake bears the loss.
  5. Distinguishing Baird: Traynor declines to follow Hand's view that promissory estoppel is limited to gifts.

Rule

An offer that the offeror should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance of a substantial character by the offeree before acceptance, and which does induce it, is binding as an option contract to the extent necessary to avoid injustice (Restatement (Second) § 87(2)).

Why It Matters

  • It is the majority view and the source of § 87(2). It protects general contractors who are themselves bound by bids they submit relying on subs.
  • Limitations Traynor built in (Note 3, slides):
    1. An offer that expressly or by clear implication allows revocation before acceptance is not protected.
    2. The general contractor may not "shop around" or reopen negotiations to get a better bid. ([Outside knowledge: courts reason that the GC cannot both rely on the sub's bid and bargain it down.])
    3. If the sub made a bona fide mistake known to the GC, the bid is not enforceable.
    4. Mere estimates, not formal bids, are not enforceable.
  • Asymmetry: the sub is bound but the GC is not. Commentators debate whether this encourages bid shopping.

Other Key Points

  • Hypo 2, "The Low Bid" (slides): FastFloor bids $180,000, the GC wins, and FastFloor claims a math error and wants $230,000. Apply Drennan, then each limitation (a known error, "estimate not firm bid," shopping around).
  • Week 2 slides flag Drennan as using § 45 by analogy, linking back to Cook v. Coldwell Banker.
  • Related: Berryman v. Kmoch (option requires real consideration, or § 87); Pops Cones v. Resorts International (promissory estoppel in negotiations).
