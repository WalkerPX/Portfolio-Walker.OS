The short version

If both sides were wrong about a basic fact that changes the deal in a big way, the disadvantaged side may be able to cancel, unless it took on that risk. If only one side was mistaken, getting out is much harder.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Mistake.

What it's about

This is about being wrong about a fact that exists when the deal is made, not about guessing wrong on the future. (Unexpected later events are covered in Impracticability and Frustration (Plain English).)

Mutual mistake (both sides wrong)

The disadvantaged side can cancel if:

  1. both were mistaken about a basic assumption of the deal,
  2. the mistake has a material effect on the exchange, and
  3. that side did not bear the risk of the mistake.

Unilateral mistake (only one side wrong)

All of the above, plus either:

  • enforcing the deal would be unconscionable (shockingly harsh), or
  • the other side knew or should have known of the mistake, or caused it.

Who bears the risk?

You bear the risk if (a) the contract says so (for example an "as is" clause), (b) you knew you had only limited information and went ahead anyway ("conscious ignorance"), or (c) the court thinks it's reasonable to put it on you.

An everyday example

You sell a painting at a yard sale for $20. Both of you think it's a cheap print. It turns out to be an original worth $50,000. Can you undo the sale? Maybe not: yard sales are a place where both sides knowingly gamble on value, so the seller probably bore that risk.

The cases, in plain words

  • Sherwood v. Walker: the "barren cow" case. A cow sold for beef turned out to be pregnant and far more valuable, and the seller could cancel.
  • Lenawee County Board of Health v. Messerly: both sides were wrong about a hidden, illegal septic system, but the "in its present condition" clause put the risk on the buyers.
  • Gartner v. Eikill: a buyer could cancel when an unknown zoning restriction made the land useless for his purpose. This sits awkwardly next to Lenawee.
  • Donovan v. RRL Corp: a dealer's ad listed a car thousands below the intended price by mistake, and the dealer could cancel because holding it to the price would be unconscionable.
  • BMW Financial Services v. Deloach: a lender that carelessly sent the wrong judgment amount to a collection agency couldn't undo the settlement. It bore the risk of its own error.

Words you'll hear

  • Rescission: cancelling a contract and returning both sides to where they started.
  • "As is": a term putting the risk of defects on the buyer.
  • Basic assumption: a fact both sides took for granted as the foundation of the deal.
