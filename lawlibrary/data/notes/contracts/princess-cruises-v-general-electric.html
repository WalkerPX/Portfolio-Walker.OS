Quick reference

Citation: 143 F.3d 828 (4th Cir. 1998) · Casebook: Knapp p. 149 · Topic: UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation › Battle of the forms (UCC 2-207) One line: A ship repair contract was predominantly for services, so the common law (not UCC § 2-207) applied. GE's final quotation, accepted by Princess's conduct, governed, and its limitation of liability cut a $4.58 million verdict to $231,925.

Overview

The course case on why the UCC/common law choice matters in a battle of the forms. Under the common law's mirror image and last shot rules, GE's terms (excluding consequential damages and capping liability) governed. Under § 2-207 the outcome might have differed.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Princess Cruises, Inc. Owner of the SS Sky Princess. It sent the purchase order and wanted lost profits and other consequential damages from cancelled cruises.
  • Defendant / appellant: General Electric Co. The original manufacturer of the ship's main turbines, hired to inspect and repair them. It conceded breach but relied on its terms excluding consequential damages and capping liability at the contract price.

Facts

  • Princess scheduled routine inspection and repair for December 1994 and asked GE to perform services and provide incidental parts.
  • Princess sent a purchase order, whose reverse-side terms were favorable to Princess. GE replied with a Final Price Quotation, whose terms limited liability to the contract price and excluded incidental and consequential damages. ([Details on the exact sequence of documents are summarized from the opinion; see Knapp pp. 149-152.])
  • GE's work was faulty. The ship missed scheduled cruises, causing substantial lost profits.

Procedural History

  • Princess sued for breach of contract, breach of warranties and negligence. The district court applied UCC principles. In January 1997 the jury awarded $4,577,743 (including incidental and consequential damages).
  • GE's renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law was denied. GE appealed.
  • Fourth Circuit: reversed and remanded for entry of judgment of $231,925 (the maximum under GE's terms), with interest.

Issue

  1. Does the UCC govern a maritime contract for ship inspection and repair that included some parts?
  2. Under the applicable law, whose terms governed, and could Princess recover incidental and consequential damages?

Holding

  1. No. When the predominant purpose of a contract is services rather than goods, the UCC does not apply. Courts use common-law principles.
  2. Under the common law, the jury could only have awarded damages consistent with GE's Final Price Quotation, which barred incidental and consequential damages and capped liability. Since GE conceded breach and the cap amount, judgment is for $231,925.

Reasoning

  • Predominant purpose factors: the contract's language (the purchase order and quotation spoke of "services"), the nature of the business of the supplier, and the relative value of goods and services. Parts were a small fraction of the price. The contract was predominantly for services.
  • Common law offer and acceptance (mirror image rule): a reply that varies the offer is a counteroffer. GE's Final Price Quotation was the operative offer (or counteroffer), and Princess accepted by conduct when it allowed GE to perform. So GE's terms controlled.
  • The district court erred in instructing on UCC concepts (including § 2-207-type analysis and implied warranties).

Rule

  • Scope: mixed goods and services contracts are governed by the UCC only if their predominant purpose is the sale of goods.
  • Common law battle of the forms: under the mirror image rule, a non-conforming response is a counteroffer, and performance or acceptance of performance manifests assent to the last form sent (the "last shot" rule).

Why It Matters

  • The § 2-207 stakes (slides): under the common law, GE's liability-limiting "acceptance" is a counteroffer, and later conduct accepts GE's terms. Under § 2-207, Princess's purchase order would set the baseline, and GE's added limitation would become part of the contract only if it did not materially alter the deal. (Between merchants, a consequential-damages exclusion is typically a material alteration, Official Comment 4.)
  • So the scope question decides whose terms govern and whether GE's cap survives.
  • It is a practical lesson on classification and form drafting.

Other Key Points

  • Hotel elevator hypo (slides): consequential damages under § 2-207. Apply § 2-207(2) materiality and § 2-207(3) conduct.
  • BuyerCo / SellerCo arbitration hypo (slides): is an arbitration clause in an acknowledgment a material alteration?
