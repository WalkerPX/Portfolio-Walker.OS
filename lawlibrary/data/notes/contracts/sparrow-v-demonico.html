Quick reference

Citation: 461 Mass. 322, 960 N.E.2d 296 (2012) · Casebook: Knapp p. 583 · Topic: Capacity › Mental incapacity One line: A party claiming she lacked capacity to agree to a mediated settlement must offer medical or expert evidence that her condition affected her understanding or her ability to act reasonably. Lay testimony about an emotional breakdown was not enough.

Overview

The course case on mental incapacity. Massachusetts recognizes both the cognitive and volitional tests. Incapacity need not be long-standing or diagnosed, but medical evidence or expert testimony is required to prove it.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Frances M. Sparrow. She sued her sister over the family home in Woburn and wanted the mediated settlement enforced.
  • Defendants / appellees: Susan A. Demonico (her sister) and David D. Demonico (Susan's husband). Susan claimed she lacked capacity at the mediation.

Facts

  • A family dispute arose over ownership of the former family home in Woburn. Before trial, the parties reached a settlement agreement in voluntary mediation.
  • Susan later refused to honor it, claiming she suffered a breakdown during the mediation and was mentally incapacitated. [Outside knowledge: evidence included her emotional distress, a hospital visit afterward, and testimony from family members.]
  • No medical evidence or expert testimony linked her condition to an inability to understand the transaction or act reasonably.

Procedural History

  • The Superior Court judge denied Sparrow's motion to enforce, finding Susan lacked capacity.
  • The SJC granted further appellate review. It vacated the order and remanded for entry of an order enforcing the settlement.

Issue

Can a party establish lack of capacity to contract, making the contract voidable, without evidence of a medically diagnosed, long-standing mental illness or defect?

Holding

Incapacity does not always require proof of a long-standing, permanent, progressive or degenerative condition. But without medical evidence or expert testimony that the mental condition interfered with the party's understanding of the transaction or her ability to act reasonably in relation to it, the evidence is insufficient. Here it was insufficient, so the settlement must be enforced.

Reasoning

  • Two tests:
    1. Cognitive (traditional): the party could not understand the nature and consequences of the transaction.
    2. Volitional (modern): the party could not act in a reasonable manner in relation to the transaction, and the other party had reason to know of the condition (Restatement (Second) § 15(1)(b); see Ortelere).
  • Lay witnesses are not competent to opine on whether a mental condition affected someone's capacity.
  • There was no evidence the settlement was unreasonable or that a reasonably competent person would not have agreed to it.

Rule

A contract is voidable for mental incapacity if, because of mental illness or defect, a party (a) cannot reasonably understand the nature and consequences of the transaction, or (b) cannot act reasonably in relation to it and the other party has reason to know of the condition (Restatement (Second) § 15). Proof generally requires medical or expert evidence. An adjudicated incompetent's contracts are void.

Why It Matters

  • Note 1: Restatement § 15 includes both tests. The volitional test requires the other party's reason to know (Ortelere is the leading application).
  • Note 2, minors vs. mental incapacity: (a) a minor's capacity turns on age alone, while mental capacity turns on individual circumstances; (b) for mental incapacity, reliance by the other party may protect it if the contract was fair and made without knowledge of the incapacity (§ 15(2)). For minors, reliance has traditionally been disregarded.
  • It also shows policy favoring the finality of settlements.

Other Key Points

  • Mental incapacity via intoxication is a related doctrine (Restatement § 16): [Outside knowledge: voidable only if the other party has reason to know].
