Big picture

  • Chapter 9 asks when a performance obligation arises under a valid, nonavoidable contract.
  • Express conditions are a contracting device. Traditional view: strictly enforced, even if harsh.
  • Courts have tools to excuse nonoccurrence: interpretation, waiver, prevention, immateriality, excuse to avoid forfeiture.
  • Conditions vs. promises: different concepts; a provision can be both.
  • Constructive conditions (court-supplied) are covered in Section B; substantial performance tempers them — but not express conditions.

Doctrine outline

  • Definition (Restatement § 224): a condition is an event, not certain to occur, which must occur, unless its non-occurrence is excused, before performance under a contract becomes due.
  • Strict enforcement: express conditions must be strictly satisfied. Substantial performance is irrelevant to an express condition (Oppenheimer).
  • Clear language required: courts require clear language to establish an express condition. Restatement (Second) § 227: a debatable term should not be construed as an express condition if that would increase the risk of forfeiture. An express condition must be explicitly stated.
  • Condition vs. promise: whether a condition is also a promise is a question of interpretation (like whether an express condition exists at all).
  • Pay-if-paid vs. pay-when-paid: pay-if-paid clauses create express conditions; pay-when-paid clauses are only a timing mechanism (neither promise nor condition — they set the time for performance of a promise).
  • Waiver: a party can waive a condition included for its benefit. Some courts require waiver be clearly established. To eliminate a condition by waiver (rather than modification), the condition cannot be material. A party can waive an entire condition or only part.
  • Whose duties conditioned: a condition may condition both parties' duties or only one. Who may waive depends on whom the condition protects.
  • Prevention: a condition may be excused if the party it benefits wrongfully prevents its fulfillment. Viewed as an aspect of the general duty of good faith.
  • Immateriality: Restatement gives courts some flexibility to excuse conditions. Prof. Childres would excuse nonoccurrence whenever the condition is immaterial; the Restatement does not go that far.
  • Excuse to avoid forfeiture: most general doctrine to avoid failure of condition (J.N.A.).
  • Other excuses (slide 25–26): (1) beneficiary wrongfully interferes; (2) contracts conditioned on parties staying in business (e.g., output contracts); (3) waiver; (4) condition becomes impossible or impractical to fulfill, so long as it is not a material part of the contract.
  • Implied conditions: (1) performance — one party's performance hinges on the other's; (2) cooperation — duty conditioned on other party's cooperation; (3) notice — non-performing party must give notice that performance is due (most common where performing party could not reasonably know performance was due).
  • Constructive conditions: not agreed by parties; supplied by court for fairness; principal use in bilateral contracts. Each party's substantial performance is generally a constructive condition to the other's subsequent duties.

Tests and elements

  • Condition (Restatement § 224): event + not certain to occur + must occur (unless excused) + before performance becomes due.
  • Strict satisfaction checklist:
    • Is the term an express condition? (clear language; § 227 construes against condition if forfeiture risk rises)
    • If yes, was it strictly satisfied? (substantial performance insufficient)
  • Waiver checklist:
    • Was the condition for the waiving party's benefit?
    • Is the waiver clearly established?
    • Is the condition material? (if material, waiver cannot eliminate it — modification instead)
    • Entire condition or only part?
  • Prevention checklist:
    • Does the party benefiting from the condition wrongfully prevent fulfillment?
    • Good faith dimension.
  • J.N.A. three-part test for excusing notice condition (forfeiture):
    • (1) tenant would suffer forfeiture
    • (2) no showing of prejudice to landlord
    • (3) failure due to "venial inattention," not culpable misconduct
  • Excuse categories (slides 25–26): wrongful interference; going-out-of-business condition; waiver; impossibility/impracticality (if not material).
  • Implied conditions: performance; cooperation; notice.
  • Constructive condition: each party's substantial performance is a constructive condition to the other's subsequent duties.

Cases

  • Oppenheimer & Co. v. Oppenheim, Appel, Dixon & Co. (p. 827) — strict enforcement of express conditions. Plaintiff subleased old offices to defendant, subject to two conditions: (1) landlord's written consent to subtenancy (obtained, timely notice given); (2) defendant's receipt, by stated date (Jan. 30, extended in writing to Feb. 25), of landlord's written consent to telephone-link construction work. Plaintiff says it got consent and phoned defendant's attorney Feb. 25; attorney said he'd "get back to him"; next day said deal off — written approval not duly delivered, no extension. Jury: no waiver, no estoppel, but plaintiff "substantially performed" — $1.2M. Trial court: judgment n.o.v. (substantial performance irrelevant to express condition). Appellate Division: reversed, reinstated verdict. Court of Appeals: reversed, limiting discussion to substantial performance. Holding: no place for substantial performance where express conditions are concerned; express conditions must be strictly satisfied. Court did not squarely address waiver or estoppel (jury found neither).
  • Jacob & Youngs v. Kent — named only (distinguished in Oppenheimer; "later in this chapter") — now briefed: Jacob and Youngs v. Kent.
  • Maxton Builders — named only as example: written notice arriving a few days late did not satisfy an express condition for cancellation, even though the other party clearly knew of intent to cancel. Losing attorney held liable for malpractice. — now briefed: Maxton Builders v. Lo Galbo.
  • J.N.A. Realty Corp. v. Cross Bay Chelsea, Inc. (p. 840) — excuse of condition to avoid forfeiture. 10-year lease with option to renew for 10 years; notice required in writing within 6 months before expiration. Tenant assigned to defendant; modification agreement made renewal term 24 years; all other terms including notice provision remained. Defendant, apparently unaware, gave no notice until over 4 months late (after notice to vacate). Defendant argued forfeiture: loss of valuable improvements and good will. Holding: equitable relief excusing notice condition could be granted if (1) forfeiture, (2) no prejudice to landlord, (3) "venial inattention" rather than culpable misconduct. Strengthened by landlord's apparent knowledge of improvements made with expectation of extension. Remanded for retrial (never held); parties settled on increased rent for renewal period.

Examples and hypos

  • Oppenheimer three readings of the conditions: (1) plaintiff promised to obtain the two consents; (2) plaintiff promised to try; (3) plaintiff made no promise — consents were merely conditions.
  • Oppenheimer waiver analysis: telephone-work condition has three components — (i) landlord's consent, (ii) written notice of consent, (iii) receipt by Feb. 25. Consent was obtained; question is waiver of (ii) and/or (iii). Were writing requirement and deadline material or merely technical?
  • Retraction hypo: condition date Feb. 25; defendant waives Feb. 15. Retract Feb. 23? Retract Feb. 28? Does plaintiff's reliance matter?
  • Whose duties: landlord's consent to defendant as new tenant likely protects both parties (plaintiff liable for improper occupant; defendant might be evicted). Telephone-work condition likely protects only defendant — only defendant could waive it.
  • Prevention hypos: (a) plaintiff obtains consent but withholds notice because it wants out — is notice condition excused? (b) plaintiff never tries to obtain consent — is defendant harmed if landlord would have refused anyway?
  • Immateriality: were the conditions in Oppenheimer material?
  • Constructive condition example: insurer issues 1-year group health policy from Jan. 1; employer promises premium Feb. 15; insurer rejects 90% of claims in first six weeks without apparent justification; employer refuses to pay. Insurer's fulfillment is a constructive condition of employer's duty to pay. If insurer materially breached, premium obligation does not become due; employer can sue for damages (e.g., lost employee goodwill), and insurer cannot sue for the premium.

Exam angles

  • Express condition issue-spot: clear language? § 227 construction against forfeiture? Strict satisfaction vs. substantial performance.
  • Distinguish condition from promise; identify whether provision is both; run the three Oppenheimer readings.
  • Waiver: whose condition, materiality, clear establishment, whole vs. partial, retraction and reliance.
  • Prevention: beneficiary's wrongful interference; good faith.
  • Forfeiture escape hatch: apply J.N.A. three-part test.
  • Pay-if-paid vs. pay-when-paid: condition vs. timing.
  • Compare express conditions (strict) with constructive conditions/substantial performance (Section B).
  • Drafting: use "condition"/"on condition that"; identify whose duties are conditioned.
