Quick reference

Citation: 233 N.Y. 230, 135 N.E. 273 (1922) (Cardozo, J.) · Casebook: Knapp p. 350 (quoted in Beaver v. Brumlow), p. 355 (Note) · Topic: Statute of Frauds › Part performance (land) One line: The source of the strict "unequivocally referable" test: part performance takes an oral land contract out of the statute only if the acts point unmistakably to the contract.

Coverage

Not reprinted. Knapp quotes it in Beaver and discusses it in the Notes. The slides cite it only as "p. 365." Facts marked [Outside knowledge] are not in your materials.

Overview

Cardozo's classic statement of the strict part-performance test. Beaver later broadens it.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs: the Burnses [Outside knowledge]. They gave up their business and home to move in with and care for an elderly man who allegedly promised them his house at his death. They wanted specific performance against his estate.
  • Defendants: McCormick and others, representing the decedent's estate or heirs [Outside knowledge]. They opposed enforcement of the oral promise.

Facts

[Outside knowledge] An elderly man (Halsey) allegedly told the plaintiffs that if they gave up their business and moved in to care for him, his house and lot would be theirs at his death. They moved and cared for him until he died, but he left no will or deed in their favor.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The trial court enforced the oral promise and the Appellate Division reversed. The Court of Appeals affirmed the reversal: no enforcement.

Issue

Does the plaintiffs' performance (moving in and caring for the decedent) take the oral promise to convey land out of the statute of frauds?

Holding

No. The acts were not "unequivocally referable" to the alleged agreement to convey land. They could be explained by other arrangements, such as boarding, caregiving for wages or family kindness.

Reasoning

  • Quoted in Knapp (via Beaver): "Not every act of part performance will move a court of equity, though legal remedies are inadequate, to enforce an oral agreement affecting rights in land. There must be performance 'unequivocally referable' to the agreement."
  • The rationale: the part-performance exception is justified only when the acts themselves are evidence of the contract, so they must point to it and to nothing else.

Rule

(Strict version) Part performance takes an oral land contract out of the statute of frauds only if the performance is "unequivocally referable" to the alleged agreement, i.e., it cannot plausibly be explained otherwise.

Why It Matters

  • It is the origin of the test that Beaver v. Brumlow clarifies and broadens. Beaver holds the promisee need not show there is no "plausible explanation" other than a contract. It is enough that the performance would lead an outsider to "naturally and reasonably" conclude the contract exists (Knapp p. 355).
  • Two crucial factors (Knapp Note): whether the party seeking enforcement obtained possession, and whether it made improvements or payments.
  • Restatement (Second) § 129 moves further away from the test and focuses on reasonable reliance and justice.

Other Key Points

  • Exam use: if the only acts are services or moving (as in Burns), part performance is weak. Consider promissory estoppel or § 139, or restitution for the value of services.
