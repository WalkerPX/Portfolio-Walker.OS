Big picture

  • Anticipatory repudiation = before performance is due, one party makes clear it will not perform a contractual obligation.
  • Effect is tantamount to a total breach.
  • It is a potential excuse for nonperformance.
  • It is a potential ground for cancellation.
  • UCC § 2-609 (adequate assurance) was created by the Code drafters to remove uncertainties in the common law rules on breach.
  • Section C frames the topic two ways: as an excuse for nonperformance, and as a ground for cancellation.

Doctrine outline

Repudiation

  • Can be made with words or actions.
  • Must be a "definite and unequivocal" manifestation of unwillingness to perform (Flatt, Note 2).
  • A mere request for change in terms is not sufficient (Flatt, Note 2).

Cancelling on anticipated breach

  • Party seeking to cancel must reasonably anticipate the other party's breach.
  • If that party is wrong, the cancellation may itself be a breach.

Demanding assurances

  • A party may require the other party to provide assurances.
  • Failing to provide assurance may justify cancellation.
  • The party must allow a reasonable time for assurance.

Retraction

  • Until the repudiating party's next performance is due, it can retract.
  • No retraction if the aggrieved party has, since the repudiation:
    • cancelled, or
    • materially changed position, or
    • otherwise indicated it considers the repudiation final.

UCC § 2-609

  • An insecure party can demand adequate assurance that performance will be forthcoming per the contract.
  • Failure to give adequate assurance constitutes repudiation — an anticipatory repudiation that opens up remedies for the party who did not get the assurance.
  • The right protects a party whose prospects are undermined even though the other has not yet committed an anticipatory repudiation.
  • If a communication raises substantial doubts about willingness to perform but is not unequivocal enough to be a clear repudiation, the recipient has a basis to demand assurances — in effect requiring the other to resolve the ambiguity.
  • Failure to respond is the equivalent of a repudiation, and thus a total breach.
  • The right is permissive, not compulsory (text of UCC § 2-609 and Restatement (Second) § 251).
  • But on economic-efficiency and good-faith grounds, it seems reasonable to require the insecure party either to:
    • make the demand, setting forth the assurances it would be satisfied with, or
    • justify its failure to do so by showing why no assurances could have been adequate.

Tests and elements

  • Anticipatory repudiation (Flatt): "definite and unequivocal" manifestation of unwillingness to perform. CHECK: a mere request for change in terms fails this test.
  • Retraction (slide 4 quote): allowed until next performance is due UNLESS aggrieved party has cancelled, materially changed position, or indicated it considers the repudiation final.
  • UCC § 2-609 / Restatement (Second) § 251 — two elements:
    1. reasonable grounds for insecurity
    2. assurances of performance
  • Reasonable grounds for insecurity: courts generally require an objective change in financial condition — either a change in actual or apparent condition, or at least a change in the information known to the other party.
  • Cancellation checklist: reasonably anticipate breach → (if wrong, cancellation may itself be a breach) → may demand assurances → allow reasonable time → failure to assure may justify cancellation.

Cases

  • Truman L. Flatt & Sons v. Schupf (p. 870) — Plaintiff contracted to buy defendants' real property, conditioned on city council approval of its asphalt-plant application. Plaintiff wrote withdrawing the application and asking whether defendants would sell at a lower price. Defendants' attorney declined the offer and expressed "regret" the variance was not approved. Days later plaintiff wrote wanting to proceed at the original price; defendants refused, claiming the earlier letter repudiated. Trial court granted summary judgment for defendant; appellate court reversed and remanded. Held: plaintiff's letter was not unequivocal enough to constitute a repudiation; and in any event plaintiff retracted before defendants took action that might preclude retraction (materially changing position or even just declaring election to treat the repudiation as final).
  • Hornell Brewing Co. v. Spry (p. 879) — Seller/manufacturer sued its Canadian distributor for a declaratory judgment that the relationship was terminated with no continuing distribution rights. Spry was engaged in 1993 after little or no investigation, on an oral arrangement. By April 1994 Spry was seriously in arrears and sales were below expectations and Spry's projections. Hornell demanded Spry clear arrears; by letter it offered to resume shipments on credit up to a $300,000 balance if Spry stayed "current" (all invoices paid in 14 days). Spry obtained financing, paid the nearly $80,000 arrears, and simultaneously ordered $390,000+ of beverages. Hornell refused to fill the order until Spry provided evidence of an existing line of credit plus a personal guarantee (backed by a verifiable financial statement), or an irrevocable $300,000 letter of credit. Spry had apparently lined up credit but never responded; the arrangement was terminated. Court (a New York trial court), applying UCC § 2-609, treated Hornell's communications as essentially two separate demands for assurance. First demand (at ~$80,000 arrears) was justified by nonpayment, as Spry apparently conceded (a sizeable Spry check had also bounced). Spry argued paying in full removed the grounds for insecurity. Court: not so, for two reasons — (1) the order placed right after payment exceeded the credit Hornell had agreed to extend (whether that alone is a ground for insecurity is unclear; Hornell might have limited shipments to the $300,000 limit); and (2) Hornell had learned from various sources that Spry's operation was a shell: no staff, no facilities, no trucks. Second set of demands was not unreasonable, and Spry's complete failure to respond justified Hornell in treating Spry as having materially breached and terminating. Court admitted being influenced by additional damaging information about Spry not known to Hornell at the time (which could not have been part of Hornell's grounds for reasonable insecurity) and by Spry's "regrettable lack of credibility."

Examples and hypos

  • Flatt: letter withdrawing the zoning application plus asking for a lower price — held not unequivocal enough to repudiate; and retracted before defendants acted.
  • Hornell: arrears + bounced check = first justified demand; payment in full does not automatically cure insecurity when a new order exceeds the agreed credit limit and the buyer looks like a shell.
  • Hornell: silence in the face of a justified demand for assurance = equivalent of repudiation / total breach.
  • Slide 3 hypo (implicit): party cancels on anticipated breach but is wrong — the cancellation may itself be a breach.

Exam angles

  • Run the repudiation test first: was the manifestation "definite and unequivocal"? A request to change terms is not enough (Flatt).
  • If the communication is ambiguous, pivot to § 2-609: substantial doubts → demand assurances → failure to respond = repudiation = total breach (Hornell, Note 1).
  • Check retraction timing: before next performance due, and before cancellation / material change / indication repudiation is final (Flatt).
  • Check the insecure party's own conduct: did it allow a reasonable time? Did it specify the assurances it would accept, or justify not demanding (Notes 4–6)?
  • For insecurity, look for an objective change in financial condition or in the information known (Note 2/3).
  • Watch the Hornell caveat: a demand exceeding the agreed credit limit may not by itself be a ground for insecurity — flag the uncertainty.
  • Note the court's reliance on after-acquired information and credibility — a fairness overlay, not a stated element.
