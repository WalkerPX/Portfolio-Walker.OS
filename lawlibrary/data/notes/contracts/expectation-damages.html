In one paragraph

Expectation damages are the usual remedy for breach. They put the injured party in the position it would have been in had the contract been performed, which usually means awarding the profit it expected. Restatement § 347 formula: loss in value + other loss − cost avoided − loss avoided. In land sales, the measure is contract price vs. market value at the time of breach, and a reasonable, non-distress resale is good evidence of market value (Crabbys v. Hamilton). When an employee breaches, the employer recovers the extra cost of a replacement. An upgrade the employer couldn't avoid is not a "benefit" that reduces damages (Handicapped Childrens Education Board v. Lukaszewski). In construction, damages are normally the cost of completion, but diminution in value applies when completion would be wasteful (Jacob and Youngs v. Kent). Expectation is limited by foreseeability, certainty and mitigation (Limits on Damages).

The general formula (Restatement 347)

Damages = loss in value + other loss − cost avoided − loss avoided

  1. Loss in value: the value of the promised performance minus the value of what was actually received.
  2. Other loss: incidental costs (for example, resale costs or agency fees) and consequential losses (for example, lost profits on other deals), subject to Limits on Damages.
  3. Cost avoided: what the plaintiff saved by not having to finish its own performance.
  4. Loss avoided: what the plaintiff saved by salvaging, reselling or reallocating resources.

Worked examples (Knapp p. 898):

  • Builder: $200,000 contract, estimated cost $180,000. The owner breaches midway, having paid $70,000. The builder has spent $95,000 and resells materials for $10,000.
    • Loss in value $130,000 ($200,000 − $70,000) + other loss $0 − cost avoided $85,000 ($180,000 − $95,000) − loss avoided $10,000 = $35,000.
    • Check: expected profit $20,000 + unreimbursed costs ($95,000 − $70,000 − $10,000 = $15,000) = $35,000.
  • Employee: a 2-year contract at $50,000 a year, wrongfully fired after 6 months. Three months out of work, a $1,000 agency fee, then a similar job at $45,000 a year.
    • Loss in value $75,000 ($100,000 − $25,000) + other loss $1,000 − cost avoided $0 − loss avoided $45,000 (earnings from the new job over the remaining 15 months) = $31,000.

Sale of real estate

  • Seller's damages when the buyer breaches: contract price − market value at the time of breach, plus incidental and consequential damages, minus loss avoided (for example, rent collected before resale). The seller has direct damages only if the contract price exceeds market value.
  • Buyer's damages when the seller breaches: market value − contract price (only if the buyer had a good deal). The buyer often seeks specific performance instead, since land is unique (Specific Performance).
  • Proving market value: usually by expert appraisal or comparable sales, or by a later resale.
  • Crabbys v. Hamilton: the buyers refused to close. The seller resold just under a year later. Held: a resale by highly motivated sellers, within a reasonable time and not a distress sale, is substantial evidence of market value at the time of breach. Paragon Ventures was a co-defendant buyer, named only in the slides.

Employment contracts

  • Employer fires employee: contract salary for the remaining term − what the employee earned or could have earned in comparable work (mitigation) + incidental costs. See Limits on Damages › Mitigation in employment cases and Maness v. Collins.
  • Employee quits (employer's damages): the cost of a replacement − the contract price.
  • Handicapped Childrens Education Board v. Lukaszewski: a teacher quit mid-contract, claiming health reasons. The Board hired the only available replacement, who was more experienced and cost more. Held: the Board recovers the difference. "Any additional value the Board may have received from the replacement's greater experience was imposed upon it and thus cannot be characterized as a benefit." If an equally qualified, cheaper teacher had been available, the Board could not recover the extra cost. Dissent: health problems are a legitimate excuse.
  • No specific performance of personal services (Specific Performance › Personal services contracts).

Cost of completion vs. diminution in value

  • Default for defective or incomplete construction: cost of completion or repair, which gives the owner what it bargained for.
  • Exception: when the cost of completion is clearly disproportionate to the gain in value (economic waste), courts award diminution in market value (Restatement § 348(2)). [Outside knowledge] section number.
  • Jacob and Youngs v. Kent: the wrong brand of pipe, of equal quality, was sealed in the walls. Tearing out walls to replace it would be wasteful, so damages are the difference in value, which was nominal. Cardozo emphasized the builder's good faith. A willful breach might not get this leniency.
  • [Outside knowledge] Compare Groves v. John Wunder Co. (cost of completion despite small diminution, willful breach) and Peevyhouse v. Garland Coal (diminution of $300 vs. $29,000 in restoration costs). These are standard cases not in the slides. Cite them only as outside knowledge.

Exam framework

  1. Identify the interest the plaintiff will claim (expectation by default).
  2. Run the formula line by line: loss in value, other loss, cost avoided, loss avoided. Show the arithmetic.
  3. Context rules: land (contract price vs. market value at breach; proof by resale or experts); employment (replacement cost vs. salary minus mitigation); construction (cost of completion vs. diminution; good faith; waste).
  4. Limits: foreseeability, certainty, mitigation (Limits on Damages).
  5. Alternatives: reliance, restitution or specific performance if expectation is uncertain or inadequate.
