Quick reference

Citation: 350 F.2d 445 (D.C. Cir. 1965) (J. Skelly Wright, J.) · Casebook: Knapp p. 662 · Topic: Unconscionability One line: Courts may refuse to enforce unconscionable contracts. Unconscionability means "an absence of meaningful choice ... together with contract terms which are unreasonably favorable to the other party." Remanded for findings.

Overview

The leading case on the meaning of unconscionability. It recognizes the doctrine as a common-law defense in D.C. (before the UCC took effect there), and states the classic two-part formulation: procedural (absence of meaningful choice) plus substantive (unreasonably favorable terms).

Parties

  • Defendant below / appellant: Ora Lee Williams [Outside knowledge: first names]. A mother on public assistance ($218/month) who had bought household goods from the store since 1957. She wanted to stop the store from repossessing everything she had bought.
  • Plaintiff below / appellee: Walker-Thomas Furniture Co. A D.C. retailer selling on installment credit. It sought replevin of all items after her default.
  • (The opinion also decided a companion case, Thorne v. Walker-Thomas. [Outside knowledge])

Facts

  • Williams made many purchases on installment contracts with an "add-on" or "dragnet" clause: each payment was credited pro rata across all outstanding purchases, and the store retained title to every item until the balance on everything was paid to zero.
  • 1962: she bought a stereo for $514. The store knew her income and that she supported seven children. [Outside knowledge: the number of children.]
  • She defaulted. The store sought to replevy all items she had bought since 1957, even though she had paid far more than the price of most of them.

Procedural History

  • The trial court ruled for the store. The D.C. Court of Appeals affirmed, expressing sympathy but holding that unconscionability could not be used as a defense (no applicable statute).
  • D.C. Circuit: reversed and remanded for findings on unconscionability. [Outside knowledge: Judge Danaher dissented.]

Issue

May a court refuse to enforce a contract on the ground that it is unconscionable, absent a statute authorizing it?

Holding

Yes. Courts have the power to refuse enforcement of unconscionable contracts. Because the trial court did not consider the question, the case is remanded for findings. The court did not hold the contract unconscionable.

Reasoning

  • Common-law courts have long refused to enforce unconscionable contracts. Congress's later adoption of UCC § 2-302 in D.C. is persuasive authority for the doctrine.
  • Definition: "Unconscionability has generally been recognized to include an absence of meaningful choice on the part of one of the parties together with contract terms which are unreasonably favorable to the other party."
  • Meaningful choice: consider the circumstances of the transaction, gross inequality of bargaining power, whether the party had a reasonable opportunity to understand the terms, and whether important terms were hidden in fine print or minimized by deceptive sales practices.
  • Unreasonably favorable terms: judged in light of the general commercial background and the needs of the particular case, at the time the contract was made.
  • A party who signs with little bargaining power, without knowledge of the terms, hardly gives assent to all of them.

Rule

A court may refuse to enforce a contract or term that is unconscionable at the time it was made, meaning an absence of meaningful choice (procedural) plus terms unreasonably favorable to the other party (substantive).

Why It Matters

  • It is the foundation of the modern two-element test, applied on a sliding scale (more of one requires less of the other).
  • Note 1 (slides): procedural: door-to-door sales, blank forms, convoluted language easily meet the "hidden or unintelligible" form. Substantive: was the problem the add-on clause, or selling a stereo to someone on public assistance?
  • Paternalism (Leff): should courts refuse enforcement because a contract isn't in a party's best interest? The counter: when terms are buried in fine print or extracted by high pressure, the buyer didn't truly act voluntarily, so refusing enforcement gives effect to what the buyer actually understood.

Other Key Points

  • Remedies: Restatement (Second) § 208 and UCC § 2-302: refuse to enforce the contract, strike the unconscionable clause, or limit its application. Unconscionability is decided by the court as a matter of law, and is assessed at formation.
  • Hypo 1, "The Furniture Store" (slides): a cross-collateral clause on page 3 of a 4-page form. Analyze procedural (one form is enough?), the evidence on remand, and paternalism.
