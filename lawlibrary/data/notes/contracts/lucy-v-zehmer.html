Quick reference

Citation: 84 S.E.2d 516 (Va. 1954) (the Knapp cite); the official reporter cite 196 Va. 493 is outside knowledge · Casebook: Knapp p. 33 (Note 6 after Ray) · Topic: Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance One line: A seller who claimed he was "only joking" when he signed a deal to sell his farm over drinks was bound, because a reasonable buyer would have taken him seriously.

Coverage

Knapp does not reprint this opinion. It appears only in a note (p. 33) and is named again at p. 92. The slides name it without facts. Details marked [Outside knowledge] come from general knowledge of the case, not your materials.

Overview

The classic "jest" case. Under the objective theory, a secret intent to joke does not matter if the promisor's words and conduct would lead a reasonable person in the promisee's position to believe the offer was serious.

Parties

  • Plaintiff: W.O. Lucy (with his brother, J.C. Lucy) [Outside knowledge]. The buyer, who wanted to enforce the sale of the farm.
  • Defendant: A.H. Zehmer (and his wife) [Outside knowledge]. The seller, who wanted to avoid the sale by claiming it was a joke.

Facts

  • From Knapp: a land-purchase contract was made during "a conversation over drinks in a bar." The seller said he was only joking and thought the buyer was joking too. The parties' past dealings made it reasonable for the buyer to believe the seller was serious.
  • [Outside knowledge]: Zehmer wrote "We hereby agree to sell to W.O. Lucy the Ferguson Farm complete for $50,000.00, title satisfactory to buyer" on the back of a restaurant check. He rewrote it once and had his wife sign. The two had discussed the farm for a long time, and the writing was negotiated over about forty minutes. Lucy offered $5 to bind the deal and arranged financing the next day.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The trial court denied the Lucys specific performance. The Supreme Court of Appeals of Virginia reversed and remanded for entry of a decree of specific performance.

Issue

Is a party bound by a written agreement to sell land when he secretly meant it as a joke, but his words and conduct reasonably appeared serious?

Holding

Yes. The contract was enforceable. Per Knapp, it was reasonable for the buyer to believe the seller was serious, and the seller should reasonably have known that.

Reasoning

  • Objective manifestation controls. A person's undisclosed intention is immaterial when his outward expression shows assent (the theory stated in Ray v. Eurice Bros).
  • Context: the bar setting cut toward jest, but the prior dealings, the extended discussion, the rewriting and the signatures cut toward seriousness. [Outside knowledge]: the court found Zehmer was not too drunk to know what he was doing.
  • [Outside knowledge]: even if Zehmer was joking, the court said a person cannot set up a secret jest when his conduct warranted a reasonable belief in a real agreement.

Rule

An offer made in apparent seriousness is binding, even if the offeror privately intended a joke, if a reasonable person in the offeree's position would have understood it as serious and the offeree did so understand it.

Why It Matters

  • It is the standard illustration of the objective theory applied to jest and excuses of intoxication.
  • It pairs with Leonard v. Pepsico, the other side of the line, where no reasonable person would take the "offer" seriously.
  • Exam use: when a promisor says "I was kidding," ask what a reasonable person in the promisee's position would have believed, given the setting, prior dealings, the writing and the formality.

Other Key Points

  • Knapp cross-reference at p. 92 (Problem 2-3 material) asks you to "recall our earlier discussions of Lucy v. Zehmer and Leonard."
  • Slides (Week 1, slide 37): "buyer should have known seller was serious, despite the bar setting." Note the slide's phrasing: it is really that the seller should have known the buyer took him seriously.
