In one paragraph

In a bilateral contract, each party's substantial performance is a constructive condition of the other's duty to perform. A party who substantially performs, despite minor defects, can enforce the contract, less damages for the defects (Jacob and Youngs v. Kent). Only a material (substantial) deviation lets the other side withhold its own performance. Breaches run along a spectrum: partial (damages only), material (suspend performance and allow time to cure) and total (an uncured material breach that lets the other side terminate and sue for everything) (Sackett v. Spindler). Substantial performance does not rescue a failed express condition (Express Conditions › Strict compliance).

Substantial performance

  • Jacob and Youngs v. Kent (Cardozo, 1921): the contract called for Reading pipe. The builder installed pipe of equal quality from other makers, and the error was not intentional. Replacing it meant tearing out finished walls. Held: the builder substantially performed, so Kent could not withhold the final payment ($3,483.46). Damages are the difference in value, which was nominal, not the cost of replacement.
    • Rationale: "partly of justice and partly of presumable intention." Courts won't impose forfeiture for "venial faults."
    • Dissent [Outside knowledge]: the owner was entitled to what he bargained for. The breach was not trivial if it was knowing or careless.
  • Factors (Note 5; compare Restatement § 241):
    • the purpose of the provision;
    • the reason for nonperformance (especially good faith; willful breaches get less sympathy);
    • the cost of remedying the defect;
    • whether damages adequately compensate the injured party.
  • Constructive conditions are default rules (Note 2). Parties may contract around them by making a term an express condition (Note 3). Most modern commercial contracts do (p. 860).
  • Damages measure: see Expectation Damages › Cost of completion vs. diminution in value.
  • UCC contrast [Outside knowledge]: for goods, the perfect tender rule (§ 2-601) allows rejection for any nonconformity, softened by the seller's right to cure (§ 2-508) and the substantial-impairment standard for installment contracts (§ 2-612) and revocation (§ 2-608).

Partial, material and total breach

BreachWhat it isInjured party may
PartialNot significant (a short delay, a minor payment shortfall)Sue for damages; must keep performing
MaterialFailure of a significant obligationSuspend its own performance; give notice and a chance to cure
TotalMaterial breach not cured within a reasonable timeTerminate, sue for damages for the whole contract
  • Sackett v. Spindler: Sackett agreed to buy newspaper stock for $85,000 in installments. His check for the $59,200 balance bounced, and he made repeated unkept promises to pay. Held: total breach, so Spindler could terminate, resell and recover damages. As the breaching party, Sackett could not get his payments back, since they were offset by Spindler's damages.
  • Material → total (Sackett Note 3): key factors are the reasonableness of notice and the opportunity to cure. A material breach is not total unless further delay in terminating would unduly prejudice the injured party.
  • Note 1: Sackett seems to treat "material" and "total" as synonyms. The Restatement (Second) does not (§§ 241, 242).
  • Two separate questions: (1) May I withhold my performance? (Is the breach material?) (2) May I terminate and walk away? (Is it total?) Terminating too early can make you the breacher.
  • Breaching party's restitution: see Reliance and Restitution Damages › Restitution for the breaching party (Lancellotti v. Thomas).

Exam framework

  1. Constructive or express condition? Express → strict compliance (Express Conditions). Constructive → substantial performance.
  2. Was performance substantial? Apply the Note 5 / § 241 factors: purpose, good faith, cost to fix, adequacy of damages.
  3. If not substantial (material breach): the other side may suspend. Was there notice? A chance to cure?
  4. Did it become total? Has the cure period run? Would further delay unduly prejudice the injured party?
  5. Remedies: the substantial performer recovers the price minus damages, measured by difference in value vs. cost of completion. On total breach, the injured party recovers full expectation. The breaching party may get restitution under § 374.
  6. Goods? Perfect tender, cure, installment contracts [Outside knowledge].

Hypos (slides)

  • Non-Reading pipe in the walls, of equal quality, with high replacement cost → substantial performance, no forfeiture, difference-in-value damages.
  • A short delay or minor payment shortfall, absent other circumstances → partial breach.
  • Failure to tender the balance of the purchase price → material breach that becomes total once the cure period runs (Sackett).
