Quick reference

Citation: 21 Cal. 4th 543, 87 Cal. Rptr. 2d 886, 981 P.2d 978 (1999) · Casebook: Knapp p. 977 · Topic: Limits on Damages › Emotional distress One line: Homeowners whose "dreamhouse" leaked everywhere because of a contractor's negligence could recover repair costs but not emotional distress damages, under either a tort or a contract theory.

Overview

The course case on the general rule that emotional distress damages are not recoverable for breach of contract, and on the line between contract and tort remedies.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs / respondents: Barry and Sandra Erlich. Homeowners who wanted repair costs plus emotional distress damages.
  • Defendant / appellant: John Menezes. A licensed general contractor who negligently built their house.

Facts

  • The Erlichs contracted with Menezes to build a "dreamhouse" on their ocean-view lot and moved in in December 1990.
  • February 1991: the rains came, and "the house leaked from every conceivable location." Walls were saturated and the defects were extensive. Repair attempts failed. The emotional impact on the Erlichs was "enormous" (slides).

Procedural History

  • Jury: awarded the full cost of repair plus emotional distress damages for the contractor's negligent performance. [Outside knowledge: about $406,000 for repair and $100,000 to each spouse for emotional distress.]
  • Court of Appeal: affirmed the emotional distress award. [Outside knowledge.]
  • California Supreme Court: reversed as to emotional distress damages.

Issue

Are emotional distress damages recoverable for the negligent breach of a contract to build a house?

Holding

No. The contractor's negligence directly caused only economic injury and property damage and breached no duty independent of the contract. The homeowners may not recover emotional distress damages under either a tort or a contract theory.

Reasoning

  • Tort: a breach of contract is actionable in tort only "when the actions that constitute the breach violate a social policy that merits the imposition of tort remedies" (Note 2), e.g., fraud, conversion, or breach of a duty independent of the contract causing physical injury. Negligent construction causing only property damage does not qualify.
  • Contract: emotional distress damages are recoverable only when they are a likely result of breach in the parties' contemplation, typically when "emotional concerns are the essence of the contract" (e.g., contracts about funerals or the delivery of messages about death). A construction contract is fundamentally economic.
  • The court also noted the homeowners could have mitigated (e.g., by moving out).

Rule

Damages for emotional distress are generally not recoverable for breach of contract unless (1) the breach also caused bodily harm, or (2) the contract or breach is of a kind where serious emotional disturbance was a particularly likely result (Restatement (Second) § 353). Erlich applies an even narrower test: emotional concerns must be the essence of the contract.

Why It Matters

  • Note 2: when can a breach give rise to tort remedies? Only when the conduct violates a social policy meriting tort remedies.
  • Note 3: the court's suggestion that the Erlichs should have moved out seems unrealistic. Its tolerance for builders and lack of sensitivity to homeowners seem unwarranted on these facts.
  • Note 4: Erlich cites the Restatement test but seems to adopt a more restrictive rule (the "essence of the contract," p. 984).
