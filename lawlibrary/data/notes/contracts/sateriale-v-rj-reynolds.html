Quick reference

Citation: 697 F.3d 777 (9th Cir. 2012) · Casebook: Knapp p. 55 · Topic: Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance › Unilateral contracts One line: A cigarette company's "Camel Cash" rewards program could be an offer for a unilateral contract, accepted by buying, saving and redeeming certificates. The breach-of-contract claim survived dismissal.

Overview

A modern application of unilateral contract and advertisement-as-offer doctrine to a consumer loyalty program. It also holds that mutuality of obligation does not apply to unilateral contracts.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs / appellants: Sateriale and other consumers (putative class). Smokers who bought Camel cigarettes, enrolled, and saved "Camel Cash" certificates (C-Notes). They wanted the promised merchandise, or damages for their now-worthless certificates.
  • Defendant / appellee: R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. (RJR). It ran the program from 1991 to 2007, then stopped accepting certificates. It wanted dismissal: no offer, too indefinite, no mutuality.

Facts

  • RJR urged consumers to buy Camels, save the C-Notes included in packs, enroll, and redeem them for catalog merchandise.
  • In 2006, RJR gave about six months' notice that it would end the program. It then allegedly had no merchandise available to redeem during that window. Unredeemed certificates became worthless.
  • The plaintiffs sued for breach of contract, promissory estoppel, and violations of California's Unfair Competition Law (UCL) and Consumer Legal Remedies Act (CLRA).

Procedural History

  • The district court dismissed the third amended complaint for failure to state a claim (Rule 12(b)(6)).
  • Ninth Circuit: affirmed in part, reversed in part and remanded. Contract and promissory estoppel claims reinstated. UCL and CLRA dismissals affirmed for insufficient allegations of fraud and reliance.

Issue

Did the plaintiffs plausibly allege that RJR's Camel Cash program was an offer for a unilateral contract that they accepted by performance, despite RJR's arguments based on the advertisement rule, indefiniteness and mutuality?

Holding

Yes. The complaint adequately alleged an offer to enter into a unilateral contract, accepted when plaintiffs bought cigarettes and saved and tendered certificates. The breach of contract and promissory estoppel claims survive.

Reasoning

  • Offer: the court doubted whether the general "ads aren't offers" rule even holds under California law. Even if it does, the exception is broad enough to cover this case, citing Izadi (Knapp p. 38). RJR's materials invited performance and promised a reward, language of commitment rather than an invitation to negotiate.
  • Acceptance: by purchasing, saving and redeeming C-Notes, the plaintiffs performed the acts requested.
  • Indefiniteness: left for a later stage (slides).
  • Mutuality of obligation: "easily rejected." The doctrine does not apply to unilateral contracts, because in a unilateral contract only one party is ever bound (Asmus v. Pacific Bell).
  • Statute of limitations argument rejected. Promissory estoppel stands pending further proceedings.

Rule

A promotional program that promises a reward in exchange for specified consumer conduct can be an offer for a unilateral contract, accepted by performance. Mutuality of obligation is not required for unilateral contracts.

Why It Matters

  • It shows advertisements and promotions can be offers when they invite performance and show commitment. Contrast Leonard v. Pepsico and Lonergan v. Scolnick.
  • It ties unilateral contracts to revocation issues: can a program sponsor end a program midstream? (See Cook v. Coldwell Banker and Restatement § 45.)
  • It is the model for Problem 2-2 (GOC gas station rewards). Unlike GOC, RJR sought no return promise, so the bilateral theory did not arise.

Other Key Points

  • Problem 2-2 discussion (slides): a bilateral theory may work if GOC sought a return promise ("by participating, you agree to comply"), since GOC is "master of the offer." But the immediate "not an offer" notice may act as revocation (compare Normile v. Miller). On the unilateral theory, the 100-gallon buyer has essentially completed performance, the 1-gallon buyer has not.
