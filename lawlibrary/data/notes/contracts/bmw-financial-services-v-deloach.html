Quick reference

Citation: 2017 WL 1832250 (Cal. Ct. App. 2017) · Casebook: Knapp p. 754 · Topic: Mistake › Unilateral mistake One line: A creditor that mistakenly sent a $114,000 judgment to a collection agency, which settled for $14,000, could not rescind for unilateral mistake. It bore the risk of its own error, and losing a treble-damages windfall was not unconscionable.

Overview

The course case on unilateral mistake under Restatement (Second) § 153 (as adopted in California via Donovan). Relief requires, among other things, either that enforcement be unconscionable or that the other party knew or caused the mistake, and that the mistaken party not bear the risk.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: BMW Financial Services NA, LLC. The judgment creditor. It wanted to rescind the settlement and enforce the full judgment.
  • Defendant / respondent: Frank Deloach. The judgment debtor. With his father's help he settled with the collection agency and wanted the judgment marked satisfied.

Facts

  • Deloach defaulted on a BMW car lease, and the car was repossessed with an altered odometer. BMW Financial obtained a default judgment of about $114,000, including treble damages.
  • Through a clerical error, BMW Financial sent the account to a collection agency, which, unaware of the judgment, settled with Deloach (through his father) for $14,000.
  • BMW Financial tried to rescind the settlement for unilateral mistake. Deloach moved to compel acknowledgment of satisfaction of judgment.

Procedural History

  • The trial court granted Deloach's motion (judgment satisfied).
  • Court of Appeal: affirmed. [Outside knowledge: affirmed, consistent with Knapp's description that relief was denied.]

Issue

May a party rescind a settlement for its own unilateral clerical mistake that led its agent to settle a large judgment for a fraction of its value?

Holding

No. BMW Financial bore the risk of the mistake, and enforcing the settlement was not unconscionable.

Reasoning

Applying the four-element test (from Donovan v. RRL Corp / Restatement § 153), per the slides:

  1. Mistake as to a basic assumption: yes. BMW Financial did not know the judgment existed when settling through the agency.
  2. Material adverse effect: yes. $14,000 instead of $114,000.
  3. Risk of mistake: BMW Financial bears the risk. The mistake was entirely its own, and Deloach and the agency had no fault and no reason to know.
  4. Unconscionable enforcement: no. BMW Financial was not losing out-of-pocket money. It was mostly losing treble damages (a windfall, "extra cash"). Compare Donovan, where the seller would have had to sell a car far below value.
  • Knapp: in § 153(a), "unconscionable" seems to mean "severe enough to cause substantial loss." The court also weighed the nature of the mistake, the delay in discovering it, and Deloach's reasonable reliance on the agency's authority.

Rule

A party may avoid a contract for unilateral mistake only if (1) the mistake concerns a basic assumption, (2) it has a material effect on the exchange, (3) the party does not bear the risk (§ 154), and (4) either (a) enforcement would be unconscionable or (b) the other party had reason to know of the mistake or caused it (Restatement (Second) § 153). Under § 157, fault short of gross negligence or bad faith does not bar relief.

Why It Matters

  • Note 4, Donovan vs. BMW: both were administrative mistakes that better procedures might have prevented. The difference is that BMW Financial was more at fault and its "loss" was largely a windfall, not actual value.
  • Note 5: many unilateral mistake cases involve some negligence. Only gross negligence or a failure to meet good-faith standards bars relief entirely (§ 157).
  • Mistake vs. misrepresentation (slides): the doctrines overlap when one party's statements contribute to the other's mistake (see Hill v. Jones).

Other Key Points

  • Hypo 2, "The Settlement Trap" (slides): a billing error closes an account and a mediator settles for $30,000 on $400,000 of lost-profits exposure. Apply § 153 and compare BMW and Donovan.
