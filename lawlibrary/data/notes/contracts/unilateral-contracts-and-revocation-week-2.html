Big picture

  • Unilateral contract = offeror's promise exchanged for offeree's act, not a promise.
  • Offeree makes no promise; offeree simply acts.
  • Core issue: when does the offeree's beginning of performance block revocation?
  • Classic problem: pure offer-and-acceptance let offeror revoke until performance was finished — even near the end.
  • Fix (Restatement (First) § 45): once offeree begins performance, power to revoke is suspended — an option contract is created.
  • Restatement (Second) drops the label "unilateral contract"; describes an offer inviting acceptance only by performance, not by promise. Same idea, different label.

Doctrine outline

  • Unilateral offer defined: exchange of offeror's promise for offeree's act. Offeree does not promise; offeree acts.
  • Example: "If you run 5 miles, I promise to pay you $1,000 when you finish" — promise exchanged for act.
  • Revocation rule (§ 45): beginning performance suspends the offeror's power to revoke; creates an option contract.
  • Substantial performance: by the time offeree has substantially performed, offer can no longer be revoked (Cook).
  • Mutuality of obligation: not required for unilateral contracts (Sateriale).
  • Ads as offers: courts doubt the general "ads aren't offers" rule (California); even if true, the exception is broad enough to cover a rewards-certificate program (Sateriale, citing Izadi).
  • Master of the offer: offeror's own drafting controls whether it sought a promise (bilateral) or an act (unilateral) — Problem 2-2.
  • "Not an offer" language: may operate as revocation before promissory language is read (Problem 2-2, comparing Normile v. Miller).

Tests and elements

  • Unilateral contract formation checklist:
    • Offeror makes a promise.
    • Offer invites acceptance only by performance, not by a return promise.
    • Offeree performs the requested act.
  • Revocation-bar checklist (§ 45 / Cook):
    • Offer is unilateral.
    • Offeree has begun performance (at minimum).
    • Offeree has substantially performed (Cook's ground).
    • → Offeror's power to revoke is suspended; option contract created.
  • Sateriale formation hurdles (checklist):
    • Was there an offer?
    • Sufficient definiteness?
    • Mutuality? (easily rejected — not required for unilateral contracts)
  • Bilateral-vs-unilateral test (Problem 2-2): did the offeror seek a return promise ("by participating, you agree to comply…")? If yes → bilateral theory. If it sought an act → unilateral.

Cases

  • Cook v. Coldwell Banker/Frank Laiben Realty Co. — Plaintiff agent; firm announced year-end bonus at specified rates (March 1991). By September she qualified for 30% bonus; told bonuses paid at March 1992 banquet and she'd need to still be with firm. She kept selling through 1991, left January 1992; denied bonus over $17,000 (more than half earned after September conversation). Holding: plaintiff won at trial (full commission + prejudgment interest), affirmed. Citing Williston, Corbin, and Restatement (First) § 45: by September she had substantially performed under defendant's unilateral-contract offer, so the offer could no longer be revoked.
  • Sateriale v. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. — Class action; RJR gave ~6 months' notice it would end "Camel Cash" (C-Notes) redemption program, then allegedly failed to stock merchandise during the window. Ninth Circuit, appeal from Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal — reverses and remands. Holding: by purchasing, saving, and redeeming C-Notes, plaintiffs performed the acts requested — sufficient for formation of a unilateral contract. Indefiniteness left for later stage; mutuality objection easily rejected.
  • Drennan v. Star Paving Co. — named only (referenced as later material: court uses § 45 by analogy in a promissory estoppel analysis) — now briefed: Drennan v. Star Paving.
  • Izadi — named only (cited at p. 38 for the breadth of the ads-aren't-offers exception) — now briefed: Izadi v. Machado Ford.
  • Normile v. Miller — named only (cited for "you snooze, you lose") — now briefed: Normile v. Miller.

Examples and hypos

  • Slide 4 hypo: A to B: "If you run 5 miles, I promise to pay you $1,000 as soon as you finish." Promise exchanged for act = unilateral contract.
  • Problem 2-2 — Gas Station Rewards Program: GOC promotion; customers who buy gas register on website for chance at free 3-day WalleyWorld pass, subject to posted terms (including user promise to comply with restrictions). Three groups bought different amounts (1 gallon; 50+; 100+) before GOC revoked.
    • Bilateral theory: did GOC seek a return promise ("by participating, you agree to comply…")? Compare Sateriale (RJR sought no such promise). GOC may be bound by its own drafting — "master of the offer."
    • Revocation wrinkle: users see "not an offer" language immediately on the site — arguable revocation before any promissory language is read. Compare Normile v. Miller ("you snooze, you lose").
    • Unilateral theory (§ 45): GOC invites performance (buy gas). 100-gallon buyers have essentially completed performance; 1-gallon purchaser is a much harder case — "substantial performance" is doing a lot of work.

Exam angles

  • Spot whether the offer seeks a promise (bilateral) or an act (unilateral) — look at the offeror's own words; offeror is master of the offer.
  • On revocation: ask when performance began and how much was done — beginning suspends revocation (§ 45); substantial performance locks it (Cook).
  • Use § 45 to create an option contract once performance begins.
  • Reject mutuality-of-obligation objections to unilateral contracts (Sateriale).
  • Watch for "not an offer" / reservation language as a possible revocation before acceptance (Problem 2-2).
  • Definiteness can be deferred at the pleading stage (Sateriale) — don't conflate it with formation by performance.
  • Remedies vocabulary to deploy: expectation (full performance position), reliance (back to pre-promise position), restitution (return benefit conferred on breacher).
