In one paragraph

An anticipatory repudiation is a definite and unequivocal statement or act, before performance is due, showing that a party will not perform. It is treated as a total breach. The other party may suspend its own performance, cancel and sue right away. A request to change terms is not a repudiation (Truman L. Flatt and Sons v. Schupf). The repudiating party may retract until its next performance is due, unless the other side has cancelled, materially changed position or said it treats the repudiation as final. When conduct falls short of repudiation but gives reasonable grounds for insecurity, the insecure party may demand adequate assurance (UCC § 2-609; Restatement § 251). Failing to give it within a reasonable time is a repudiation (Hornell Brewing v. Spry).

Repudiation

  • Words or conduct may repudiate. The test is a "definite and unequivocal" manifestation that the party won't perform (Flatt Note 2).
  • Not enough: a mere request for a change in terms, an expression of doubt, or a hint at financial trouble.
  • Restatement § 250 [Outside knowledge]: a statement that the obligor will commit a breach that would itself give a claim for total breach, or a voluntary affirmative act that makes performance impossible.
  • Effects:
    • The injured party may treat it as a total breach, suspend its own performance, cancel, and sue immediately (no need to wait for the performance date).
    • Or it may wait a commercially reasonable time and urge performance (UCC § 2-610). [Outside knowledge] It must still mitigate (Limits on Damages › Mitigation (avoidable consequences); compare Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge, where the county repudiated and the builder kept working).
  • Risk of being wrong: a party that cancels based on an anticipated breach that turns out not to be a repudiation has itself breached.

Retraction (UCC § 2-611; Restatement § 256 [Outside knowledge: section numbers]):

  • A repudiation can be retracted until the repudiating party's next performance is due, unless the aggrieved party has since:

    • cancelled;
    • materially changed position in reliance; or
    • otherwise indicated that it considers the repudiation final.

  • Truman L. Flatt and Sons v. Schupf: Flatt agreed to buy land, conditioned on getting zoning approval for an asphalt plant. Facing opposition, Flatt withdrew its zoning application and wrote asking whether the sellers would accept a lower price. The sellers declined. Flatt then said it would go ahead at the original price, and the sellers refused, calling the first letter a repudiation. Held: the letter was not unequivocal enough to be a repudiation. In any event, Flatt retracted before the sellers cancelled, changed position or declared the repudiation final. Summary judgment for the sellers was reversed.

Adequate assurance (UCC 2-609)

Elements (UCC § 2-609 / Restatement § 251):

  1. Reasonable grounds for insecurity about the other party's performance. Courts generally look for an objective change in the other party's actual or apparent financial condition, or in the information known about it.
  2. A demand for adequate assurance of due performance. Under the UCC it must be in writing. [Outside knowledge] the writing requirement in § 2-609(1).
  3. Until assurance arrives, the insecure party may suspend any performance it hasn't yet been paid for, if commercially reasonable.
  4. Failure to provide adequate assurance within a reasonable time (at most 30 days under the UCC [Outside knowledge]) is a repudiation, and so a total breach.
  • Purpose: to resolve ambiguity. If a communication raises substantial doubt but isn't a clear repudiation, the recipient can force the other side to clarify.
  • Permissive, not mandatory: the text of § 2-609 and § 251 lets the insecure party demand assurance but doesn't require it. On efficiency and good-faith grounds, though, it is sensible to expect the insecure party to either (a) make the demand and say what would satisfy it, or (b) show why no assurance could have been adequate (Notes 4-6).
  • Adequacy depends on the circumstances and commercial standards. Between merchants, commercial standards govern.
  • Hornell Brewing v. Spry: Hornell (maker of Arizona Iced Tea [Outside knowledge]) gave Spry, a Canadian distributor, an oral distributorship after little investigation. Spry fell seriously behind on payments, and a sizeable check bounced. Hornell offered credit up to $300,000 if Spry stayed current. Spry paid nearly $80,000 in arrears but immediately ordered more than $390,000 of product. Hornell, having learned Spry was essentially a shell (no staff, facilities or trucks), demanded proof of a credit line plus a personal guaranty, or a $300,000 letter of credit. Spry never responded. Held: (1) the first demand was justified by the nonpayment; (2) paying in full did not remove the grounds for insecurity, given the over-limit order and the shell information; (3) silence in response to a justified demand = repudiation, so Hornell properly terminated. The court admitted it was influenced by information Hornell didn't know at the time and by Spry's lack of credibility.
  • Open question (Hornell): whether an order over the credit limit is, by itself, a ground for insecurity. Hornell could have simply capped shipments at $300,000.

Exam framework

  1. Was there a repudiation? Words or conduct, definite and unequivocal? A request to renegotiate or an expression of doubt is not enough (Flatt).
  2. If yes: the injured party's options are to suspend, cancel and sue now (total breach), or wait a reasonable time. It must still mitigate.
  3. Retraction? Before the next performance is due, and before cancellation, a material change of position or a declaration that the repudiation is final?
  4. If not a clear repudiation: are there reasonable grounds for insecurity? (An objective change in finances or information.)
  5. Demand for assurance: in writing (UCC)? Does it say what would satisfy? Is it reasonable, not excessive? Was a reasonable time (≤ 30 days) allowed?
  6. Response: adequate assurance → performance continues. None or inadequate → repudiation → total breach.
  7. Warning: a party that cancels too early may be the one in breach.
  8. Common law: Restatement § 251 extends the assurance idea beyond goods. Note that some jurisdictions limit it. [Outside knowledge]

Hypos (slides)

  • Flatt: withdrawing the zoning application and asking for a lower price → not unequivocal; retracted in time.
  • Hornell: arrears plus a bounced check → a justified first demand. Paying in full doesn't cure insecurity when a new order exceeds the credit limit and the buyer looks like a shell. Silence → repudiation.
  • Slide 3 (implicit): a party that cancels on an anticipated breach and is wrong has itself breached.
