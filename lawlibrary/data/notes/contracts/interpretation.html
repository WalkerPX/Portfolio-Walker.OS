In one paragraph

Interpretation decides what the words of a contract mean. The objective theory governs: meaning is what a reasonable person in the other party's position would understand, not a party's secret intent (Ray v. Eurice Bros, Lucy v. Zehmer). Courts split on when outside evidence may be used. Plain meaning (textualist) courts look first at the text and use extrinsic evidence only if the text is ambiguous. Contextualist (Corbin) courts consider the evidence first and ask whether the language is "reasonably susceptible" to the offered meaning (Taylor v. State Farm, Shay v. Aldrich). Under the UCC, course of performance, course of dealing and usage of trade always help explain terms (§ 1-303). Interpretation is closely tied to the Parol Evidence Rule: evidence offered to interpret is not barred by it.

The objective theory

  • Meaning turns on outward manifestations, read the way a reasonable person would read them (Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance).
  • Ray v. Eurice Bros: a party who signed a contract is bound by its objective meaning, even if they misunderstood it.
  • Lucy v. Zehmer, Leonard v. Pepsico: jest claims are judged objectively.

Plain meaning vs. contextual approaches

ApproachWhen extrinsic evidence comes inCases
Plain meaning / four corners (textualist)Only if the judge first finds the text ambiguous on its faceThompson v. Libby (PER analog); the Shay dissent
Contextual / CorbinJudge provisionally hears it to decide whether the text is reasonably susceptible to the offered meaningTaylor v. State Farm; the Shay majority
  • Taylor v. State Farm: a general release for uninsured-motorist benefits. Extrinsic evidence was admitted on whether the release covered a later bad-faith claim, and "plain meaning" was rejected.
  • Shay v. Aldrich (4-3): evidence was admitted to show a release was meant to cover only the two named officers.

Ambiguity

  • Patent ambiguity: obvious on the face of the document. Latent ambiguity: appears only when the words are applied to the facts (existing note: PER exam angles).
  • [Outside knowledge] In Raffles v. Wichelhaus (the two ships Peerless), when each party reasonably attached a different meaning to a latently ambiguous term and neither knew or should have known of the other's meaning, there is no contract. Restatement § 201: if one party knew or had reason to know the other's meaning, that meaning controls.
  • Whether a term is ambiguous is a question of law. What it means, once found ambiguous, is often a question of fact. [Outside knowledge]

UCC sources of meaning (§ 1-303)

  • Course of performance: how the parties performed under this contract.
  • Course of dealing: how they dealt in earlier transactions.
  • Usage of trade: practices regularly observed in the trade.
  • Hierarchy [Outside knowledge]: express terms > course of performance > course of dealing > usage of trade.
  • These come in to explain or supplement even a fully integrated writing (§ 2-202; Parol Evidence Rule › Exceptions).

Interpretive maxims [Outside knowledge]

  • Contra proferentem: ambiguity is construed against the drafter (Restatement § 206). Especially relevant to standard forms (Unconscionability).
  • Specific terms control general ones. Handwritten terms control printed ones.
  • Read the contract as a whole and give effect to every term.
  • Prefer an interpretation that makes the contract lawful and reasonable.

Gap-filling vs. interpretation

  • When the contract is silent rather than ambiguous, courts imply terms: good faith, reasonable efforts, reasonable notice (Good Faith and Fair Dealing), and UCC gap-fillers (UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation).

Exam framework

  1. Identify the disputed term and each side's reading.
  2. Objective theory: what would a reasonable person in the other party's position understand?
  3. Is the term ambiguous? Analyze under plain meaning and under Corbin's "reasonably susceptible" test.
  4. What evidence? Negotiations, purpose, course of performance or dealing, trade usage. Raise PER issues for prior agreements.
  5. Maxims and tiebreakers: contra proferentem, specific over general.
  6. If both meanings are reasonable and neither party knew the other's → possibly no contract (Raffles) [Outside knowledge].
