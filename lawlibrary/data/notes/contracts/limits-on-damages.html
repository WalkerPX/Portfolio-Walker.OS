In one paragraph

Expectation damages give the plaintiff "as good as, but no better than" performance. Several limits apply. Foreseeability: only losses that arise naturally from the breach, or from special circumstances communicated when the contract was made, are recoverable (Hadley v. Baxendale; § 351). Lost profits can satisfy it (Florafax v. GTE Market Resources). Certainty: the loss must be provable with reasonable certainty (§ 352), and only net profits count. Causation: the breach must cause the loss. Mitigation: there is no recovery for loss the plaintiff could have avoided with reasonable effort (§ 350; Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge). Employees need only seek comparable work, and the employer must prove it was available (Maness v. Collins). Attorney fees aren't recoverable under the American rule without a contract, statute or court rule (Zapata Hermanos v. Hearthside Baking). Emotional distress is generally not recoverable in contract (Erlich v. Menezes).

Foreseeability (Hadley)

Hadley two-prong test: damages are recoverable if they

  1. arise naturally from the breach (in the usual course of things) ("general" damages); or
  2. result from special circumstances communicated to the breaching party when the contract was made ("special" or consequential damages).

Restatement § 351:

  • (1) No recovery for loss the breaching party had no reason to foresee as a probable result of breach when the contract was made.

  • (2) Loss is foreseeable if it follows (a) in the ordinary course of events, or (b) from special circumstances the breaching party had reason to know.

  • (3) A court may limit even foreseeable damages (for example, exclude lost profits or allow only reliance) to avoid disproportionate compensation.

  • Hadley v. Baxendale (Eng. 1854): the mill's crankshaft broke. The carrier delivered the shaft to the maker for a pattern late, and the mill sat idle. Lost profits were not recoverable: the carrier wasn't told the mill would be stopped without the shaft, and mills often had spares. [Outside knowledge] the year and the spare-shaft reasoning come from general knowledge.

  • Florafax v. GTE Market Resources: GTE failed to supply enough telemarketing staff (especially over Mother's Day weekend in 1990). Florafax's contract with Bellerose was cancelled. Lost profits on the Bellerose deal were recoverable: GTE knew it would serve Florafax's partners, and the Bellerose contract was signed two weeks before the GTE contract. Only net profits were allowed. A 60-day termination clause in the Bellerose contract didn't cut off recovery, because GTE couldn't show Bellerose would have used it. [Unverified] the dollar amounts in the brief have not been checked against the casebook.

  • Note 1 hypo: what if the Bellerose contract had been signed after the GTE contract? Then it's harder to say it was contemplated when the contract was made.

  • Contractual limits: parties can exclude consequential damages by contract (see Princess Cruises v. General Electric; UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation › Battle of the forms (UCC 2-207)).

Certainty and causation

  • Restatement § 352: no recovery beyond what the evidence establishes with reasonable certainty.
  • Net, not gross, profits: gross revenue ignores costs avoided and would overcompensate (Florafax).
  • New businesses: lost profits are hard to prove. Fall back on reliance damages (Wartzman v. Hightower Productions; Reliance and Restitution Damages › Reliance damages).
  • Causation: the breach must have caused the loss. In Florafax, the jury could find GTE's failures caused Bellerose to cancel.

Mitigation (avoidable consequences)

  • Restatement § 350(1): no recovery for loss the injured party could have avoided without undue risk, burden or humiliation.
  • Measure: expectation minus what reasonable mitigation would have saved. Costs reasonably incurred in trying to mitigate are recoverable, even if the effort fails. [Outside knowledge] (§ 350(2)).
  • Justifications: (1) don't let a plaintiff pile up damages with no benefit to anyone; (2) efficiency, since it discourages waste.
  • Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge (4th Cir., Judge Parker): the county repudiated a bridge contract soon after work began. Luten kept building and sued for the full bill (about $18,000). Held: after notice of repudiation, Luten had to stop. It recovers only costs to the time of breach plus expected profit (about $1,900). A bridge to nowhere helps no one.
  • Example (slide 33): a carrier fails to show up for perishables. The shipper must find another carrier, even a pricier one, and recover the difference instead of letting the fish rot.
  • Lost-volume sellers [Outside knowledge]: a seller with unlimited supply doesn't "mitigate" by reselling, because it would have made both sales (UCC § 2-708(2)).

Mitigation in employment cases

  • A wrongfully fired employee must make reasonable efforts to find substantially equivalent employment.
  • The duty covers only comparable work, not jobs that are inferior in type, rank or location. If the employee does take a non-comparable job, those earnings still reduce damages.
  • Burden (majority view): the breaching employer must prove that comparable work was available and obtainable with reasonable effort. No proof → nothing is subtracted.
  • Maness v. Collins: a fired employee made no effort to find work (he built himself a house). The employer offered no evidence of suitable, comparable jobs, so damages were not reduced. The court rejected the minority view that an employee who doesn't try forfeits without employer proof. Also: a three-year term (not at-will) meant firing only for cause. The employer's own prior breach (hindering performance; good faith) excused Maness. A 5-year, U.S.-wide noncompete was probably unenforceable (footnote 8, not resolved).
  • Compare the employer-side measure in Expectation Damages › Employment contracts.

Attorney fees (American rule)

  • American rule: each side pays its own fees, win or lose. The English rule and civil-law systems make the loser pay.
  • Three bases for shifting fees in U.S. courts:
    1. a contract provision (enforced under freedom of contract; Note 4);
    2. a statute (many fee-shifting statutes exist; UCC §§ 2-710 and 2-715 do not count fees as incidental or consequential damages; Note 2);
    3. court rules or inherent power (for example, Rule 11 sanctions for litigation misconduct; Note 3).
  • Zapata Hermanos v. Hearthside Baking (7th Cir., Posner): the trial court awarded fees (1) as consequential damages under CISG Art. 74 and (2) as a sanction for pre-suit bad faith. Both reversed. The CISG governs substantive contract law, not the procedural question of fees, and pre-litigation bad faith can't be sanctioned with fees. Remanded to consider misconduct during the litigation. The result matches the UCC.

Emotional distress

  • General rule: no emotional distress damages for breach of contract.
  • Restatement § 353 [Outside knowledge: section number]: an exception where the breach also caused bodily harm, or the contract or breach is of a kind where serious emotional disturbance was a particularly likely result (for example, contracts about funerals or the delivery of death messages).
  • Erlich v. Menezes (Cal.): the builder of the Erlichs' "dream house" did defective work that leaked badly, causing serious distress. No recovery for emotional distress in tort (a breach of promise is tort-actionable only if it "violate[s] a social policy that merits the imposition of tort remedies") or in contract. The court cited the Restatement test but seemed to adopt a stricter rule: only when "emotional concerns are the essence of the contract" (p. 984). [Unverified] dollar figures in the brief.
  • Punitive damages are generally unavailable for breach of contract unless the conduct is also a tort (Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure). [Outside knowledge] (Restatement § 355).

Exam framework

  1. Compute the expectation baseline (Expectation Damages).
  2. Foreseeability: general (natural) vs. special (communicated at formation) damages. Apply Hadley and § 351, including § 351(3).
  3. Certainty: net profits; new business; switch to reliance if needed.
  4. Causation.
  5. Mitigation: what could the plaintiff reasonably have avoided? For employment: comparable work, the employer's burden, and offsetting actual earnings. After repudiation: stop work (Luten Bridge).
  6. Nonrecoverable items: attorney fees (American rule, three bases); emotional distress (Erlich; § 353 exceptions); punitive damages.
  7. Contract terms: exclusions of consequential damages; fee-shifting clauses; liquidated damages (Liquidated Damages).
