In one paragraph

The parol evidence rule (PER) limits using prior or contemporaneous agreements, oral or written, to change a final written agreement. A partially integrated writing (final, but not complete) can't be contradicted but can be supplemented by consistent additional terms. A fully (completely) integrated writing can't be contradicted or supplemented. Evidence is always admissible to interpret the writing and to show fraud, duress, mistake or a condition precedent. The fight is over how to decide integration. The classical approach looks only at the writing's "four corners" (Thompson v. Libby). The modern Corbin / Restatement approach first hears all relevant evidence (Taylor v. State Farm, Shay v. Aldrich). Some courts read the fraud exception narrowly (Sherrodd v. Morrison-Knudsen). UCC § 2-202 codifies the rule for goods.

The two-step test

  1. Integration: is the writing the parties' final expression (integrated)? Is it also the complete and exclusive statement (fully integrated)?
  2. Classify the offered term:
    • It contradicts the writing → excluded if the writing is integrated at all.
    • It supplements with a consistent additional term → admissible if the writing is only partially integrated; excluded if it is fully integrated.
  • The rule excludes only evidence that is (a) of a prior or contemporaneous agreement and (b) offered to vary the terms. Later modifications aren't covered.

Consistent additional term (admissible even against some writings): a term (a) agreed for separate consideration, or (b) that might naturally be omitted from a writing like this one (Restatement § 216). Under the UCC test, terms that "would certainly have been included" if agreed are excluded. [Outside knowledge] the UCC Comment 3 "certainly" test is supplied from general knowledge.

Factors on integration (existing note): whether counsel was involved, the parties' sophistication, price, time spent negotiating, and whether there is a merger clause. A merger clause is a strong factor but not dispositive under the modern view.

Classical approach

  • Williston / four corners. The judge decides integration from the writing alone. If the writing looks complete on its face, or has a merger clause, it is fully integrated and outside evidence is excluded.
  • Thompson v. Libby: a written contract for the sale of logs said nothing about quality. The buyer offered evidence of an oral warranty of quality. Held: the writing was complete on its face, so the oral warranty was inadmissible.
  • Policy: certainty, protecting writings from perjured testimony, and keeping juries away from unreliable evidence.

Modern (Corbin and Restatement) approach

  • Corbin / Restatement (Second) §§ 209-216. The judge first considers all relevant extrinsic evidence, including the negotiations and circumstances, to decide whether the writing is integrated and what it means. Only then is the rule applied.
  • Taylor v. State Farm: Taylor signed a general release in exchange for $15,000 in uninsured-motorist benefits. Later he brought a bad-faith claim against State Farm worth a potential $2.1 million. The court rejected "plain meaning" and admitted extrinsic evidence on whether the release was meant to cover the bad-faith claim. The test is whether the language is "reasonably susceptible" to the offered meaning.
  • Shay v. Aldrich (note case, 4-3): parol evidence was admitted to show that a release was intended to cover only the two named officers. The contextualist majority prevailed over a textualist dissent.
  • Policy: get the parties' actual intent, at the cost of predictability.

Exceptions

Evidence is not barred by the rule when offered:

  1. To interpret ambiguous terms (Interpretation).
  2. To show fraud, duress, mistake, illegality or other invalidating causes (formation defenses).
  3. To show a condition precedent to the writing taking effect at all. [Outside knowledge]
  4. To prove a later modification.
  5. Under the UCC, evidence of course of performance, course of dealing and usage of trade (§ 1-303) is admissible to explain or supplement even a fully integrated writing.

Fraud and the "directly contradicts" limit:

  • Sherrodd v. Morrison-Knudsen: a fixed-sum earthwork subcontract for $97,500. The work turned out to be about twice what was represented. The court excluded evidence of oral representations that directly contradicted the fixed price, even though fraud was alleged. The dissent criticized this as gutting the fraud exception.
  • Compare Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure › Fraud in the execution vs. inducement and Hill v. Jones (an integration clause did not bar a nondisclosure claim).

UCC 2-202

  • Terms in a final writing may not be contradicted by prior or contemporaneous agreements.
  • They may be explained or supplemented by (a) course of performance, course of dealing and usage of trade (§ 1-303), and (b) consistent additional terms, unless the writing is intended as complete and exclusive.

Exam framework

  1. Is there a written agreement, and is the offered evidence a prior or contemporaneous agreement? (Later → no PER issue.)
  2. Which approach? Analyze under both: four corners (Thompson) and all the evidence (Taylor / Corbin).
  3. Integration: run the factors. Treat a merger clause as strong but not dispositive.
  4. Contradict or supplement? For a supplement: separate consideration? naturally omitted?
  5. Exceptions: interpretation, fraud (note the Sherrodd limit), duress, mistake, condition precedent, the UCC trade-usage evidence.
  6. Interpretation step: is the term ambiguous (patent or latent)? See Interpretation.
