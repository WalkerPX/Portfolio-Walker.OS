In one paragraph

Formation requires a manifestation of mutual assent, usually an offer and an acceptance, judged by the objective theory: what a reasonable person in the other party's position would understand. An offer creates a power of acceptance that ends by rejection or counteroffer, lapse, revocation (direct or indirect), or death or incapacity. Unilateral offers invite acceptance by performance. Once performance begins, the offer becomes irrevocable (Restatement § 45).

Objective theory of contract

  • Intent is judged by outward manifestations, not secret intent (Ray v. Eurice Bros: Learned Hand's "twenty bishops"; Restatement (Second) § 21 rejects subjective intent).
  • Duty to read / signature rule: one who signs is bound absent fraud, duress, mutual mistake, etc. (Ray v. Eurice Bros). It is limited where the other side actively misrepresents the document (Park 100 Investors v. Kartes).
  • Jest: a secret joke doesn't matter if a reasonable person would take the offer seriously (Lucy v. Zehmer). But no offer exists if no reasonable person would (Leonard v. Pepsico).
  • Why objective? (Holmes): efficiency (subjective inquiry is costly) and fairness (words are read in their ordinary sense).

Offers

  • Definition (Restatement (Second) § 24): a manifestation of willingness to enter a bargain, made so as to justify the other person in understanding that assent is invited and will conclude it.
  • Preliminary negotiations (§ 26): not an offer if the recipient knows or has reason to know the sender intends no commitment until further assent (Lonergan v. Scolnick: "decide fast, as I expect to have a buyer").
  • Advertisements: generally invitations to deal (§ 26 cmt. b; Lonergan v. Scolnick, Leonard v. Pepsico). Exceptions:
    • language of commitment, leaving nothing open ("first come, first served," Lefkowitz);
    • bait and switch or deception (Izadi v. Machado Ford);
    • offers seeking performance (rewards and promotions: Sateriale v. RJ Reynolds, Harris v. Time);
    • statutory regimes (Donovan v. RRL Corp).
  • Hypos (slides): Maya's app terms (pre-checked box); Professor Walsh; Marcus's group chat to 15 recipients (to whom was the offer made?); the tenant and landlord at $1,500 vs. $1,600 with a Thursday acceptance.

Acceptance (bilateral contracts)

  • Mirror image rule (common law): acceptance must match the offer. A reply that changes terms is a counteroffer, which rejects the offer (Normile v. Miller). The UCC modifies this in § 2-207 (UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation).
  • Offeror is "master of the offer": it can prescribe the manner and time of acceptance.
  • Mailbox rule: acceptance is effective on dispatch. Offers, revocations and rejections are effective on receipt (§§ 30, 63-66). Exceptions: the offeror requires receipt, and option contracts (acceptance is effective on receipt; Berryman v. Kmoch Note 6). CISG: the offer is irrevocable once acceptance is dispatched, but acceptance is effective only on arrival (Lonergan v. Scolnick Note 4).
  • Silence is generally not acceptance ([Outside knowledge: Restatement § 69 exceptions]).

Termination of the power of acceptance

  1. Rejection or counteroffer (Normile v. Miller).
  2. Lapse of the stated time or a reasonable time.
  3. Revocation: direct, or indirect when the offeree learns reliably that the offeror took definite inconsistent action (§ 43; Normile v. Miller: "you snooze, you lose"; Berryman v. Kmoch).
  4. Death or incapacity of either party ([Outside knowledge: § 48]).
  • Irrevocable offers: option contracts with consideration (Berryman v. Kmoch); § 87(1)(a) recital; firm offers under UCC § 2-205; reliance under § 87(2) (Drennan v. Star Paving); part performance of a unilateral offer under § 45 (Cook v. Coldwell Banker). See Option Contracts and Firm Offers.

Unilateral contracts

  • Definition (slides): an exchange of the offeror's promise for the offeree's act. The offeree doesn't promise, it acts. Example: "If you run 5 miles, I'll pay $1,000."
  • Key issue: when does beginning performance stop the offeror from revoking?
    • Classical: revocable until performance is complete (harsh on an offeree who is nearly done).
    • Restatement (First) § 45: substantial performance or beginning makes the offer irrevocable. The offeror's duty is conditioned on completion. Applied in Cook v. Coldwell Banker (bonus offer, no change after substantial performance).
    • Restatement (Second) § 45: beginning invited performance creates an option contract. The Second Restatement doesn't use the term "unilateral contract" ("same idea, different label").
  • Promotions: Sateriale v. RJ Reynolds (Camel Cash: offer, acceptance by performance, no mutuality requirement for unilateral contracts); Harris v. Time (opening an envelope is consideration, but de minimis).
  • Problem 2-2 (GOC gas rewards): bilateral theory (did GOC seek a return promise?) vs. unilateral theory (§ 45: is buying 1 gallon "beginning performance"?).
  • FitIndy hypo (slides): a training bonus for six runs, with Angela, Ben and Carla at different stages when the offer is revoked.

Standard-form contracts

  • Assent to boilerplate through tickets, clickwrap and shrinkwrap (Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute).
  • Policing through Unconscionability and Restatement § 211(3) (terms the drafter knows the other would reject are not part of the agreement).

Exam checklist

  1. Was there an offer (commitment, definiteness, addressed to the offeree)? Is it an ad or a jest?
  2. Was the power of acceptance still alive (lapse, revocation, rejection or counteroffer)?
  3. Was there a valid acceptance (mirror image or § 2-207; method; timing and mailbox)?
  4. Is the offer unilateral or bilateral? If unilateral, did performance begin (§ 45)?
  5. Is the offer irrevocable (option, firm offer, § 87(2) reliance)?
