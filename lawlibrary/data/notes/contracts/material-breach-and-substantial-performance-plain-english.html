The short version

Not every slip-up lets the other side walk away. A small breach means you pay for the harm. Only a serious ('material') breach lets the other side stop performing, and eventually end the contract.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Material Breach and Substantial Performance.

What it's about

When one side falls short, two questions arise:

  1. Can the other side stop performing? Only if the breach is material (serious).
  2. Can the breaching side still get paid? Yes, if it substantially performed, minus damages for what it got wrong.

Material or minor? Factors courts weigh (Restatement § 241)

  • How much of the expected benefit the injured side lost.
  • Whether money can make up for it.
  • How much the breaching side would lose (forfeit) if the contract ended.
  • Whether the breaching side is likely to cure (fix) it.
  • Whether the breaching side acted in good faith.

What happens step by step

  • Minor breach: the injured side must keep performing but can sue for damages.
  • Material breach: the injured side can suspend its own performance and give the other side a chance to cure.
  • Uncured material breach ("total breach"): the injured side can terminate the contract and sue for all its damages.

Be careful: if you treat a minor breach as material and walk away, you may become the one in breach.

An everyday example

A builder finishes your house but uses a different brand of pipe of equal quality. That's a minor breach. You pay the price minus the (tiny) difference in value; you can't refuse to pay at all. If the builder abandons the job halfway, that's material, and you can hire someone else and sue.

The cases, in plain words

  • Jacob and Youngs v. Kent: the builder used a different but equal-quality pipe brand. That was substantial performance, so the owner had to pay, minus the small difference in value, not the huge cost of tearing out walls.
  • Sackett v. Spindler: a buyer kept missing payments, bounced a check and broke promises. That was a total breach, so the seller could end the deal, resell and sue.
  • Lancellotti v. Thomas: even a party who breached can recover what it paid beyond the harm it caused.

Words you'll hear

  • Cure: fixing a breach in time.
  • Substantial performance: close enough to earn the price, minus damages.
  • Total breach: a breach serious enough to end the contract.
