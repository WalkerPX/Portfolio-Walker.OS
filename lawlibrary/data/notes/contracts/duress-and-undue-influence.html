In one paragraph

These defenses attack the bargaining process. Physical duress (actual force) makes a contract void. Economic duress makes it voidable. Totem Marine v. Alyeska Pipeline lists three elements: (1) a wrongful or improper threat, which bad faith can supply even if the threat is lawful; (2) no reasonable alternative; and (3) the threat actually induced the contract. Undue influence is unfair persuasion of someone unusually susceptible to it. It is judged by the totality of the circumstances, often using the Odorizzi factors (Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District). Bad faith and a wrongful threat are not required. Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing shows how hard both defenses are to win, even in a coercive setting.

Physical duress

  • Actual physical compulsion (a gun to the head; a hand forced to sign) → void. There was never any assent (Restatement § 174). [Outside knowledge] section number.
  • Threats of physical harm → usually voidable as improper threats; the effect depends on the facts.

Economic duress

Totem three-element test (checklist):

  1. A wrongful or improper threat. It need not be illegal: a threat made in bad faith, or to breach a contract in bad faith, can qualify.
  2. No reasonable alternative to giving in (for example, suing is too slow when bankruptcy is imminent).
  3. The threat actually induced the contract (causation).
  • Restatement (Second) §§ 175-176 [Outside knowledge]: a threat is improper if it is a crime or tort, a threat of criminal prosecution, a bad-faith threat of civil process, or a breach of the duty of good faith. It is also improper if the resulting exchange is unfair and the threat brings no legitimate benefit to the threatening party.
  • Totem Marine v. Alyeska Pipeline: Alyeska ended a transport contract, and Totem, owed about $300,000 and facing bankruptcy, accepted $97,500 in settlement and signed a release. Totem alleged that Alyeska deliberately withheld payment knowing Totem was desperate. That stated a claim for economic duress, so summary judgment was reversed.
  • Not duress: threatening to do what you have a legal right to do, in good faith (Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District: threatening dismissal proceedings). Ordinary hard bargaining and financial pressure the other side did not cause.
  • Modifications [Outside knowledge]: duress often comes up when a party coerces a modification (the pre-existing duty rule; UCC § 2-209 good-faith modifications).

Undue influence

Restatement elements (existing note; Restatement (Second) § 177):

  1. Inducement: the unfair persuasion caused the assent.
  2. Unfair persuasion, judged under the Odorizzi factors (none required; look at the totality):
    • unusual or inappropriate time or place;
    • insistent demand that the business be finished at once;
    • emphasis on the bad consequences of delay;
    • multiple persuaders on the dominant side;
    • no third-party advisers;
    • statements that there is no time to consult advisers or lawyers.
  3. Undue susceptibility: weakness of mind, or poor physical, emotional or mental condition.
  4. Inequitable result: an economically unfair outcome, or one that departs from the person's usual course.
  • A confidential relationship is not required, but § 177(1) covers a person under the other's domination or in a relationship that justifies assuming the other won't act against their welfare.
  • Bad faith is not an element. Duress can be harder to prove because it needs a wrongful threat.
  • Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District: a teacher arrested on criminal charges was visited at home by the superintendent and principal after about 40 hours without sleep. They pressed him to resign immediately, warning of publicity. Duress, fraud, mistake and incapacity all failed. Undue influence was adequately stated.

Duress and undue influence together

  • Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing: farm workers signed arbitration agreements at the end of a long workday, in a hotel parking lot, in a language many did not fully understand, with no meaningful explanation. Majority: neither duress nor undue influence was established under California law. Dissent: the majority ignored the "coercive atmosphere" Elkhorn deliberately created. Compare Unconscionability › Arbitration clauses and the FAA.

Remedy

  • Void (physical compulsion): no contract at all.
  • Voidable (threats, economic duress, undue influence): the victim may rescind and recover restitution, or affirm. The victim must act promptly, or delay may count as ratification. [Outside knowledge]

Exam framework

  1. What pressure was there? Physical (void) or economic or psychological (voidable)?
  2. Economic duress: walk through Totem's three elements in order. For wrongfulness, consider bad faith and whether the threatening party caused the predicament. For alternatives, ask whether suing was realistic.
  3. Undue influence: walk through the four elements and weigh the Odorizzi factors against the facts.
  4. Rule out the near misses the way Odorizzi does: menace, fraud, mistake, incapacity.
  5. Remedy: rescission and restitution; watch for ratification.

Hypos (slides)

  • Hypo 2, "Take It or Leave It" (Totem facts): $300,000 owed, bankruptcy looming, and a $97,500 "full and final" offer with a threat of years of litigation. Wrongful because made in bad faith? No reasonable alternative, given the timing? Induced? Likely voidable.
  • Hypo 3, "Sign Here — It's Just Paperwork": farm workers arrive at 9 p.m. after an 1,800-mile bus trip. They are told "standard onboarding forms" with no translation, and that they can't start work tomorrow without signing. Totem: is that threat wrongful? Odorizzi: time and place, haste, no advisers. Compare Martinez-Gonzalez and its dissent. Consider unconscionability as an alternative.
