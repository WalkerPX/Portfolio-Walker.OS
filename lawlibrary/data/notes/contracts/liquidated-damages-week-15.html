Big picture

  • Parties may fix the remedy for breach in their original agreement; such a clause is a "liquidated damages" clause where a fixed or determinable sum is specified in advance (p. 1089).
  • Courts scrutinize these clauses rather than enforcing them automatically: a term aimed at compensation is enforceable; a clause intended to penalize is not (p. 1090).
  • Valid clause substitutes party assessment for court-assessed damages — no proof of actual loss, no mitigation inquiry (pp. 1095–1097).
  • Central tension: "single look" at contract formation (majority) vs. review also at breach for gross disproportion to actual harm (dissent; modern trend) (pp. 1094, 1098–1102).

Doctrine outline

  • Settlement agreements after breach are enforced absent fraud, mistake, or duress; parties may also stipulate the amount of damages, litigating only whether breach occurred (p. 1089).
  • Originally agreed remedy terms are treated differently — subject to judicial scrutiny and traditional tests (pp. 1089–1090).
  • Valid liquidated damages clause: non-breaching party has no duty to mitigate (p. 1090, 1097).
  • No-actual-harm defense rejected where clause is valid; judging at formation (p. 1097).
  • Mitigation is part of court-assessed damages; liquidated damages substitute party assessment, so mitigation is irrelevant once clause is valid (pp. 1095–1096, citing Lake River).
  • If doubt whether clause is liquidated damages or penalty, construe as penalty (p. 1095).
  • Challenger bears burden of proving clause unenforceable (p. 1093).
  • Contract of adhesion not void per se; unenforceable only if unconscionable — (1) lack of meaningful choice and (2) terms unreasonably favoring the other party (p. 1098).

Tests and elements

  • Three essential elements (Heister, as stated in Barrie School) (p. 1094):
    1. Clause must provide in clear and unambiguous terms for a certain sum.
    2. Liquidated damages must reasonably be compensation for the damages anticipated by the breach.
    3. Clauses are mandatory binding agreements before the fact, not alterable to correspond to actual damages determined after the fact.
  • Decisive element is intention of the parties — penalty or agreed damages — gleaned from subject matter, contract language, and circumstances of execution (p. 1094).
  • Traditional three-pronged test (Note 1) (p. 1100): (1) damages anticipated from breach uncertain in amount or difficult to prove; (2) parties intended to liquidate damages rather than penalize; (3) amount is a reasonable forecast of just compensation.
  • Restatement (Second) § 356 two-part test (Note 1) (p. 1100): enforceable if amount fixed is reasonable in light of "anticipated or actual loss" and "difficulties of proof of loss." Greater difficulty of proof → easier to show amount is reasonable.
  • Maryland formulation (Baltimore Bridge, quoted p. 1093): sum named is liquidated damages, not penalty, unless "grossly excessive and out of all proportion to the damages that might reasonably have been expected"; judged from subject matter and surrounding facts known at execution.
  • Reasonableness judged at time of contract formation (majority) (pp. 1094, 1097).

Cases

  • Barrie School v. Patch (Md. 2007) (pp. 1090–1098):
    • Facts: Parents signed re-enrollment agreement; § 3 made them liable for full year's tuition ($13,490 balance + $1,000 deposit) unless written cancellation received before May 31, 2004; they cancelled July 14, 2004 (p. 1090).
    • Held: § 3 is a valid liquidated damages clause, not a penalty; no duty to mitigate; no-actual-harm defense rejected; not a contract of adhesion (pp. 1095–1098).
    • Reasoning: one year's tuition was a reasonable forecast; impact of losing one student "next to impossible" to estimate (pp. 1095–1096); mitigation is court-assessed damages doctrine, blunted by party assessment (p. 1096, quoting Lake River).
    • Dissent: validity should also be reviewed post-breach; school met enrollment projections ("no empty desk"), so full tuition grossly disproportionate to actual harm; would require proof of actual damages and mitigation (pp. 1098–1100).
  • Lake River Corp. v. Carborundum Co. (7th Cir. 1985) (Posner, J.) (p. 1096): clause invalid as penalty, but distinguishes mitigation (court-assessed damages) from liquidated damages (party assessment).
  • Baltimore Bridge Co. v. United Railways & Electric Co. (Md. 1915) (pp. 1093–1094, 1099): settled rule — named sum treated as liquidated damages unless grossly excessive; if actual damages less, not a penalty unless so exorbitant as to show not a bona fide pre-estimate; if stipulated amount inadequate, no greater recovery.
  • Heister (Md. 2006) (p. 1094): three essential elements of valid clause.
  • Circuit City v. Rockville Pike (Md. 2003) (p. 1095): avoidable consequences rule — mitigation determines proper amount of court-assessed damages.
  • Kelly v. Marx (Mass. 1999) (Note 2) (p. 1101): "single look" — clause evaluated only at contract formation.
  • Wasserman's Inc. v. Twp. of Middletown (N.J. 1994) (Note 2) (p. 1102): modern approach — hindsight frequently better than foresight.
  • Wassenaar v. Panos (Wis. 1983) (Note 2) (p. 1102): clause "grossly disproportionate to the actual harm" may be unreasonable.
  • Cal. & Hawaiian Sugar Co. v. Sun Ship (9th Cir. 1986) (Note 2) (p. 1102): disjunctive reading — upheld if reasonable in light of anticipated OR actual harm; sophisticated parties; $4M recovery despite ~$400K actual harm.
  • XCO Int'l v. Pac. Sci. Co. (7th Cir. 2004) (Note 2) (p. 1102): rule against penalties an "anachronism" between commercial enterprises; enforceable if reasonable in light of actual harm.
  • Colonial at Lynnfield v. Sloan (1st Cir. 1989); Arcese v. Daniel Schmitt & Co. (Mo. Ct. App. 2016) (Note 3) (p. 1102): complete absence of actual loss invalidates clause as penalty.
  • Frick Co. v. Rubel Corp. (2d Cir. 1933); Wallace Real Estate v. Groves (Wash. 1994) (Note 3) (p. 1102): under traditional approach, actual harm not relevant / not prerequisite.
  • NPS, LLC v. Minihane (Mass. 2008); VII MP Miami Hotel Owner v. Hycroft (N.Y. App. Div. 2022) (Note 4) (p. 1102): no-mitigation rule is consensus view.
  • Atrium Med. Ctr. v. Hous. Red C LLC (Tex. 2020); S. Bldg. Servs. v. City of Fort Smith (Ark. Ct. App. 2013) (Note 4) (p. 1103): minority — mitigation considered in assessing divergence from actual damages.
  • Vanderbilt Univ. v. DiNardo (6th Cir. 1999) (Note 7) (p. 1103): coach's liquidated damages equal to net annual salary enforceable given tangible and intangible harm.
  • Equity Enters. v. Milosch (Wis. Ct. App. 2001) (Note 7) (pp. 1103–1104): forfeiture of commissions for breach of noncompete not reasonable liquidated damages.
  • Wassenaar (Note 7) (p. 1104): employment clause entitling discharged manager to full remaining compensation upheld as reasonable forecast of uncertain damages (reputation, emotional stress); no deduction for new job.
  • D.A. Nolt v. Phila. Mun. Auth. (E.D. Pa. 2020) (Note 6) (p. 1103): $10,000/day construction delay measure unreasonable when based only on recollections; not retroactively reasonable because actual damages happened to be comparable.
  • Hutton Contracting v. City of Coffeyville (10th Cir. 2007) (Note 6) (p. 1103): $500/day delay damages enforced.
  • Hot Devs. v. Willow Lake Ests. (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 2007) (Note 8) (p. 1104): $550,000 deposit on $5.7M commercial sale (<10%) reasonable liquidated damages.
  • Allen v. Smith (Cal. Ct. App. 2002) vs. O'Shield v. Lakeside Bank (Ill. App. Ct. 2002) (Note 8) (p. 1104): split on whether liquidated damages clause bars specific performance of real estate contract.

Examples and problems

  • Barrie School: full year's tuition as agreed remedy for late withdrawal; upheld despite school filling its enrollment (pp. 1090, 1097).
  • Lake River: shipping contract breached after market shift; clause invalid as penalty but mitigation/liquidated damages distinction stated (p. 1096).
  • Construction delay clauses: per diem rates common and usually enforceable given tangible and intangible delay injuries (Note 6) (p. 1103).
  • Employment: coach's salary-based clause (DiNardo); manager's full-term compensation (Wassenaar); commission forfeiture struck (Milosch) (Note 7) (pp. 1103–1104).
  • Real estate earnest money: majority rule bars breaching purchaser's recovery; other courts scrutinize as liquidated damages (Note 8) (p. 1104).
  • Problem 10-4 reconsidered (p. 1089): should injunctive relief be available to "Operating Room" producers if Green accepts a contract with "Flying High"?

Exam angles

  • Is the clause a pre-breach agreed remedy (scrutinized) or a post-breach settlement (enforced absent fraud/mistake/duress)? (p. 1089).
  • Apply the elements: certain sum; reasonable compensation forecast; not alterable after the fact (p. 1094). Add the traditional three-prong test and § 356 (p. 1100).
  • Identify the jurisdiction's perspective: formation-only (Kelly) vs. formation-or-breach (Wasserman's; Restatement; UCC § 2-718) vs. disjunctive (Sun Ship; XCO) (pp. 1101–1102).
  • Burden on the party challenging the clause; doubts construed as penalty (pp. 1093, 1095).
  • If clause valid: no mitigation, no no-actual-harm defense (pp. 1096–1097). If invalid: actual damages must be proved and mitigated (dissent, p. 1100).
  • Difficulty of proof cuts in favor of enforcement (p. 1100).
  • Consider consumer vs. commercial context and party sophistication (Note 5) (p. 1103).
  • Watch for overcompensation: no double recovery of liquidated damages and specific performance (Note 8) (p. 1104).
  • Underliquidated clauses: inadequacy alone does not invalidate; no greater recovery (Baltimore Bridge, pp. 1093–1094, 1099).
