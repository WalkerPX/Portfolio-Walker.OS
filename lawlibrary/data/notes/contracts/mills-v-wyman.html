Quick reference

Citation: 20 Mass. (3 Pick.) 207 (1825) · Casebook: Knapp p. 317 · Topic: Restitution and Promissory Restitution › Promissory restitution (Restatement 86) One line: A father's promise, made after the fact, to pay a stranger for caring for his sick adult son was unenforceable. A moral obligation and past services are not consideration.

Overview

The classical view on promises based on moral obligation or past benefits. It is the counterpoint to Webb v. McGowin and Restatement (Second) § 86.

Parties

  • Plaintiff: Mills, of Hartford, Connecticut. He housed and nursed the defendant's son and wanted compensation for board and nursing.
  • Defendant: Wyman, of Shrewsbury, Massachusetts. The father, who promised to pay and then refused.
  • Levi Wyman, about 25, the adult son, who had long since left his father's household.

Facts

  • Levi, returning from a sea voyage, fell suddenly ill in Hartford. He was poor and in distress. Mills cared for him from February 5 to 20, 1821.
  • On February 24, after all expenses had been incurred, the father wrote promising to pay Mills's expenses. He later refused. (Levi died, per the slides.)

Procedural History

Action in assumpsit. The Court of Common Pleas directed a nonsuit. The Supreme Judicial Court held the nonsuit "was right" and entered judgment for the defendant (affirmed).

Issue

Is a promise made in recognition of past services, rendered to the promisor's adult son without the promisor's request, enforceable on the basis of moral obligation?

Holding

No. Past services are not consideration for a later promise, and a moral obligation alone does not make a promise legally binding.

Reasoning

  • Contractual obligation rests on exchange. Services already completed were not given in exchange for the later promise (past consideration).
  • The father had no legal duty to support his adult son, so there was no pre-existing legal obligation that a new promise could revive.
  • The court praised the father's moral duty but held it "is not sufficient to support an action." Enforcement belongs to "the interior forum" of conscience.
  • Note 2 (slides), two policy concerns: (1) moral obligation varies among people; (2) judicial enforcement of moral duty risks "judicial tyranny."

Rule

A promise made in recognition of a past benefit or a moral obligation, without a pre-existing legal obligation, is not enforceable for lack of consideration (classical rule).

Why It Matters

  • It is the baseline against which promissory restitution (§ 86, Webb v. McGowin) developed.
  • Exceptions recognized even classically (Notes 3-6): a promise to pay a debt barred by the statute of limitations, a debt discharged in bankruptcy, or a contract voidable for infancy. These are promises based on a pre-existing legal obligation, and they are enforceable. Note 5: some states require such promises to be in writing.

Other Key Points

  • Note 1: if Wyman had promised before the services, they would have been bargained-for consideration. If Levi had been a minor, the father would have had a legal duty of support, a matter of family law, not contract.
  • Restitution angle: Mills could not recover in restitution from the father either, since the father was not enriched. Compare Levi himself and Credit Bureau Enterprises v. Pelo.
  • Hypo 9 (slides): a past-benefit promise. Apply Mills, then § 86.
