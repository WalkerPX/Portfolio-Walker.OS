The short version

When the deal is for goods (movable things), a special statute, the UCC, applies instead of ordinary contract rules. It is more flexible about forming contracts and has its own fix for clashing paperwork, the 'battle of the forms.'

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation.

What it's about

UCC Article 2 governs sales of goods: things you can move, like lumber, cars, computers or crops. It does not cover services (a haircut, legal advice), land or employment. Those stay under the common law.

First question: does the UCC even apply?

Many deals mix goods and services (a contractor who sells and installs a furnace). Courts use the predominant purpose test: what was the deal mainly about?

  • Jannusch v. Naffziger: the sale of a food-truck business was mainly a sale of goods (the truck and equipment), so the UCC applied.
  • Princess Cruises v. General Electric: a ship repair job was mainly services, so the common law applied.

The UCC is flexible about forming a deal

Under UCC § 2-204, a contract exists if the parties acted like they had one, even if some terms were left open or no one can point to the exact moment of agreement. The UCC fills in missing terms such as a reasonable price, a reasonable time and the place of delivery.

  • But there must still be real agreement. In EC Styberg Engineering v. Eaton, e-mails hinting at a big purchase didn't create a contract, because these companies normally used formal purchase orders.

The "battle of the forms" (UCC § 2-207)

Businesses send each other preprinted forms: a buyer's purchase order, then a seller's acknowledgment, each with its own fine print. The terms rarely match.

  • Under old common law, a response with different terms was a counteroffer, and whoever sent the last form usually won.
  • UCC § 2-207 says a response that clearly agrees to the deal is an acceptance even if it adds or changes terms. Then:
    • Between merchants, added terms become part of the deal unless the offer limited acceptance to its own terms, the new terms materially change the deal, or the other side objects.
    • If the paperwork doesn't make a contract but the parties act like they have one, the contract is the terms both forms agree on, plus the UCC's default rules.

An everyday example

A bakery orders 1,000 lbs of flour on its form. The supplier ships with an invoice that says "No refunds for any reason." Both sides go ahead. Is the bakery stuck with "no refunds"? Under § 2-207, probably not, because that term materially changes the deal.

Words you'll hear

  • Goods: movable things.
  • Merchant: someone who deals in those goods or holds themselves out as an expert in them.
  • Gap filler: a UCC default term that fills a hole the parties left.
