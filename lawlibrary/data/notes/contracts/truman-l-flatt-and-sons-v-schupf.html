Overview

The course case defining anticipatory repudiation: a definite and unequivocal manifestation that a party will not perform. A mere request to change terms is not enough. It also applies the rule on retraction of a repudiation.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Truman L. Flatt & Sons Co., the buyer. It wanted the land for an asphalt plant and sought specific performance.
  • Defendants / appellees: the Schupfs, the sellers. They treated the buyer's letter as a repudiation and refused to sell.

Facts

  • The parties signed a contract to buy land, conditioned on city council approval of rezoning or a permit for an asphalt plant. [Outside knowledge: price about $160,000.]
  • Facing public opposition, the buyer withdrew its rezoning application. It wrote to the sellers that it was still interested, but at a lower price ([Outside knowledge: about $110,000]), and asked them to consider it.
  • The sellers' attorney declined and expressed "regret" that the sale would not be completed.
  • The buyer then wrote that it would proceed at the original price and waive the condition. The sellers treated the earlier letter as a repudiation and refused to close.

Procedural History

  • The buyer sued for specific performance. The trial court granted summary judgment for the sellers.
  • Appellate Court: reversed and remanded.

Issue

  1. Was the buyer's letter requesting a lower price an anticipatory repudiation?
  2. If so, did the buyer effectively retract it?

Holding

  1. No. The letter was not a definite and unequivocal refusal to perform. It expressed continued interest and proposed new terms.
  2. Alternatively, even if it were a repudiation, the buyer retracted it before the sellers materially changed position or indicated they considered the repudiation final.

Reasoning

  • Repudiation requires a clear manifestation of intent not to perform. Courts require it to be "definite and unequivocal" (Note 2) because repudiation lets the other side suspend performance, terminate and sue. A request for changed terms, without a threat not to perform, is not enough.
  • Retraction (Restatement § 256; UCC § 2-611): a repudiating party may retract until its next performance is due, unless the aggrieved party has (a) cancelled, (b) materially changed position in reliance, or (c) indicated it considers the repudiation final. The attorney's letter of "regret" did not clearly elect to treat the contract as ended, and the sellers had not changed position.

Rule

An anticipatory repudiation is a definite and unequivocal statement or voluntary act indicating that a party will not or cannot perform, made before performance is due. A request for modification is not a repudiation. A repudiation may be retracted until the next performance is due, unless the other party has cancelled, materially relied, or indicated it considers the repudiation final.

Why It Matters

  • Definition (slides): repudiation before performance is due, by words or actions, "tantamount to a total breach." It may excuse the other party and justify cancellation.
  • Risk for the aggrieved party (slides): it must reasonably anticipate breach. Cancelling on a misread "repudiation" may itself be a breach. A safer path is to demand assurances (UCC § 2-609, Restatement § 251; see Hornell Brewing v. Spry).

Other Key Points

  • Exam pattern: "We may not be able to perform unless..." or "We'd like to renegotiate" is usually not a repudiation. "We will not perform" usually is.
