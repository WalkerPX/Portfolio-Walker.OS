Quick reference

Citation: 608 N.W.2d 20 (Iowa 2000) · Casebook: Knapp p. 287 · Topic: Restitution and Promissory Restitution One line: A man involuntarily hospitalized under a court order must pay the reasonable value of the necessary services in restitution (contract implied in law), whatever the validity of the form he signed.

Name

The slides call this case "Credit Enterprises, Inc. v. Pelo." The casebook caption is Credit Bureau Enterprises, Inc. v. Pelo.

Overview

The course case on restitution without a promise. One who provides necessary services in an emergency may recover their reasonable value even without the recipient's consent, under quasi-contract (contract implied in law).

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellee: Credit Bureau Enterprises, Inc. A collection agency suing on the hospital's assigned bill. It wanted payment of the hospital charges.
  • Defendant / appellant: Russell N. Pelo. The patient, who had been held under emergency and involuntary hospitalization orders. He argued he never validly agreed to pay and was held against his will.
  • Assignor: Ellsworth Municipal Hospital, Iowa Falls.

Facts

  • January 8, 1995: after an argument with his wife, Pelo checked into a motel, called her "making threats of self harm" and bought a shotgun. Police took him to the hospital.
  • A magistrate found probable cause he was seriously mentally impaired and likely to injure himself, and entered an emergency hospitalization order (up to 48 hours). His wife then applied for involuntary hospitalization, and an immediate-hospitalization order was entered.
  • On admission, Pelo refused to sign a financial responsibility form. At about 5 a.m., a nurse woke him and demanded he sign "or the hospital could not insure the safety or return of his personal items." He read and signed it.
  • He was later released ([Outside knowledge: after about six days, and the commitment petition was ultimately dismissed]). His insurer did not pay the full bill. The hospital assigned the bill for collection.

Procedural History

  • Small claims / district court: judgment against Pelo for the hospital bill (slides: on the theory that he was bound by the intake contract he signed).
  • Iowa Supreme Court (en banc): affirmed, on a restitution (implied-in-law) rationale.

Issue

Is a patient who was involuntarily hospitalized liable for the reasonable value of the hospital's services, when his signed consent to pay may be invalid?

Holding

Yes. Pelo is personally liable for the hospital bill. Liability rests on a contract implied in law (restitution), not on the questionable signed form.

Reasoning

  • The signed form is legally dubious given Pelo's mental state at the time (Note 1: Restatement (Second) § 15, mental incapacity).
  • Contract implied in fact (actual agreement shown by conduct) is distinguished from contract implied in law (quasi-contract), an obligation imposed by law to prevent unjust enrichment regardless of assent.
  • Restatement (First) of Restitution § 116: one who supplies necessary services to another in an emergency, when the recipient cannot consent, is entitled to restitution if the supplier acted unofficiously and with intent to charge, and the services were necessary to prevent serious harm.
  • The hospital provided necessary care under court order. Pelo was enriched by services he would have had to pay for, and it would be unjust for him to keep the benefit without paying.

Rule

A person who renders necessary services (especially professional or medical services) to another in an emergency, acting unofficiously and with intent to charge, is entitled to restitution of their reasonable value, even without the recipient's consent.

Why It Matters

  • It shows restitution as a separate cause of action, independent of contract and promissory estoppel (slides: plead all three in the alternative).
  • Two elements of unjust enrichment: (1) enrichment; (2) retention is unjust (slides).
  • Note 2: one who accepts services knowing they are offered for compensation is liable for reasonable value.
  • Note 3: Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 20 is stricter (requires professional services) but more liberal (eliminates the "officiousness" and "intent to charge" requirements).
  • Note 5 (Posner): restitution is efficient where bargaining is impossible. The law supplies the deal the parties would have made.

Other Key Points

  • Hypo 6, "I Never Asked for This" (slides): a nurse gives 40 minutes of emergency care to a collapsed stranger and sends a $500 bill. Apply the elements and consider consent, objection while conscious, and professional status.
  • Related: Capacity (mental incapacity), Sparrow v. Demonico.
