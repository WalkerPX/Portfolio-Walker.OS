Quick reference

Citation: 248 Cal. App. 2d 220, 56 Cal. Rptr. 435 (1967) · Casebook: Knapp p. 863 · Topic: Material Breach and Substantial Performance › Partial, material and total breach One line: A buyer who repeatedly failed to pay the balance for newspaper stock (with a bounced check and broken promises) committed a total breach, so the seller could terminate, resell and recover damages.

Overview

The course case distinguishing partial, material and total breach, and explaining when the non-breaching party may (1) suspend its own performance and (2) terminate the contract and sue for total breach.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / cross-defendant / appellant: Sheldon Sackett. The buyer of the newspaper company's stock. He sued for money had and received (to recover his $25,800 in payments).
  • Defendant / cross-complainant / respondent: Paul Spindler. The majority owner of S & S Newspapers (the Santa Clara Journal) and its publisher, editor and general manager. He counterclaimed for breach.

Facts

  • July 8, 1961: Sackett agreed to buy all 6,316 shares of S & S Newspapers for $85,000: $6,000 by July 10, $20,000 by July 14, and $59,000 by August 15, plus 6% interest on unpaid amounts. Stock was to be delivered on final payment.
  • Sackett paid $6,000 on time and $19,800 on July 21 (late). On August 10 he gave a check for the $59,200 balance, which bounced for insufficient funds.
  • Spindler acquired the minority shares and put most stock in escrow with Sackett's attorneys pending payment.
  • Over the following weeks, Sackett made repeated promises to pay, which he didn't keep. [Outside knowledge: he offered other arrangements, and Spindler eventually gave a final deadline.] Spindler finally declared the contract terminated and later resold the stock (at a lower price).

Procedural History

  • Trial court: Sackett takes nothing. Spindler awarded $34,575.74 plus interest on his cross-complaint.
  • Court of Appeal: affirmed. [Outside knowledge: on the main points.]

Issue

  1. Did Sackett's failure to pay constitute a breach that justified Spindler in withholding performance and terminating the contract?
  2. Was Sackett entitled to restitution of his payments?

Holding

  1. Yes. Sackett's failure to pay the balance, after repeated opportunities, was a total breach. Spindler was entitled to terminate and recover damages.
  2. No restitution. As the breaching party, Sackett could not recover his payments, which were offset by Spindler's damages.

Reasoning

  • Two problems for Spindler (slides): (1) Was he privileged to withhold his own performance until Sackett at least tendered his? Under Restatement (Second), that depends on whether Sackett's failure was at least material (as opposed to partial). (2) Was he entitled to treat the contract as terminated and sue for total breach? That depends on whether and when the breach became total.
  • Sackett's non-payment, which went to the essence of the deal, his bounced check and his repeated failures to perform after assurances, together justified termination.

Rule

  • Partial breach: not significant (e.g., a short delay or minor deficiency). The victim gets damages but must keep performing.
  • Material breach: failure of a significant performance. The victim may suspend its performance, but a material breach is necessary but not sufficient for termination.
  • Total breach: a material breach not cured within a reasonable time. The victim may terminate and sue for damages for the whole contract. (Restatement (Second) §§ 237, 241, 242.)

Why It Matters

  • Note 1: Sackett seems to treat "material" and "total" as synonymous. Restatement (Second) does not.
  • Note 2: substantial performance = a partial breach. The breaching party pays damages, but the other party is not discharged (Jacob and Youngs v. Kent).
  • Note 3, factors for when material becomes total: reasonableness of notice of the breach and opportunity to cure. A material breach isn't total unless further delay in terminating would unduly prejudice the other party (§ 242).
  • Restatement § 241 materiality factors: [Outside knowledge: (a) the extent the injured party is deprived of its reasonably expected benefit; (b) adequacy of compensation; (c) forfeiture by the breaching party; (d) likelihood of cure; (e) good faith and fair dealing of the breaching party.]

Other Key Points

  • Terminating too early, on a merely partial breach, can make the terminating party the one in breach. That is a classic exam trap.
