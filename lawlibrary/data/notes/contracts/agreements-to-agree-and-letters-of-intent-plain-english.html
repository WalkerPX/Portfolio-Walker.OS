The short version

Sometimes people agree on the big picture but leave key details to work out later. Whether that's a binding contract depends on whether they meant to be bound and whether a court can fill in the gaps.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Agreements to Agree and Letters of Intent.

What it's about

Business deals often start with handshake terms, a "letter of intent" or a promise to "agree on the rent later." The question is: have the parties actually made a contract yet, or are they still negotiating?

Two problems come up:

  1. Too vague? If an essential term is missing and there's no sensible way for a court to fill it in, there's no contract.
  2. Did they mean to be bound now? Some documents are written to be a deal; others are just a road map toward a final contract.

An everyday example

A landlord's lease says you can renew for three more years "at a rent to be agreed upon." When the time comes, you can't agree. Is the renewal enforceable? Under the traditional rule, usually not, because the parties never actually agreed on the price, and courts won't invent it for them.

How a court decides (plain checklist)

  • What did the parties' words say about being bound ("subject to a formal contract," "binding," "non-binding")?
  • Was anything important left open? Can it be filled in by a standard or formula?
  • Did the parties start performing as if there were a deal?
  • For sales of goods, the UCC is more forgiving. It fills gaps (price, delivery, time) if the parties clearly meant to make a deal. See UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation (Plain English).

The cases, in plain words

  • Walker v. Keith: a lease renewal with rent "to be agreed upon" was too vague to enforce.
  • Quake Construction v. American Airlines: a letter of intent that both awarded the job and mentioned a future formal contract was ambiguous, so a jury had to decide what the parties meant.
  • Jannusch v. Naffziger: a business sale had loose ends, but the parties' conduct (handing over the business and running it) showed they had a deal.
  • Pop's Cones v. Resorts International: even without a finished contract, a party who relied on assurances that "the deal is done" may recover through promissory estoppel.

Words you'll hear

  • Letter of intent (LOI): a document outlining a deal that the parties plan to finalize later.
  • Indefinite: too vague for a court to enforce.
  • Gap filler: a default rule a court or statute uses to fill a missing term.
