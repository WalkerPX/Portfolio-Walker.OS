In one paragraph

These excuse doctrines discharge a party's duty when an unexpected event after formation undermines the deal. A discharged party is not liable for breach. Impossibility: performance is literally impossible (Taylor v. Caldwell). Impracticability: performance is possible but so costly it defeats a basic assumption (Mineral Park Land v. Howard; Restatement § 261; UCC § 2-615). Frustration of purpose: performance is possible, but the reason for contracting is destroyed (Krell v. Henry; § 265). All three require a basic assumption, no fault of the party seeking excuse, and that the party did not assume the risk. Courts rarely grant relief. Market shifts don't qualify (Hemlock Semiconductor v. Solarworld). Frustration requires the contract to be rendered "virtually worthless" (Mel Frank Tool v. Di-Chem, Lloyd v. Murphy).

Impossibility

  • Classic cases: destruction of the subject matter, death or incapacity of a person essential to performance, supervening illegality.
  • Taylor v. Caldwell (1863): a music hall rented for concerts burned down, without either party's fault, before the performances. Both parties were excused. The court implied a condition that the hall continue to exist.
  • Modern law folds impossibility into impracticability (§ 261).

Impracticability

Checklist (Restatement § 261 / UCC § 2-615):

  1. Performance has become impracticable,
  2. because a contingency occurred
  3. whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption of the contract,
  4. without the fault of the party seeking discharge, and
  5. that party did not assume the risk (by contract or by circumstances).
  • Increased cost or lost profitability, even if extreme, is generally not enough (slide 4 NOTE).
  • Mineral Park Land v. Howard (1916): a contractor agreed to take all the gravel it needed from the plaintiff's land. The remaining gravel was below the water line and cost about ten times the normal price to extract. Performance was excused as impracticable, though not literally impossible. [Outside knowledge] the "ten times" figure; see the brief.
  • Hemlock Semiconductor v. Solarworld: long-term (about 14-year) fixed-price, take-or-pay contracts for polysilicon. Chinese government subsidies crashed the market price below the contract price, and Sachsen stopped buying. Impracticability rejected: continued market conditions are not a basic assumption of a long-term fixed-price contract. Fixing a price allocates market risk. Sachsen could have bargained for an escape clause. Frustration also rejected: the purpose (supply at a fixed price) was intact, and Hemlock stood ready to deliver.
  • COVID and campus closure: Shaffer v. George Washington University (tuition and restitution; Reliance and Restitution Damages › Restitution when the contract doesn't cover the issue).

Frustration of purpose

Checklist (Restatement § 265):

  1. The party's principal purpose is substantially frustrated,
  2. by the occurrence of a contingency
  3. whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption,
  4. without the fault of that party, and
  5. that party did not assume the risk.
  • Impracticability vs. frustration: frustration affects the value of what a party receives, not the cost or possibility of performing.
  • Krell v. Henry (1903): Henry rented a London flat to watch King Edward VII's coronation procession. The procession was cancelled because the King fell ill. Paying rent was still possible, but the purpose was frustrated, so Henry was excused.
  • Mel Frank Tool v. Di-Chem: Di-Chem leased a warehouse to store hazardous chemicals. A later city ordinance banned that storage. Frustration failed. Di-Chem still had a "serviceable use" (storing other materials, other business). "The contract must be rendered virtually worthless." The force majeure clause did not cover the facts. If the only possible use had been banned, the result might differ.
  • Lloyd v. Murphy: a lease for a car dealership. Wartime government restrictions on new-car sales did not frustrate the lease, because the tenant could still sell used cars or put the property to other commercial use, and the risk was foreseeable. Not in the casebook (slide mention only). [Outside knowledge] Cal. 1944; the citation (25 Cal. 2d 48) needs verifying.

Foreseeability, government action and force majeure

  • Foreseeability (Hemlock Note 3): foreseeability doesn't automatically bar relief. Under § 261 cmt. c, the mere chance that a party could have contracted against a risk isn't enough to assign it. But the more foreseeable an event, the harder it is to say its non-occurrence was a basic assumption.
  • Government action vs. market change (Mel Frank Note 1): claims based on government action do better than market-change claims, but frustration must still be substantial.
  • Force majeure clauses (Mel Frank Note 2): a well-drafted clause can strengthen or replace the common-law excuse. Courts read it according to its terms. Advice: negotiate one.
  • Remedy [Outside knowledge]: discharge; restitution for part performance (Restatement § 272). UCC § 2-615 allows a seller to allocate limited supply among customers.

Exam framework

  1. What changed after formation? (A pre-existing fact is Mistake.)
  2. Which doctrine? Impossible, impracticable (cost) or frustrated (value)?
  3. Basic assumption? How foreseeable was the event? Was it government action or market change?
  4. Fault? Did the party seeking excuse cause the event?
  5. Risk allocation: fixed price (Hemlock)? Force majeure clause? Insurance? Who is the better risk bearer?
  6. Degree: a cost increase alone isn't enough; frustration requires "virtually worthless" (Mel Frank).
  7. Remedy: discharge, restitution, or allocation under § 2-615.

Hypos (slides)

  • Hypo 1, "The Solar Contract": a 10-year fixed price of $80/kg. A U.S. technological breakthrough drops industry costs to $15/kg. GreenPower's cost is $75/kg on older equipment, so it is losing money, and there is no force majeure clause. Like Hemlock, this is market risk allocated by the fixed price, so no excuse. Variation: a U.S. regulation bans the main input chemical and costs rise to $200/kg. That is government action and a much stronger impracticability claim.
  • Hypo 2, "The Flower Shop": a 5-year lease next to Good Samaritan Hospital for a flower shop. The hospital announces it will close in 6 months. Is the purpose substantially frustrated? Can the space be used for other business (Mel Frank, Lloyd)? If the landlord Stewart knew of the likely closure at signing, consider nondisclosure or misrepresentation, or that Barlow didn't assume a risk known only to the landlord.
