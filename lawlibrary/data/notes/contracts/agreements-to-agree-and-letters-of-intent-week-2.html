Big picture

  • Two problems: (1) parties agree on some terms but leave others for later ("agreement to agree"); (2) parties sign a letter of intent before a formal contract.
  • Classical approach to open terms: no enforceable contract; courts won't fill the gap. Walker v. Keith.
  • Modern approach to letters of intent: enforceable only if the parties intended to be bound. Quake.
  • Recurring theme: courts split on whether to supply missing terms or hold parties to a duty to negotiate.

Doctrine outline

  • Agreement to agree (postponed bargaining): parties fix some terms, expressly leave others (e.g., future rental rate) to be worked out later.
  • Walker (classical): renewal option with no rent and no method (e.g., arbitration) to set it → no enforceable renewal contract. Court will not supply a "reasonable rent."
  • Walker acknowledges other jurisdictions will supply a reasonable rental term; the Walker court is simply not persuaded.
  • Quake (letters of intent): a letter of intent can be enforceable, but only if the parties intended it to be contractually binding.
  • Quake threshold question: is the letter's language ambiguous as to intent? If yes, the trier of fact weighs evidence beyond the letter to find actual intent.
  • Quake majority: contract is either binding despite a contemplated writing, or not binding until executed.
  • Quake concurrence: third option — a binding duty to negotiate in good faith toward the fuller agreement.

Tests and elements

  • Enforceability of a letter of intent (Quake):
    • Step 1: Is the letter's language ambiguous as to the parties' intent to be bound?
    • Step 2: If ambiguous, trier of fact weighs evidence beyond the letter itself.
    • Step 3: Determine actual intent — bound despite no formal writing, or not bound until execution.
    • Concurrence alternative: binding duty to negotiate in good faith.
  • Enforceability of a renewal/agreement to agree (Walker):
    • Was the term (e.g., rent) agreed?
    • If not, was there a definite method to determine it (e.g., arbitration)?
    • If neither → no enforceable contract; court will not set a "reasonable" term.
  • NOTE (Walker Note 1): court frames judicial rate-setting as "judicial paternalism," "as obnoxious to courts as…legislation by judicial fiat." Slides ask: do students buy that framing of the court's role?
  • NOTE (Walker Note 2): two reasons for refusing to fix rent — (1) cost/time of judicial fact-finding; (2) paternalism where parties could have resolved it themselves.
  • NOTE (Walker Note 3): other courts go the other way, often because a tenant reasonably and materially relied on what looked like an assurance of renewal. Refusing to fix a rate can effectively read the renewal clause out of the contract entirely.
  • NOTE (Quake Note 1 — drafting lesson): the Quake letter didn't clearly state whether the parties intended to be bound — hence the ambiguity. Fix: state expressly whether the letter is binding; if not binding until a formal contract is signed, say so.

Cases

  • Walker v. Keith — Lease renewal option; parties never fixed renewal rent or a method (like arbitration) to set it. Holding: no enforceable renewal contract; courts won't supply a "reasonable rent." Court notes other jurisdictions would supply a reasonable rental term but is not persuaded.
  • Quake Construction, Inc. v. American Airlines, Inc. — General contractor sent Quake a letter of intent saying a formal contract was being prepared and would be available for signature shortly. American Airlines/Jones Brothers later terminated Quake's involvement; Quake sued. Holding: letters of intent enforceable only if parties intended to be binding. Language was ambiguous (the cancellation clause cut against an intent to be bound before a formal agreement); on remand, trier of fact weighs evidence beyond the letter. Majority: binding despite contemplated writing, or not binding until executed. Concurrence: binding duty to negotiate in good faith.

Exam angles

  • Open-term problem: check for agreed term or definite method (arbitration). If neither, classical answer = unenforceable (Walker). Argue the modern/other-jurisdiction counter (reliance; reading the clause out entirely).
  • Letter of intent problem: threshold is intent to be bound. Look for ambiguity; then marshal evidence beyond the letter (cancellation clause, contemplated formal writing, reliance).
  • Three positions to argue (Quake): (1) binding despite contemplated writing; (2) not binding until executed; (3) binding duty to negotiate in good faith (concurrence).
  • Drafting fix (Quake Note 1): expressly state whether the letter is binding; if not binding until signature, say so.
  • Policy arguments (Walker Notes 1–2): judicial paternalism / "legislation by judicial fiat"; cost and time of judicial fact-finding; parties could have resolved it themselves.
  • Reliance angle (Walker Note 3): tenant's reasonable and material reliance on an assurance of renewal supports the contrary result.
  • Hypos: apply the above to FitIndy (offer/revocation — note the slides frame it as contract type and revocation, not open terms), World's Best Coffee (Walker applied), Waystar (Quake applied).
