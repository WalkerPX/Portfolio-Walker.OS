Demonico (Knapp p. 586) and Notes (pp. 588-591) · Topic: Capacity › Mental incapacity One line: The leading case adopting the volitional test: a contract (here, a pension election) is voidable when mental illness prevents a party from acting reasonably in relation to the transaction and the other party knew or should have known of the condition.

Coverage

Not reprinted. Slides call it "the leading application of the volitional test." Facts marked [Outside knowledge] are not in your materials.

Overview

Ortelere moved New York (and influenced Restatement (Second) § 15(1)(b)) beyond the purely cognitive test. A person who understands a transaction may still lack capacity if mental illness prevents her from controlling her conduct reasonably.

Parties

  • Plaintiff: Francis Ortelere [Outside knowledge], the husband and beneficiary of a deceased teacher. He wanted to set aside her retirement election.
  • Defendant: Teachers' Retirement Board of the City of New York. It administered the pension and defended the election.

Facts

[Outside knowledge] Grace Ortelere, a New York City schoolteacher, suffered a "nervous breakdown" (diagnosed involutional psychosis, plus cerebral arteriosclerosis) and was on leave. While ill, she revoked her earlier election (which provided a reduced allowance with the reserve payable to her husband at her death) and elected maximum lifetime benefits with no survivor benefit. She borrowed the maximum from the fund. She died about two months later, leaving her husband with nothing from the pension. The Board knew she was on leave for mental illness.

Procedural History

[Outside knowledge] The trial court set aside the election. The Appellate Division reversed. The Court of Appeals reversed and ordered a new trial.

Issue

Is a contract voidable where a party understood the transaction but, because of mental illness, could not act reasonably in relation to it?

Holding

[Outside knowledge] Yes, if the other party knew or should have known of the condition. Because the Board knew of her psychiatric leave, the election could be voidable, and a new trial was ordered.

Reasoning

  • [Outside knowledge] Modern psychiatric understanding shows that some mental illnesses impair volition (impulse control and judgment), not just cognition.
  • The cognitive test alone fails to protect such persons. The "other party has reason to know" requirement protects reliance interests.
  • The election was unwise, given her husband's circumstances and her shortened life expectancy.

Rule

Contracts are voidable where a party, by reason of mental illness or defect, is unable to act in a reasonable manner in relation to the transaction and the other party has reason to know of the condition (Restatement (Second) § 15(1)(b)).

Why It Matters

  • It is the leading application of the volitional test (Sparrow Note 1, slides).
  • It explains why Restatement § 15 has two prongs, and why the volitional prong includes a reason-to-know element.
