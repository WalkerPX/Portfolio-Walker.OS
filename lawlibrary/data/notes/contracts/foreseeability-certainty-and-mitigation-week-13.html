Big picture

  • Expectation damages are restricted by foreseeability, certainty, and causation (slide 19).
  • Foreseeability: recover only losses the breaching party had reason to foresee at contract formation.
  • Certainty: loss must be provable with reasonable certainty.
  • Mitigation: breachee must take reasonable steps to avoid loss; recovery is reduced by what could have been saved.
  • Purpose: prevent waste and overcompensation — plaintiff gets "as good as but no better" than full performance.

Doctrine outline

  • Foreseeability (Hadley rule): damages recoverable only if they (a) arise naturally from the breach, or (b) result from special circumstances communicated to the breaching party at contract formation.
  • Reasonableness is fact-specific: foreseeability requires case-by-case analysis of all relevant facts.
  • Restatement §351(1): no recovery for loss the breaching party had no reason to foresee as a probable result of breach when the contract was made.
  • §351(2): loss is foreseeable if it follows from the breach (a) in the ordinary course of events, or (b) from special circumstances beyond the ordinary course that the breaching party had reason to know.
  • §351(3): court may limit damages for foreseeable loss (e.g., exclude lost profits, allow only reliance) if justice requires to avoid disproportionate compensation.
  • Certainty — Restatement §352: damages not recoverable beyond an amount the evidence permits to be established with reasonable certainty.
  • Causation: defendant's breach must cause the plaintiff's damages (illustrated in Florafax).
  • Mitigation: breachee who refuses to mitigate recovers expectation MINUS what would have been saved had they mitigated.
  • Mitigation justifications: (1) precludes inflicting damage without benefit to plaintiff; (2) economic efficiency — incentive against waste of resources.
  • Avoidable consequences: plaintiff may not recover damages that could have been avoided by reasonable efforts (Restatement §350(1)).
  • Employment mitigation: employee must make reasonable efforts to obtain substantially equivalent employment.
  • Burden: breaching employer bears burden of proving failure to mitigate (majority view).
  • §350(1) standard: steps required only as can be accomplished "without undue risk, burden or humiliation."
  • Comparable work only: duty to mitigate applies only to jobs not inferior to the one lost.
  • Non-comparable job taken: damages still reduced by wages from that second job.
  • Net vs. gross profits: recover net profit only; gross income would overcompensate because it ignores costs avoided.

Tests and elements

  • Hadley test (two prongs):
    1. Damages arise naturally from the breach; OR
    2. Damages result from special circumstances communicated to the breaching party at the time the contract was formed.
  • Restatement §351 foreseeability:
    1. Loss must be foreseeable as a probable result of breach when contract was made.
    2. Foreseeable if it follows (a) in ordinary course of events, or (b) from special circumstances the breaching party had reason to know.
    3. Court may limit foreseeable loss to avoid disproportionate compensation.
  • Certainty (§352): evidence must permit establishing the loss amount with reasonable certainty.
  • Mitigation checklist:
    • Did breachee take reasonable steps to avoid loss?
    • Steps required only "without undue risk, burden or humiliation" (§350(1)).
    • Recovery = expectation MINUS what would have been saved.
  • Employment mitigation checklist:
    • Employee made reasonable efforts to obtain substantially equivalent employment?
    • Burden on breaching employer to prove comparable work was available and obtainable.
    • Only subtract amounts the employer proves could have been earned through reasonable efforts.
    • No proof → subtract nothing.
    • Duty applies only to non-inferior jobs; wages from non-comparable job still reduce damages.

Cases

  • Hadley v. Baxendale (English Exchequer Court): mill owner's replacement crankshaft delivered late; mill could not fill pre-existing orders; lost profits NOT recoverable. Defendant was not told of the pre-existing work orders; no reason to suspect lost profits from late delivery. Holding: only reasonably foreseeable damages recoverable.
  • Florafax International, Inc. v. GTE Market Resources, Inc.: GTE failed to provide sufficient telemarketing reps (esp. Mother's Day weekend 1990); Florafax/Bellerose contract cancelled; Florafax sued for lost profits. Lost profits satisfied Hadley rule — within contemplation of both parties at contract formation; GTE knew it would serve Florafax's contractual partners; Bellerose contract signed two weeks before Florafax/GTE contract. Court allowed net (not gross) profit. 60-day termination clause in Bellerose contract did not limit recovery; GTE showed no likelihood Bellerose would have terminated.
  • Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge Co.: County repudiated bridge contract shortly after work began; Luten continued and sued for entire bill ($18,000). Judge John J. Parker (4th Cir.): County liable only for costs to time of breach plus anticipated profit ($1,900). Illustrates avoidable consequences / duty to mitigate (Restatement §350(1)).
  • Maness v. Collins: terminated employee took no steps to mitigate (built himself a home). Court adopts majority view: burden on breaching employer to prove comparable work was available; employer offered no proof of "suitable, comparable alternative employment" → employer loses on mitigation; nothing subtracted. Court rejects minority view (no employer proof needed when employee fails to try). Also: three-year term (not at-will) → terminable only for cause; employer's prior breach (hindering performance, breach of implied covenant of good faith) excused Maness's duties; covenant not to compete (5 years, continental U.S.) probably unenforceable per footnote 8 (not resolved).

Examples and hypos

  • Hadley hypo (slides): mill with pre-existing orders; carrier not told → lost profits unforeseeable → not recoverable.
  • Mitigation example (slide 33): shipper brings perishables to dock; carrier fails to show. Shipper must try to sell — call a different carrier, even a more expensive one — and recover the difference, rather than let the fish rot.
  • Florafax Note 1 hypo: would result differ if Bellerose contract signed AFTER Florafax/GTE contract? Probably not — Hadley rule covers losses reasonably within contemplation at contract time, even from subsequent transactions.
  • Florafax Note 3 hypo: lost profits from contemplated third-party contracts not yet entered (esp. new business) would be a much harder certainty case.

Exam angles

  • Apply Hadley two prongs: natural consequence vs. special circumstances communicated at formation.
  • Ask WHEN the special circumstances were communicated — must be at time of contract formation.
  • Foreseeability is case-by-case; argue facts both ways.
  • Use §351(2) to classify loss as ordinary course vs. special circumstances.
  • Use §351(3) to argue for limiting disproportionate damages.
  • Certainty attack: is there an adequate basis to measure the loss? Pre-existing contract with substantial evidence = strong; contemplated future contracts / new business = weak.
  • Causation attack: would the third party have terminated anyway? (Florafax termination clause analysis).
  • Net vs. gross: always deduct costs avoided.
  • Mitigation: identify reasonable steps; apply "undue risk, burden or humiliation" standard.
  • Employment: burden on employer; no proof of comparable job → no reduction.
  • Employee who took inferior job → damages still reduced by those wages.
  • Watch for statutory claims alongside common-law wrongful termination — duty to mitigate may still apply.
  • Watch for prior breach by employer excusing employee's performance (Maness).
