The short version

Every contract carries an unwritten promise that neither side will act dishonestly or unfairly to cheat the other out of what the deal was supposed to give them.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Good Faith and Fair Dealing.

What it's about

Contracts can't spell out everything. The law fills part of the gap with an implied duty of good faith and fair dealing (Restatement § 205; UCC § 1-304). It means you can't use the contract's gaps or your own discretion to sabotage the other side's reasonable expectations.

Good faith does not:

  • create brand-new obligations the parties never agreed to, or
  • override clear terms. If the contract plainly lets you do something, you generally can.

It does limit how you use discretion the contract gives you.

An everyday example

You sign a deal that pays you a bonus "if the company decides your project is successful." The company has discretion, but it can't refuse to even look at your project just to avoid paying. It has to make the decision honestly.

Under the UCC

For sales of goods, good faith means honesty in fact plus reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing. Contracts to buy "all I need" (requirements) or sell "all I make" (output) must be performed in good faith.

The cases, in plain words

  • Locke v. Warner Bros: Warner had discretion to accept or reject Sondra Locke's film ideas. Evidence that it never intended to consider any of them could be bad faith.
  • Seidenberg v. Summit Bank: sellers who became bank employees could claim the bank fired them on a pretext to avoid paying their earn-out. Being sophisticated business people didn't bar the claim.
  • Wood v. Lucy Lady Duff-Gordon: an exclusive agent implicitly promised to use reasonable efforts, so the deal wasn't one-sided.
  • Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing: ending an open-ended distributorship for goods requires reasonable notice.

Words you'll hear

  • Implied covenant: a promise the law reads into the contract.
  • Discretion: freedom the contract gives one side to make choices.
  • Earn-out: extra sale price paid later if the business hits targets.

Connected ideas: Consideration (Plain English) (reading in a promise saves a deal from being illusory) and Express Conditions (Plain English) (you can't block a condition from happening).
