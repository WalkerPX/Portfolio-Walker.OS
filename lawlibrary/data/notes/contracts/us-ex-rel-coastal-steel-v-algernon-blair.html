Quick reference

Citation: 479 F.2d 638 (4th Cir. 1973) · Casebook: Knapp p. 1040 · Topic: Reliance and Restitution Damages › Restitution as a remedy for breach One line: A subcontractor that justifiably quit after the prime contractor's material breach could recover the reasonable value of its work in quantum meruit, even though it would have lost money on the whole contract.

Overview

The course case on restitution as an alternative remedy for breach. The non-breaching party may recover the reasonable value of its performance, without the contract price acting as a ceiling and even on a losing contract, to prevent the breaching party's unjust enrichment.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Coastal Steel Erectors, Inc., a steel-erection subcontractor, suing under the Miller Act in the name of the United States. It wanted the value of the labor and equipment it had furnished.
  • Defendants / appellees: Algernon Blair, Inc., the prime contractor on a federal naval hospital (in Charleston, S.C., [Outside knowledge]), and its surety.

Facts

  • Blair contracted with the United States to build a naval hospital. It subcontracted steel erection and some equipment to Coastal.
  • Coastal rented cranes to do the work. Blair refused to pay for the crane rental.
  • After completing about 28% of the subcontract, Coastal terminated performance because of Blair's refusal to pay. Blair finished the work itself.

Procedural History

  • District court: Blair's refusal to pay for the cranes was a material breach, and Blair owed Coastal about $37,000 for work done. But the court denied recovery because Coastal would have lost more than $37,000 had it completed the job.
  • Fourth Circuit: reversed and remanded with instructions to award Coastal the reasonable value of its labor and equipment, less payments already made.

Issue

May a subcontractor that justifiably stops work because of the prime contractor's breach recover in quantum meruit the value of its work, even if it would have lost money on the full contract?

Holding

Yes. Coastal may recover in quantum meruit the reasonable value of the labor and equipment it furnished, regardless of whether it could have recovered on the contract.

Reasoning

  • Denying recovery would let Blair keep the benefit of the very services it refused to pay for, which is unjust enrichment.
  • Quantum meruit ("what one has earned") measures recovery by the reasonable value of the performance: the amount for which such services could have been purchased from someone in the plaintiff's position.
  • The purpose of restitution is to prevent unjust enrichment, so the expected loss on the contract does not bar recovery.

Rule

A party injured by a material (total) breach may elect restitution of the reasonable value of the benefit it conferred, instead of expectation damages, even if it would have lost money on full performance (Restatement (Second) § 373). (In most jurisdictions the contract price does not cap the recovery, though some courts and the Restatement (Third) of Restitution would cap it. [Outside knowledge: on the cap debate.])

Why It Matters

  • Restitution's purpose (slides): no one should profit from their own wrong. It is measured by the defendant's gain.
  • Note 2: Restatement § 373 recognizes the right to elect restitution, as do recent decisions.
  • Restitution is a way out of a losing contract for the non-breaching party, a strategic choice on exams.

Other Key Points

  • Compare Lancellotti v. Thomas (the breaching party's restitution) and Commerce Partnership v. Equity Contracting (a subcontractor's restitution claim against the owner).
