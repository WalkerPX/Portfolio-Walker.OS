The short version

If someone receives a benefit they'd be unfair to keep for free, the law can make them pay for it, even without a contract. Sometimes a promise made after receiving a benefit becomes enforceable for the same reason.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Restitution and Promissory Restitution.

What it's about

Restitution is about unjust enrichment: one person got a benefit at another's expense, and it would be unfair to let them keep it without paying. It is not really a contract. Courts sometimes call it a contract "implied in law," meaning the law creates the duty to pay.

It does not cover:

  • Volunteers who give unrequested benefits when there was time to ask (you can't wash a stranger's car and then bill them).
  • Gifts that were meant as gifts.

Promissory restitution (§ 86)

What if someone helps you first, and afterward you promise to pay? Normally that's "past consideration" and unenforceable (see Consideration (Plain English)). But under Restatement § 86, a promise made in recognition of a benefit you received can be enforced to the extent needed to prevent injustice.

An everyday example

You're unconscious after a car accident, and a doctor treats you at the scene. You never agreed to pay, but you'd have to pay the reasonable value of that care. You got a real benefit, and the doctor obviously wasn't giving it away.

The cases, in plain words

  • Mills v. Wyman: a father promised to pay a stranger who had cared for his sick adult son, then didn't. The old rule: no consideration, so no enforcement.
  • Webb v. McGowin: a worker was crippled saving a man's life, and the man promised lifetime payments. Because he received a material benefit, the promise was enforced.
  • Credit Bureau Enterprises v. Pelo: a man hospitalized by court order had to pay the reasonable value of the necessary care.
  • Commerce Partnership v. Equity Contracting: an unpaid subcontractor can go after the property owner only if the owner hasn't already paid someone for the work.
  • Watts v. Watts and Marvin v. Marvin: unmarried partners can have contract and unjust-enrichment claims over property built up together.
  • Shaffer v. George Washington University: students who paid for in-person classes before COVID moved everything online could bring restitution claims.

Words you'll hear

  • Unjust enrichment: keeping a benefit it would be unfair to keep for free.
  • Quantum meruit: Latin for "as much as deserved," the reasonable value of services.
  • Implied-in-law contract: a duty to pay created by the law, not by agreement.
