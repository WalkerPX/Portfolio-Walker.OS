The short version

Parties can agree in advance on how much will be paid if someone breaches. Courts enforce the amount if it was a reasonable estimate of hard-to-predict losses, but not if it's really a penalty meant to scare people into performing.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Liquidated Damages.

What it's about

A liquidated damages clause sets the damages for breach ahead of time ("If the building isn't done by June 1, the builder pays $1,000 per day"). This saves everyone the cost of proving damages later.

The line courts draw:

  • Enforceable (liquidated damages): the amount is a reasonable estimate of the likely harm, and that harm was hard to measure in advance.
  • Unenforceable (penalty): the amount is way out of proportion and really aims to punish or pressure the other side into performing. Contract law compensates. It doesn't punish.

Sources: Restatement § 356; UCC § 2-718.

When do we judge "reasonable"?

  • Single look (traditional): judge the clause as of the day the contract was signed.
  • Second look: also consider the actual harm after the breach. If there was clearly no loss at all, a large fixed sum looks like a penalty.

An everyday example

A private school's contract says that if you withdraw your child after May 31, you owe the full year's tuition. That's because the school plans staff and budgets around enrollment and can't easily measure the cost of one empty seat. Courts often enforce this kind of clause.

The case, in plain words

  • Barrie School v. Patch: parents withdrew their child after the deadline. The clause was a reasonable estimate of hard-to-measure harm, so they owed the full year. The school didn't have to try to fill the seat, and it didn't matter that it ended up fully enrolled. A dissent argued that because the school lost nothing, full tuition was a penalty.
  • Compare Princess Cruises v. General Electric: a clause limiting how much can be recovered, the flip side of liquidated damages.

Words you'll hear

  • Penalty: an amount meant to punish, which courts won't enforce.
  • Pre-estimate: a good-faith forecast of likely damages.
  • Limitation of liability: a cap on how much a party can owe.
