Quick reference

Citation: 348 N.J. Super. 243, 791 A.2d 1068 (App. Div. 2002) · Casebook: Knapp p. 498 · Topic: Good Faith and Fair Dealing One line: Sellers of an insurance brokerage who became employees of the buyer bank could pursue a good-faith claim that the bank pretextually ended their employment and frustrated their earn-out. Sophistication does not bar the claim.

Overview

The course's framework case for the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. It sets out the three categories in which the covenant is successfully invoked and holds that a claim can survive even between sophisticated, counseled parties.

Parties

  • Plaintiffs / appellants: Seidenberg and his partner ([Outside knowledge: Richard Seidenberg and Mark Ramer]). Former owners of insurance brokerage firms sold to Summit. They became employees and were to receive earn-out payments. They wanted damages for breach of the implied covenant.
  • Defendant / respondent: Summit Bank. The buyer and employer. It allegedly sidelined and then terminated them to avoid paying the contingent compensation.

Facts

  • The plaintiffs sold their businesses to Summit Bank. The deal included employment agreements and compensation tied to future performance of the business. [Outside knowledge: earn-out payments based on the brokerage's future profits, plus promises to integrate and expand the business through Summit.]
  • Summit later terminated their employment, allegedly to avoid paying the bonuses and earn-out (slides), and allegedly failed to take steps it had promised to grow the business.

Procedural History

  • The trial court dismissed the good-faith claim ([Outside knowledge: on a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim]), reasoning in part that sophisticated parties' detailed contracts left no room for the covenant.
  • Appellate Division: the order of dismissal is reversed and the matter remanded.

Issue

Can sophisticated parties to a negotiated commercial contract state a claim for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing based on the other party's allegedly pretextual termination and frustration of the earn-out?

Holding

Yes. The claim should not have been dismissed. Sophistication is relevant, but it does not bar a good-faith claim. Whether Summit acted in bad faith is a fact question.

Reasoning

  • Every contract in New Jersey contains an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing (Restatement § 205).
  • Three categories where the covenant is successfully invoked (Knapp pp. 502-503, slides):
    1. Implication of a term necessary to protect the parties' reasonable expectations.
    2. Termination or other action by one party that appears to be in bad faith or pretextual.
    3. Exercise of discretion granted by the contract, which must be limited by fair dealing.
  • Bad faith turns on motive and intent: "Bad faith, like beauty, is in the eye of the beholder — or more to the point, of the trier of fact."
  • The parties' sophistication and the detail of their contract bear on what expectations were reasonable, but do not eliminate the covenant.

Rule

The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing applies to every contract, including negotiated contracts between sophisticated parties. A party breaches it by acting in bad faith (e.g., pretextually terminating or exercising discretion) to deprive the other of the fruits of the contract. Bad faith is generally a question of fact.

Why It Matters

  • The three-category framework organizes the whole good-faith unit: Wood and Leibel (implied terms), Seidenberg (bad-faith termination), Locke (discretion).
  • It is a reminder that good faith is not just a consumer-protection doctrine.

Other Key Points

  • UCC § 1-304: every contract under the UCC imposes good faith in performance and enforcement. UCC definition: "honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing."
  • "Fruit of the contract": neither party may do anything that destroys or injures the other's right to receive the fruits of the contract.
  • Limit: good faith constrains how rights are exercised. It does not create obligations the contract does not impose (Locke Notes).
