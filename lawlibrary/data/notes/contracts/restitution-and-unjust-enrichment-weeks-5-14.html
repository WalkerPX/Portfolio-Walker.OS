Big picture

  • Restitution = liability for unjust enrichment. "A person who is unjustly enriched at the expense of another is subject to liability in restitution."
  • Asks whether D was enriched at P's expense — not whether D harmed P.
  • Arises without any promise. Separate cause of action from contract and promissory estoppel.
  • Policy: prevent unjust enrichment — not enforce agreements, not protect reliance.
  • Three distinct causes of action, each pled separately with distinct elements: (1) breach of contract (enforcing bargained-for promise); (2) promissory estoppel § 90 (protecting reasonable reliance without consideration); (3) restitution (preventing unjust enrichment, no promise needed). One set of facts may support all three — plead in the alternative.
  • Two parts in the deck: Part I — restitution in the absence of a promise (pp. 287–315); Part II — promises based on past benefits / promissory restitution (pp. 317–329).
  • Remedies deck: restitution damages measure D's gain, not P's loss. Rationale: no one should profit from their own wrongdoing.
  • Restitution as contract remedy: available on breach (quantum meruit) and, under the modern rule, to a breaching party minus harm caused.

Doctrine outline

Part I — No promise

  • Involuntary/emergency services: one who renders necessary services in an emergency gets restitution for reasonable value even without consent (Pelo, Restatement (First) of Restitution § 116).
  • Distinguish contract implied-in-law (quasi-contract) from implied-in-fact (Pelo).
  • Related medical-services rule: one who accepts services knowing they are offered for compensation is liable for reasonable value (Pelo Note 2).
  • Restatement (Third) § 20: stricter (requires professional services) but more liberal (eliminates "officiousness" and "intent to charge") (Pelo Note 3).
  • Economic justification (Posner): restitution is efficient where bargaining is impossible; law supplies the deal parties would have reached (Pelo Note 5).
  • Commercial/subcontractor: subcontractor sues owner directly only on the two-element test (Commerce Partnership).
  • Restatement (Third) § 25(1): broad restitutionary liability when a party confers a benefit on a third party under contract with someone else (Commerce Partnership Note 1).
  • Contractor claims against lessors (lessee fails to pay) = harder case; lessor never contracted for improvements (Commerce Partnership Note 2).
  • Non-marital cohabitants: express/implied-in-fact contract and unjust enrichment both available; no writing required in Wisconsin; enforceable so long as claim not based on sexual services (Watts).
  • Unjust enrichment between cohabitants: benefits conferred; accepted with knowledge of expectation to share; retention unjust (Watts).

Part II — Promissory restitution (past benefit + later promise)

  • Classical (Mills): past services are not consideration for a subsequent promise; promise unenforceable. Moral obligation ≠ legal obligation.
  • Policy against enforcement: moral obligation varies among people; enforcing moral duty risks "judicial tyranny" (Mills Note 2).
  • If promise precedes services, services at request = consideration (Mills Note 1).
  • Legal duty of support (e.g., minor child) = family law, not contract (Mills Note 1).
  • General principle: a promise based on a pre-existing legal obligation IS enforceable (Mills Notes 3–6).
  • Some states require promises based on pre-existing debts to be in writing (Mills Note 5).
  • Modern (Webb): promise enforceable where promisor received a material benefit — not mere moral obligation. Restatement § 86 "promissory restitution."
  • § 86: promise in recognition of a benefit previously received is binding to the extent necessary to prevent injustice.
  • § 86 differs from Mills: past benefit + promise of recognition + unjust enrichment = enforcement.
  • § 86 limits: (a) not binding if benefit conferred as a gift; (b) not binding to extent value is disproportionate to benefit.
  • § 86 Comment f: does not apply where benefit was conferred under a pre-existing contract.
  • The promise adds: evidence promisor values the benefit, and a method of measuring that value.
  • Only a few jurisdictions endorse the material benefit rule — but widely discussed and pedagogically essential.
  • "Promissory restitution" more precise than "moral obligation"; § 86 requires promisor "has not been unjustly enriched" (Henderson, Webb Note 1).

Restitution as remedy for breach

  • P may elect restitution instead of contract damages (Restatement (Second) § 373; Algernon Blair Note 2).
  • Quantum meruit = "what one has earned" = reasonable value of services; measured by amount for which such services could have been purchased (Algernon Blair).
  • Recovery in quantum meruit permitted even if P would have lost money on the contract; otherwise D benefits from services it refused to pay for (Algernon Blair).
  • Traditional rule: breaching party recovers nothing on the contract or in restitution. Modern rule: breaching party recovers value of benefits conferred minus harm caused by breach (Lancellotti; Restatement (Second) § 374). UCC § 2-718 gives buyers of goods a similar right.
  • Modern rule avoids forfeiture by breacher but lets nonbreacher reduce recovery by damages suffered (Lancellotti Note 2).
  • Bad faith may justify denying restitution (Lancellotti Note 3, dissent).
  • Restitution unavailable when a contract specifically covers the issue; available when contract does not cover the issue or is invalid/unenforceable (Shaffer).

Tests and elements

Unjust enrichment — two elements (checklist):

  • (1) Enrichment of one person.
  • (2) Retention of that enrichment is unjust to another.

Subcontractor recovery from owner — two-element test (Commerce Partnership) (checklist):

  • (1) Subcontractor exhausted all remedies against the GC and remains unpaid.
  • (2) Owner has not given consideration to any person for the improvements.

Restatement § 86 — promissory restitution (checklist):

  • Past benefit received by promisor from promisee.
  • Promise made in recognition of that benefit.
  • Binding only to extent necessary to prevent injustice.
  • Limit (a): not binding if benefit was a gift.
  • Limit (b): not binding to extent value is disproportionate to benefit.
  • Comment f: not applicable if benefit conferred under a pre-existing contract.

Emergency/necessary services (Pelo, Rest. (First) § 116) (checklist):

  • Necessary services rendered to another.
  • In an emergency.
  • Entitled to restitution for reasonable value even without consent.

Measure of recovery — choose the measure (checklist):

  • Reasonable value of services (market-based) — commercial cases (Commerce Partnership; Algernon Blair quantum meruit).
  • Increase in defendant's wealth — intimate relationships (Watts; Commerce Partnership Note 2).

Breaching party's restitution (Lancellotti / § 374) (checklist):

  • Value of benefits conferred.
  • Minus harm caused by the breach.

Cases

  • Credit Enterprises, Inc. v. Pelo (p. 287) — Pelo involuntarily hospitalized; signed intake paperwork; hospital sued for reasonable value. Iowa Supreme Court: signed contract legally dubious given mental state; liability based on Rest. (First) of Restitution § 116 — necessary services in an emergency recoverable for reasonable value even without consent. Distinguishes quasi-contract from implied-in-fact.
  • Commerce Partnership v. Equity Contracting Co. (p. 298) — Subcontractor sued owner directly for unjust enrichment after GC failed to pay. Two-element test applied; Commerce wins because Equity failed to prove Commerce had not already paid the GC.
  • Watts v. Watts (p. 304) — 12-year cohabitation; Sue's four theories: family law division (rejected), estoppel (rejected), express/implied contract (allowed), unjust enrichment (allowed). Express/implied: no writing required in Wisconsin; not against public policy if not based on sexual services. Unjust enrichment: benefits conferred; accepted knowing she expected to share; retention unjust. 10% recovery on remand.
  • Marvin v. Marvin — named only (Watts Note 1: leading case; three arguments against relief — Family Code, legislative preemption, public policy — all rejected) — now briefed: Marvin v. Marvin.
  • Mills v. Wyman (p. 317) — Mills nursed Wyman's adult son Levi; after Levi died, Wyman promised in writing to repay expenses, then refused. Past services are not consideration for a subsequent promise; promise unenforceable. Moral obligation ≠ legal obligation.
  • Webb v. McGowin (p. 322) — Webb crippled saving McGowin from a falling pine block; McGowin promised $15 every two weeks for life, paid 1925–1934; estate refused to continue. Promise enforceable — McGowin received a material benefit, not mere moral obligation. Restatement § 86. Compare Mills: father received no material benefit.
  • Harrington — named only (Webb Note 1: "Webb and Harrington reconciled"). Needs a brief.
  • United States ex rel. Coastal Steel Erectors, Inc. v. Algernon Blair, Inc. (p. 1040) — Blair subcontracted steel erection to Coastal; Blair refused to pay crane rental; Coastal terminated after ~28% of job. District court found material breach and $37,000 owed but precluded recovery because Coastal would have lost money completing. Reversed: precluding recovery would unjustly enrich Blair; recovery in quantum meruit = reasonable value of performance (amount for which services could have been purchased); permitted even if P would have lost money on the contract.
  • Lancellotti v. Thomas (p. 1044) — P bought Ds' business for $25K, agreed to 5-year lease and to build a 16' x 16' addition (≥$15K); P refused to build, Ds built 20' x 40' addition for $11K; after a year P stopped paying rent and vacated to open a competing restaurant; Ds resumed operation, some equipment missing. P sued for $25K purchase price; Ds counterclaimed $52K ($6,635 unpaid rent + $45,335 damage to business and Mrs. Thomas' emotional distress). Modern view: breaching party recovers benefits conferred minus harm caused by breach (§ 374).
  • Shaffer v. George Washington University (p. 1053) — Students sued GWU and American University over virtual instruction during Covid-19 on three theories: express contract, implied-in-fact contract, restitution. Express contract rejected — in-person education not an explicit term. Implied-in-fact sufficient to survive motion to dismiss — communications touted on-campus benefits; higher on-campus tuition; "reservation of rights" provisions insufficient to allocate pandemic risk to students; remanded, including impracticability. Restitution claims also proceed — contract did not cover payments for tuition/services that could not be performed on campus; unjust enrichment left for district court.

Examples and hypos

  • Hypo 6 — "I Never Asked for This": Maria collapses; nurse gives 40 minutes of emergency care; Maria refuses to pay; $500 bill. Q: liable in restitution? Does lack of consent matter — what if conscious and objecting? Apply the two elements. What if nurse were a medical student, not licensed?
  • Hypo 7 — "The GC Disappeared": ElectroCo installs wiring for $80,000; GC bankrupt after receiving $75,000 from BuildRight but before paying ElectroCo; ElectroCo sues BuildRight. Q: two elements? Effect of the $75,000 paid? What if only $30,000 paid? What should ElectroCo have done before starting work?
  • Hypo 8 — "We Built This Together": Alex and Jordan cohabit 8 years unmarried; Alex gives up career to manage household and support Jordan's real estate business; they separate; Jordan claims all assets. Q: legal theories? Strongest? What must Alex prove for unjust enrichment? Damages — reasonable value of services or share of increase in wealth; which is larger?
  • Hypo 9 — "You Helped My Son": pre-med neighbor saves college student's life; father promises in writing "$5,000 for what you did for my son"; later refuses. Q: enforceable under Mills? What if neighbor were a licensed EMT? Does the written promise help or hurt? What exceptions to Mills?
  • Hypo 10 — "I Promise to Pay You Back": neighbor rescues homeowner's elderly mother from house fire; $12,000 smoke-inhalation treatment; homeowner promises in writing to pay all medical bills; refuses three months later. Q: apply Mills; apply Webb/§ 86; walk through § 86 elements and limits; what damages?
  • Shaffer damages illustration (Note 2): tuition — difference between tuition for rest of semester and value of virtual instruction (if implied-in-fact contract not discharged); other services — no damages until closure orders, then lost value. If discharged by impracticability: tuition — difference between tuition and cost of providing virtual instruction; nontuition services — percentage = days from closure order to end of semester over days in semester.

Exam angles

  • Always plead all three causes of action in the alternative — contract, promissory estoppel, restitution — with distinct elements of proof.
  • Run the two-element unjust enrichment test on every facts pattern.
  • Identify which measure applies: reasonable value (commercial) vs. increase in wealth (intimate relationships).
  • Subcontractor vs. owner: run the two-element Commerce Partnership test; owner's payment to GC defeats element (2).
  • Past benefit + later promise: start with Mills (unenforceable), then test Webb/§ 86 (material benefit to promisor). Distinguish on whether promisor personally received the benefit.
  • Check § 86 limits: gift; disproportionate value; pre-existing contract (Comment f).
  • Breaching party seeking restitution: modern rule (§ 374) — benefits conferred minus harm; flag bad faith (Lancellotti Note 3).
  • Quantum meruit available even if the contract would have lost money — otherwise D profits from its own refusal to pay (Algernon Blair).
  • Restitution is barred where the contract specifically covers the issue; available where contract is silent, invalid, or unenforceable (Shaffer).
  • Watch for the "legal success, practical failure" point — Watts's 10% remand recovery (same lesson as Marvin).
  • Note the policy contrast: Mills (moral duty → judicial tyranny) vs. Webb (material benefit → injustice to deny).
