Quick reference

Citation: 2010 WL 4629614 (Tenn. Ct. App. 2010) · Casebook: Knapp p. 946 · Topic: Limits on Damages › Mitigation in employment cases One line: A wrongfully terminated employee who built himself a house instead of job-hunting still recovered full damages, because the employer never proved that comparable jobs were available.

Overview

Extends mitigation to employment contracts. The employee must make reasonable efforts to find substantially equivalent work, but under the majority view the burden of proof is on the breaching employer to show such work was available.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / appellant: Maness. He sold his manufacturing business to the defendants and agreed to stay on as production manager under a three-year employment contract terminable only for cause. He wanted damages for wrongful termination.
  • Defendants / appellees: the new owners (SKM, LLC, Joannie Collins, Mike Smith and Josh Smith). They argued he was fired for cause and failed to mitigate.

Facts

  • Maness sold his business to the new owners and signed a three-year employment agreement (for cause termination only), plus a five-year, continental-U.S. covenant not to compete.
  • Friction followed. At some point Maness "sulked" in his office and played solitaire on his computer rather than working.
  • The court found the new owner had first breached by hindering Maness's performance (excluding him from his duties), violating the implied covenant of good faith. That prior breach excused his non-performance.
  • After termination, Maness took no steps to find work. He built himself a home instead.

Procedural History

  • [Outside knowledge: the trial court ruled for the defendants on key issues.]
  • Court of Appeals: the trial court's decision is affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded. Costs were taxed to the appellees.

Issue

  1. Was Maness wrongfully terminated?
  2. Must his damages be reduced for failure to mitigate when he made no effort to find work, and who bears the burden of proof?

Holding

  1. Yes. The employer's prior breach (hindering performance) excused Maness's later conduct, so the termination was wrongful.
  2. No reduction. The burden is on the breaching employer to prove that substantially equivalent employment was available and that the employee, with reasonable diligence, could have obtained it. The employer offered no such proof, so nothing is subtracted.

Reasoning

  • Majority rule on the burden: the employer, as the wrongdoer, must prove what the employee could have earned. The court rejects a minority view that excuses the employer from proving available work when the employee made no effort.
  • The court subtracts only what the employer proves could have been earned. Without proof, it doesn't know what to subtract, so it subtracts nothing.
  • Two reasons for placing the burden on the employer (slides): (1) it is the breaching party; (2) employers often have better information about the job market.
  • Footnote 8: the five-year, nationwide non-compete is probably unenforceable (the court did not need to decide).

Rule

A wrongfully discharged employee's damages are reduced by what the employee earned or with reasonable diligence could have earned in substantially equivalent employment, but the employer bears the burden of proving that such employment was available.

Why It Matters

  • Employment mitigation notes (slides): the duty applies only to jobs not inferior to the lost one. If the employee does take a non-comparable job, the earnings still reduce damages. For statutory employment claims, the duty to mitigate usually still applies.
  • Note 2: if the employee shows breach and a basis for damages, they recover absent affirmative proof from the employer of a reasonable opportunity to mitigate.
  • It shows prior material breach excusing the other party's performance (see Material Breach and Substantial Performance).
  • Compare Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge.
