The short version

Normally an offer can be withdrawn any time before it's accepted. An option is a promise to keep the offer open, and it's binding only if it's paid for, made by a merchant in a signed writing, or relied on.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Option Contracts and Firm Offers.

What it's about

Imagine someone says, "You have until Friday to decide." Can they change their mind on Wednesday? Usually yes. A bare promise to keep an offer open is a free promise, and free promises generally aren't enforceable (see Consideration (Plain English)).

There are three main ways to lock an offer open:

  1. Pay for it (an option contract). You give something, even a small sum, in exchange for the promise to hold the offer open.
  2. A merchant's "firm offer" (UCC § 2-205). A merchant selling or buying goods who promises in a signed writing to hold an offer open is bound, without payment, for the stated time (no more than three months).
  3. Reliance. If the other side reasonably relied on the offer, a court may hold it open to prevent injustice.

An everyday example

A homeowner says, "For $500, I'll give you 30 days to decide whether to buy my house at $300,000." You pay the $500. The homeowner can't sell to someone else during those 30 days. You bought that time.

The construction-bid problem

A general contractor gathers bids from subcontractors, uses the lowest one to build its own bid, and wins the job. Then the subcontractor says "Oops, our price was wrong" and backs out. Is the sub stuck with its bid?

  • Drennan v. Star Paving: yes. The general contractor reasonably relied on the bid, so the sub couldn't withdraw it. This is the majority view.
  • James Baird v. Gimbel Bros: the older, minority view. A bid is just an offer and can be withdrawn before acceptance.

Other cases, in plain words

  • Berryman v. Kmoch: a land option said "$10 and other valuable consideration," but the $10 was never paid. No payment, no binding option.
  • Normile v. Miller: when you learn the seller has sold to someone else, your chance to accept is gone.
  • Cook v. Coldwell Banker: once someone has substantially performed in response to an offer, the offeror can't pull the offer.

Words you'll hear

  • Option: a paid-for promise to keep an offer open.
  • Firm offer: the UCC's no-payment-needed version for merchants, in writing.
  • Irrevocable: can't be taken back.
