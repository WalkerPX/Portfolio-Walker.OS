Part 1. The architecture of contract law

Contract law answers four questions in order: Is there a contract? What are its terms? Was it performed or breached? What is the remedy? One question comes before all of them: what law applies? (Foundations of Contract Law, UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation › Which law applies).

Sources. The common law, summarized in the Restatement (Second) of Contracts, governs services, employment, land and most other deals. UCC Article 2 governs the sale of goods and changes the common-law rules on formation (§§ 2-204, 2-206, 2-207), firm offers (§ 2-205), the statute of frauds (§ 2-201), parol evidence (§ 2-202), gap-fillers, adequate assurance (§ 2-609) and excuse (§ 2-615). The CISG governs some international sales of goods (Zapata Hermanos v. Hearthside Baking).

Why enforce promises at all? The course offers several theories: respect for autonomy and the will of the parties; protecting reliance; promoting efficient exchange; and fairness, which polices bargains (unconscionability, duress) (Foundations of Contract Law › Theories of contract law (slides)). Each theory explains a different part of the doctrine. Consideration reflects the bargain theory. Promissory estoppel reflects reliance. Restitution reflects unjust enrichment. The defenses reflect fairness. On an essay, policy arguments drawn from these theories are often the tiebreaker.

Three ways to enforce a promise. Keep this map in mind for every fact pattern:

BasisRequirementUsual measure
Bargain (contract)Offer, acceptance, considerationExpectation
Reliance (promissory estoppel, § 90)Promise + foreseeable, actual, reasonable reliance + injusticeReliance (or expectation "as justice requires")
Restitution (unjust enrichment; § 86)Benefit conferred, unjust to retainThe defendant's gain

Standard forms. Most real contracts are forms nobody reads. Carnival Cruise Lines v. Shute (Problem 1-2) is the course's first look at this: a forum-selection clause printed on a cruise ticket. Its later echoes are the duty to read (Ray v. Eurice Bros), unconscionability (Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture) and the battle of the forms (Princess Cruises v. General Electric).

Part 2. Mutual assent

Topic note: Mutual Assent and Offer and Acceptance.

The objective theory. Contract formation depends on what the parties manifest, read through the eyes of a reasonable person in the other party's position, not on what they secretly intend. Lucy v. Zehmer is the classic case: Zehmer claimed he sold his farm as a joke over drinks, but his words and conduct (rewriting the contract, getting his wife's signature) reasonably appeared serious, so he was bound. Leonard v. Pepsico is the reverse: no reasonable person would take a TV ad offering a Harrier jet for Pepsi Points as a serious offer. Ray v. Eurice Bros applies the theory to written contracts. A builder who signed a contract incorporating specifications was bound by them, even though it claimed it had never agreed to them. Signing is assent, and the duty to read follows from that. The exception is when the other side misrepresents the document (Park 100 Investors v. Kartes).

Offers. An offer invites acceptance and creates a power of acceptance in the offeree. The key question is whether a reasonable person would think that saying "I accept" would close the deal. Price quotes, catalogs and ads are usually invitations to make offers, because the speaker didn't intend to be bound to an unlimited number of acceptances. Lonergan v. Scolnick shows the default: an ad and follow-up letters about land ("this is a form letter," "you would have to act fast") were preliminary negotiations, so no contract was formed when the seller sold to someone else. The exception is an ad that is clear, definite and explicit and leaves nothing open for negotiation (the old Lefkowitz fur-stole case [Outside knowledge]). Izadi v. Machado Ford shows that a bait-and-switch ad may be treated as an offer, so a dealer can't hide behind fine print it designed to mislead.

Acceptance. For a bilateral contract, acceptance is a promise. It must be unequivocal and, at common law, mirror the offer. A response that adds or changes terms is a counteroffer, which both rejects the offer and makes a new one. Acceptance is effective on dispatch (the mailbox rule). Revocation, rejection and counteroffers are effective on receipt.

Termination. The offeree's power of acceptance ends by rejection or counteroffer, by lapse (the stated time or a reasonable time), by the offeror's death or incapacity, or by revocation. An offer is revocable at any time before acceptance, even if it says it will stay open, unless it is an option (Option Contracts and Firm Offers). Revocation can be indirect: if the offeree learns from a reliable source that the offeror has taken definite action inconsistent with the offer (such as selling the property to someone else), the offer is revoked. Normile v. Miller combines these rules. The buyers' offer had a time limit, the seller made a counteroffer, and the buyers' power to accept the counteroffer ended when they learned the property had been sold to another buyer. The time limit in the original offer did not turn the counteroffer into an option.

Unilateral contracts. Some offers ask for performance, not a promise. The classical problem is revocation midway through performance. The modern rule (Restatement § 45) says that once the offeree begins the invited performance, an option is created: the offeror can't revoke, but the offeree isn't bound to finish. Cook v. Coldwell Banker applied this to an employer's bonus program: once the agent had substantially performed, the employer couldn't change the terms. Sateriale v. RJ Reynolds treated a loyalty-rewards program ("Camel Cash") as an offer for a unilateral contract, accepted by buying cigarettes and saving certificates. The breach of contract and promissory estoppel claims survived dismissal.

Exam pattern

Start with whether there is an offer. If the communication is an ad or a quote, explain why it is (or isn't) an offer. Then check acceptance (mirror image or UCC § 2-207), the method (promise or performance) and timing (mailbox). Last, check termination: lapse, rejection, revocation (direct or indirect) and whether an option exists.

Part 3. Definiteness and preliminary agreements

Topic note: Agreements to Agree and Letters of Intent.

A contract must be reasonably certain: definite enough that a court can tell whether there was a breach and frame a remedy. The classical rule refuses to enforce an agreement to agree. In Walker v. Keith, a lease renewal "at a rental to be agreed upon" with no formula was unenforceable. The court would not make the bargain for the parties. The modern trend (the Restatement and UCC § 2-204(3), § 2-305) fills gaps more readily when the parties plainly intended to be bound: a reasonable price, market rent, arbitration.

Letters of intent and preliminary agreements raise the same question: did the parties intend to be bound before signing a final document? Courts look at the document's language (for example, "subject to a definitive agreement"), how many terms are left open, the context and the parties' conduct. In Quake Construction v. American Airlines, a letter of intent was ambiguous. It contemplated a formal contract, but it also reserved AA's right to "cancel this letter of intent if the parties cannot agree on a fully executed subcontract agreement". A right to cancel suggests there was something binding to cancel. So intent was for the trier of fact, with extrinsic evidence. Where there is no contract, promissory estoppel may still protect reliance during negotiations (Pops Cones v. Resorts International). Compare UCC Article 2: Jannusch v. Naffziger (conduct showed a deal) vs. EC Styberg Engineering v. Eaton (no deal between sophisticated parties who customarily signed final agreements).

Part 4. Consideration

Topic note: Consideration.

The doctrine. A promise is enforceable as a bargain if supported by consideration. There are two formulations. Benefit/detriment: in Hamer v. Sidway, a nephew gave up drinking, smoking, swearing and gambling until 21 in exchange for his uncle's promise of $5,000. The detriment of giving up legal rights was enough, even though it may have benefited him. Bargained-for exchange (§ 71): the performance or return promise must be sought by the promisor in exchange for its promise and given by the promisee in exchange for it. Most courts use both. Run both on the exam.

Gift or bargain? The hard cases are conditional gifts: "come pick it up and it's yours." The test is whether the promisor wanted the condition to happen, or whether it was just a necessary step to receive a gift. In Pennsy Supply v. American Ash Recycling, taking "free" AggRite was consideration, because American Ash saved disposal costs: it wanted the material taken. In Kirksey v. Kirksey, a brother-in-law's promise of a place to live if the widow moved was a gift, even though she moved at great cost. That harsh result later inspired promissory estoppel. In Plowman v. Indian Refining, the company's promise to keep paying former employees who came to pick up their checks was a gift condition, and their past service was past consideration. In Dougherty v. Salt, an aunt's note to her nephew "for value received" was a gift: a recital of consideration doesn't make it so, and love and affection are motive, not consideration.

Past consideration and moral obligation. A promise made because of something already done is not bargained for. Mills v. Wyman (a father's promise to pay for care already given to his adult son) is the classic case of an unenforceable promise based on moral obligation. The modern exception, § 86 promissory restitution, is discussed in Part 6. Restitution.

Adequacy. Courts don't weigh whether the exchange was fair. In Batsakis v. Demotsis, a loan of 500,000 drachmae (worth about $25 at wartime rates) was enforced for a promise to repay $2,000. Gross inadequacy may signal fraud, duress or unconscionability, but it is not itself a defense. In Harris v. Time, opening an envelope was technically consideration, but the claim was dismissed as de minimis. Nominal consideration ($1 recited, not paid) is a sham under the classical view. The Restatement still accepts it for options (§ 87(1)(a)), but Berryman v. Kmoch refused to.

Illusory promises. A promise that leaves performance entirely to the promisor's discretion ("I'll buy what I want") is no promise. Courts save such deals by implying obligations. In Wood v. Lucy Lady Duff-Gordon, an exclusive agency implied a promise to use reasonable efforts, so the deal was enforceable. A promise can also be conditional without being illusory. In Marshall Durbin Food v. Baker, an at-will employee's continued work was consideration for the employer's promise of benefits on a change of control. Satisfaction clauses and termination rights don't make a promise illusory if they are limited by good faith or notice (Good Faith and Fair Dealing).

Exam pattern

Name the promise sued on. Ask what the promisee gave in return. Run the bargain test and the detriment test. Then look for the red flags: a gift condition, a recital, motive, past consideration, an illusory promise. If consideration fails, move to promissory estoppel and § 86.

Part 5. Reliance-based enforcement

Topic notes: Promissory Estoppel, Option Contracts and Firm Offers.

Restatement § 90(1): a promise that the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance, which does induce it, is binding if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. The remedy may be limited as justice requires. The doctrine grew out of the unfairness of cases like Kirksey v. Kirksey. It now does four main jobs.

  1. Family and gratuitous promises. Reliance replaces the missing bargain. The questions are whether the reliance was foreseeable, whether it was reasonable, and whether it was substantial. (Kirksey itself predates § 90.)
  2. Charitable subscriptions. Under § 90(2), a charitable subscription is binding without proof of reliance. Courts have long strained to find consideration or reliance in pledges. In King v. Trustees of Boston University, Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.'s letter depositing his papers with BU, saying he would give papers from time to time and that all deposited materials would become BU's absolute property on his death, raised a jury question of a promise supported by consideration (BU's care of the papers) or reliance.
  3. Offers relied on before acceptance. The subcontractor-bid problem. In James Baird v. Gimbel Bros, Judge Hand treated a supplier's price quote as an offer looking for acceptance, not reliance, so it was revocable until the GC accepted. In Drennan v. Star Paving, Justice Traynor implied a subsidiary promise not to revoke: a sub's bid that the sub knows the GC will use becomes irrevocable once the GC reasonably relies on it by using it in its own bid. That rule is now § 87(2). Drennan won the difference between Star's bid and the next-lowest sub. Note the asymmetry: the GC isn't bound to the sub. (Option contracts generally: consideration, or the § 87(1)(a) signed-writing rule, or the UCC § 2-205 merchant's firm offer. Berryman v. Kmoch refused to enforce a $10 "option" that was never paid, and also rejected promissory estoppel.)
  4. Reliance in negotiations. No contract was ever formed, but one side induced the other to rely. In Pops Cones v. Resorts International, Resorts assured Pop's that a lease at its casino was nearly certain, so Pop's gave up its existing location. New Jersey no longer strictly requires a "clear and definite promise." A jury could find reasonable reliance, and the remedy is reliance damages.

Remedy. The trend is to award reliance damages (out-of-pocket losses), with expectation reserved for cases where justice requires it (Walser v. Toyota Motor Sales: a mistaken Lexus dealership approval led to out-of-pocket damages, but no lost profits, because the deal was far from certain). See Promissory Estoppel › Remedy and Reliance and Restitution Damages › Reliance damages.

Promissory estoppel elsewhere. It also gets around the statute of frauds (§ 139; Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice) and can rescue indefinite or preliminary deals.

Part 6. Restitution

Topic note: Restitution and Promissory Restitution.

Restitution is the law of unjust enrichment. It asks not what was promised but what the defendant received and whether it would be unjust to keep it. The two elements are enrichment and injustice. The second element filters out volunteers and officious intermeddlers: you can't force a benefit on someone and send a bill.

Without any promise. Three settings in the course:

  • Emergencies. In Credit Bureau Enterprises v. Pelo (the slides call it "Credit Enterprises v. Pelo"), a patient involuntarily hospitalized after threats of self-harm argued he lacked capacity when he signed the admission forms. He was still liable for the reasonable value of the care, on a contract implied in law. Necessary services provided in an emergency, to protect life or health, support restitution without consent.
  • Construction chains. An unpaid subcontractor's improvements benefit the owner. But in Commerce Partnership v. Equity Contracting, recovery against the owner requires that the sub exhausted remedies against the GC and that the owner gave no consideration to anyone for the work. An owner that paid the GC isn't unjustly enriched.
  • Cohabitants. In Watts v. Watts, a woman who lived with a man for twelve years and contributed to the household and his business stated claims for an implied-in-fact contract and unjust enrichment. Public policy did not bar them. Marvin v. Marvin is the California precedent. Measure: in commercial cases, the reasonable (market) value of services. In intimate relationships, often the increase in the defendant's wealth.

After a benefit: promissory restitution (§ 86). Back to Mills v. Wyman: a father's written promise to pay for the care a stranger gave his dying adult son was unenforceable, because moral obligation is not consideration. Compare Webb v. McGowin: Webb was crippled diverting a falling pine block from McGowin. McGowin promised and paid $15 every two weeks for life, and his estate stopped paying when he died. The promise was enforced because McGowin received a material benefit. Section 86 makes such a promise binding to the extent necessary to prevent injustice, but not if the benefit was a gift, not to the extent the promise is disproportionate to the benefit, and not where the benefit was conferred under an existing contract (comment f). One way to reconcile Mills and Webb: in Mills, the benefit went to the son, not to the father who made the promise.

Restitution as a remedy for breach is covered in Part 20. Reliance, restitution and specific relief.

Part 7. The UCC and the battle of the forms

Topic note: UCC Article 2 - Scope and Formation.

Scope. Article 2 covers transactions in goods: things movable and identifiable at the time of contracting (§ 2-105). For mixed deals, courts ask which predominates. In Jannusch v. Naffziger, a food-truck business (truck, trailer, equipment, goodwill) was predominantly goods. In Princess Cruises v. General Electric, ship maintenance was predominantly services. The answer matters because it decides which formation rules govern, and so whose terms control.

Formation. Article 2 is relaxed: a contract can be made "in any manner sufficient to show agreement," including conduct (§ 2-204(1)), even without an identifiable moment of formation (§ 2-204(2)) or with open terms (§ 2-204(3)). In Jannusch, the buyers ran the business for months, and that conduct showed a contract existed. Their return of the truck was a breach. But intent still matters. In EC Styberg Engineering v. Eaton, the parties' practice of signing formal agreements, and their continued bargaining, showed they had not agreed to a 13,000-unit commitment.

The battle of the forms. At common law, an acceptance that varies the offer is a counteroffer, and when the other side performs, the last shot wins. Section 2-207 rejects this. (1) A definite and seasonable expression of acceptance is an acceptance even if it adds or changes terms, unless it is expressly conditional on assent to them. (2) Additional terms are proposals. Between merchants they become part of the contract unless the offer limits acceptance to its own terms, they materially alter the deal (surprise or hardship: disclaimers of warranty, remedy limits), or the offeror objects. (3) If the writings don't form a contract but conduct does, the terms are those on which the writings agree, plus Code gap-fillers. For different (conflicting) terms, most courts apply the knockout rule [Outside knowledge].

Princess Cruises as a case study. GE's Final Price Quotation excluded consequential damages and capped liability. Under the common law (the deal was predominantly services), GE's quote was a counteroffer that Princess accepted by letting the work go forward, so the cap applied and the judgment fell from about $4.5 million to $231,925. Under § 2-207, the cap would be a proposal that likely materially alters the deal and so drops out. The predominant purpose test decided the case.

Part 8. Good faith and implied terms

Topic note: Good Faith and Fair Dealing.

Every contract carries an implied duty of good faith and fair dealing in performance and enforcement (§ 205; UCC § 1-304). The UCC defines good faith as honesty in fact plus the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing. The duty fills gaps and polices discretion. It doesn't override express terms.

Gap-filling. In Wood v. Lucy Lady Duff-Gordon, an exclusive dealing implied a promise of reasonable efforts (now UCC § 2-306(2)). In Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing, terminating an indefinite-term distributorship required reasonable notice (§ 2-309(3)), so the dealer could recoup its investment and find a substitute. Wood carries out what the parties intended. Leibel imposes fairness.

Policing discretion. In Locke v. Warner Bros, Sondra Locke's development deal gave Warner discretion to reject her projects. Evidence that Warner rejected them categorically, without considering their merits (the deal had been arranged by Clint Eastwood, Locke's former partner), raised a triable issue of bad faith. Subjective good faith (honesty) is required where the discretion involves taste or judgment. Seidenberg v. Summit Bank lists three settings where the covenant works: protecting reasonable expectations by implying a term, pretextual or bad-faith termination, and discretion that needs limiting. Sophisticated parties can still claim bad faith, and it is usually a question of fact.

Links across the course. Good faith saves promises from being illusory (Consideration › Illusory promises and mutuality), underlies the prevention doctrine for conditions (Express Conditions › Excuse of conditions), shapes the reasonableness of demands for assurance (Anticipatory Repudiation and Adequate Assurance) and appears in Maness v. Collins (the employer's prior breach of good faith excused the employee).

Part 9. The statute of frauds

Topic note: Statute of Frauds.

The statute of frauds makes certain contracts unenforceable without a signed writing. The aim is to prevent fraud by perjured testimony that a contract existed. Use the three-step frame on every problem: within the statute? → sufficient writing? → exception?

Within the statute. The categories are suretyship, marriage, interests in land, contracts not performable within one year, and goods of $500 or more. The one-year provision is read narrowly: if full performance within a year is possible by the contract's terms, it is outside the statute, however unlikely that is. Termination by breach or excuse doesn't count as performance. A two-year employment deal is within the statute (Crabtree v. Elizabeth Arden, Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice). An "as long as the project lasts" deal may not be. Lifetime employment is traditionally outside, though some modern courts disagree.

The writing. It must identify the subject matter, show that a contract was made, state the essential terms, and be signed by the party to be charged (the defendant). It needn't be one document. In Crabtree v. Elizabeth Arden, signed payroll cards and an unsigned memo ("2 years to make good") were read together. The unsigned writing must clearly refer to the same transaction, and parol evidence may show assent to it.

Exceptions.

  • Part performance (land). Classically, the acts must be "unequivocally referable" to the oral contract (Burns v. McCormick, Cardozo). Beaver v. Brumlow softened this: possession plus valuable improvements (the buyers, the Brumlows, moved a double-wide home onto the land and spent about $85,000 on improvements) suffice if they would lead a reasonable outsider to "naturally and reasonably" conclude a contract existed. The remedy is specific performance. The Restatement asks whether reliance was reasonable and justice requires enforcement.
  • Promissory estoppel (§ 139). In Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice, Rice quit her job in Maryland and moved to Alaska on an oral promise of a two-year executive director post. The court applied § 139 without requiring fraud. Reliance must be reasonable, which includes reasonably believing the promisor had authority.
  • UCC § 2-201. The writing needs only a quantity and a signature. Other terms can be filled in. There are five exceptions: merchant's confirmation (no written objection within 10 days); specially manufactured goods; admission in court; payment made and accepted; goods received and accepted. The last three are enforceable only to the extent of what was admitted, paid or accepted. In Buffaloe v. Hart, an unendorsed check wasn't a signed writing, but handing it over was payment, and possessing and insuring the barns was acceptance.
Common trap

The writing must be signed by the party being sued, not the plaintiff. Under the merchant's confirmation rule, the sender's writing can bind a merchant recipient who stays silent for 10 days.

Part 10. The parol evidence rule and interpretation

Topic notes: Parol Evidence Rule, Interpretation.

The rule. When parties adopt a writing as their final expression (an integration), earlier and contemporaneous agreements can't contradict it. If the writing is also the complete and exclusive statement (full integration), they can't even supplement it with consistent additional terms. The rule doesn't touch later agreements, and it never bars evidence offered to interpret the writing or to show fraud, duress, mistake or a condition precedent.

Who decides integration, and how? This is the main split.

  • Classical (Williston / four corners). The judge looks at the writing alone. If it looks complete, it is fully integrated. In Thompson v. Libby, a written contract for logs said nothing about quality, so evidence of an oral warranty was excluded.
  • Modern (Corbin / Restatement). The judge first considers all relevant evidence (negotiations, circumstances, purpose) to decide whether the writing is integrated and what it means. In Taylor v. State Farm, extrinsic evidence was admitted on whether a general release (signed for $15,000 in uninsured-motorist benefits) also released a later bad-faith claim, because the language was "reasonably susceptible" to the narrower meaning. In Shay v. Aldrich (4-3), evidence was admitted that a release was meant to cover only two named officers, over a textualist dissent.
  • Merger clauses strongly point toward full integration but are not conclusive under the modern view. Other factors: whether counsel was involved, the parties' sophistication, the time spent negotiating.
  • Consistent additional terms are allowed if made for separate consideration or if they would naturally be omitted from a writing like this one (§ 216). Under the UCC, a term comes in unless it would "certainly" have been included [Outside knowledge].

The fraud exception, narrowed. In Sherrodd v. Morrison-Knudsen, an earthwork subcontractor agreed to a fixed $97,500 and claimed it was induced by oral representations about the quantity of work, which turned out to be about twice as much. The court barred the evidence because it directly contradicted the fixed-price term. The dissent called this a gutting of the fraud exception. Compare Hill v. Jones, where an integration clause didn't bar a nondisclosure claim, and Park 100 Investors v. Kartes on fraud in the execution.

Interpretation. The objective theory governs meaning. Plain meaning courts look first for ambiguity on the face of the document. Contextual courts admit evidence to show that the language is reasonably susceptible to another meaning. Under the UCC, course of performance, course of dealing and usage of trade (§ 1-303) always inform meaning and can supplement even a fully integrated writing (§ 2-202). Where both parties reasonably meant different things by a latently ambiguous term and neither knew the other's meaning, there may be no contract (Raffles v. Wichelhaus [Outside knowledge]). Construe against the drafter as a tiebreaker [Outside knowledge].

Exam pattern

(1) Is the evidence of a prior or contemporaneous agreement? (2) Is the writing integrated? Analyze under both the four-corners and the Corbin approaches. (3) Does the evidence contradict or supplement? (4) Does an exception apply (interpretation, fraud with the Sherrodd caveat, duress, mistake, condition precedent, trade usage)?

Part 11. Capacity

Topic note: Capacity.

Defenses assume a contract was otherwise properly formed and ask whether one party may escape it. They work as a shield (a defense to enforcement) or a sword (rescission plus restitution). Capacity is the first type: defenses based on the person's status.

Minors. A minor's contracts are voidable by the minor, not void. The minor may disaffirm before turning 18 or within a reasonable time after. Keeping the benefits or doing nothing for too long after majority is ratification. A minor is liable in restitution for the reasonable value of necessaries. The interesting question is what the minor must give back on disaffirmance. Traditionally, only what remains. Dodson v. Shrader moved toward fairness to sellers: a 16-year-old paid $4,900 for a used truck, drove it until the engine failed, and disaffirmed. The seller may offset depreciation and use if it did not overreach, defraud or take unfair advantage. The tension is that the offset takes away much of the protection minority is supposed to give.

Mental incapacity. If a person has been adjudicated incompetent, their contracts are void. Otherwise they are voidable under either of two tests. The cognitive test: the person couldn't understand the nature and consequences of the transaction. The volitional test: the person couldn't act reasonably in relation to it, and the other party had reason to know. In Ortelere v. Teachers Retirement Board, a teacher with depression made a disastrous retirement election, and the volitional test applied because the board knew of her condition. In Sparrow v. Demonico, a woman trying to escape a mediated settlement needed medical evidence of incapacity. Lay testimony wasn't enough. If a contract was made on fair terms with someone who didn't know of the incapacity, a court may limit avoidance where reliance makes it unjust (§ 15(2)). Minors get no such limit.

Part 12. Duress and undue influence

Topic note: Duress and Undue Influence.

The second type of defense concerns defects in the bargaining process.

Duress. Physical compulsion means there was never assent, so the contract is void. Economic duress makes it voidable. Totem Marine v. Alyeska Pipeline sets out three elements: (1) a wrongful or improper threat; (2) no reasonable alternative; (3) the threat induced the agreement. Totem, owed about $300,000 and facing bankruptcy, settled for $97,500 after Alyeska allegedly withheld payment knowing Totem was desperate. That stated a claim. The key points are that a threat can be wrongful without being illegal (a bad-faith threat to withhold what is owed), and that "sue them" is not a reasonable alternative when the creditor needs cash now. By contrast, threatening to do what you have a legal right to do, in good faith, is not duress (Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District: threatening dismissal proceedings).

Undue influence. This is unfair persuasion of someone vulnerable to it, by a person who dominates them or whom they trust. Bad faith is not required. In Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District, a teacher was arrested, went 40 hours without sleep, and was visited at home by his superintendent and principal, who pressed him to resign immediately and warned of publicity. The court ruled out duress, fraud, mistake and incapacity, but found undue influence adequately pleaded. The Odorizzi factors signal overpersuasion: an unusual time or place, insistence on finishing at once, emphasis on the consequences of delay, multiple persuaders, no third-party advisers, and statements that there is no time to consult a lawyer. In Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing, farm workers signed arbitration agreements in a hotel parking lot at the end of a long day, in a language many didn't fully understand. The majority still found neither duress nor undue influence. The dissent pointed to a deliberately coercive atmosphere. On an exam, use Martinez-Gonzalez to show the limits of these defenses, and then argue unconscionability in the alternative.

Part 13. Misrepresentation and nondisclosure

Topic note: Misrepresentation and Nondisclosure.

A misrepresentation is an assertion not in accord with the facts. Sort it first:

  • Fraud in the execution: the victim is deceived about what the document is. The contract is void. In Park 100 Investors v. Kartes, the Karteses signed a personal guaranty that the landlord's agent called "lease papers," and they were not bound. Normally the duty to read binds signers (Ray v. Eurice Bros), but active misrepresentation of the document's nature overrides it. Whether reliance was reasonable is a fact question.
  • Fraud in the inducement: a knowing or reckless misrepresentation of fact, meant to induce assent, that does induce it, with justified reliance. The contract is voidable, and tort damages are possible. In Syester v. Banta, an elderly widow was flattered into nearly $30,000 of Arthur Murray dance lessons ("you could be a professional dancer") and then talked into releasing her claims without a lawyer. The jury could find fraudulent overreaching, and the judgment was affirmed. The doctrinal puzzle is reliance: she had already sued, alleging she had been lied to. Undue influence may explain the result better.
  • Nonfraudulent misrepresentation: voidable if material and justifiably relied on.
  • Opinions are actionable if the speaker knew they were false, if there was a relationship of trust, or if the opinion implied facts.

Silence. The classical rule is caveat emptor. In Laidlaw v. Organ, a tobacco buyer who knew the War of 1812 had ended didn't have to tell the seller, but couldn't lie if asked. The modern rule (§ 161; Hill v. Jones) imposes a duty to disclose material, latent facts the other side can't readily discover, where silence amounts to bad faith. The four routes are a statute requiring disclosure, active concealment, a relationship of trust, or correcting the other party's mistake about a basic assumption. In Hill, sellers who knew of a prior termite infestation had to disclose it.

Part 14. Unconscionability

Topic note: Unconscionability.

Unconscionability lets a court refuse to enforce a contract, or a term, that was unconscionable when made. Most courts need both procedural unconscionability (no meaningful choice, or hidden or unintelligible terms) and substantive unconscionability (terms unreasonably favorable to one side). The prongs are weighed on a sliding scale. The court decides.

Williams. In Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture, a welfare recipient bought furniture and then a stereo under a cross-collateral clause that let the store repossess everything she had bought. The D.C. Circuit held that unconscionability was a valid defense and remanded. It did not find the contract unconscionable. Professor Leff's paternalism critique asks why a court should second-guess a bargain the buyer chose. The answer: hidden terms and pressure mean the choice wasn't real.

Arbitration. The FAA preempts state rules that target arbitration, but generally applicable defenses survive. In Higgins v. Superior Court, orphaned siblings featured on Extreme Makeover: Home Edition signed an adhesive agreement with a one-sided arbitration clause. It was procedurally (adhesion, minors not expected to read it) and substantively (lack of bilaterality) unconscionable, so arbitration was not compelled.

Price. Courts rarely strike down price alone. But in De La Torre v. CashCall, the California Supreme Court held that interest rates of 96-135% on $2,500+ consumer loans can be unconscionable, even though the legislature set no cap for loans that size. Courts can fashion remedies without becoming rate-setters. The factors: the lender's costs and risks, how salient the term is to consumers, the borrower's creditworthiness, and market imperfections.

Remedies. Refuse to enforce, sever the term, or limit it (§ 208; UCC § 2-302). Unconscionability is traditionally a defense. In re Checking Account Overdraft Litigation is the minority view allowing affirmative recovery.

Part 15. Mistake

Topic note: Mistake.

Mistake concerns facts existing at formation. Future events belong to impracticability. The pivot is always who bears the risk (§ 154: by agreement, by conscious ignorance, or as the court allocates it).

Mutual mistake. Both parties were wrong about a basic assumption, with a material effect. The adversely affected party may avoid unless it bears the risk. In Lenawee County Board of Health v. Messerly, both sides thought an apartment building was habitable, but a former owner's illegal septic system made it unusable. That was mutual mistake, but the "as is" clause put the risk on the buyers. Lenawee also discarded the older test of Sherwood v. Walker (the "barren" cow that was pregnant: a mistake about nature versus quality) as unworkable. Compare Gartner v. Eikill: zoning is public record, so the buyer bore the risk.

Unilateral mistake. The same elements, plus either unconscionable enforcement or that the other party knew, had reason to know, or caused the mistake. In BMW Financial Services v. Deloach, BMW's own clerical error led its collection agency to settle a $114,000 judgment for $14,000. BMW bore the risk, and losing a mostly treble-damages windfall wasn't unconscionable. Compare Donovan v. RRL Corp, where a dealer's proofreading error advertised a Jaguar $12,000 below its intended price. The error was made in good faith and enforcement would have been unconscionable, so the dealer could rescind.

Distinguish misrepresentation (one side caused the false belief) and nondisclosure (one side knew and kept quiet). Ordinary negligence doesn't bar relief for mistake. Only gross negligence or bad faith does (§ 157).

Part 16. Changed circumstances

Topic note: Impracticability and Frustration.

These doctrines deal with events after formation. The contract is fine. The world changed. Every version needs (1) an event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption, (2) no fault of the party seeking excuse, and (3) that the party did not assume the risk. Then:

  • Impossibility: Taylor v. Caldwell. A music hall burned before the concerts, and both sides were excused.
  • Impracticability (§ 261; UCC § 2-615): performance is possible but unreasonably expensive, far beyond the normal range. In Mineral Park Land v. Howard, gravel below the water line cost about ten times the normal price to extract, and the contractor was excused. But market changes are what fixed-price contracts allocate. In Hemlock Semiconductor v. Solarworld, subsidized Chinese competition crashed polysilicon prices, and the buyer was not excused from its long-term take-or-pay contracts.
  • Frustration of purpose (§ 265): performance is still possible, but its value to one party is destroyed. In Krell v. Henry, a flat was rented to watch a coronation procession that was cancelled, and the renter was excused. The bar is high. In Mel Frank Tool v. Di-Chem, an ordinance barred storing hazardous chemicals in a leased warehouse, but the tenant could still use it for other purposes. The contract must be "virtually worthless." Lloyd v. Murphy (slide mention only; not in the casebook) reached the same result for a car dealership lease during wartime sales restrictions.

Foreseeability doesn't automatically bar excuse, but the more foreseeable the event, the more likely the party assumed the risk. Government action claims fare better than market-shift claims. A force majeure clause can expand, narrow or replace the default rules, so read it first (Mel Frank). Compare COVID-19 and tuition in Shaffer v. George Washington University, remanded in part on impracticability.

Hypo to practice. The slides' solar contract: costs drop because of a U.S. technological breakthrough → no excuse (market risk). Costs rise to $200/kg because a regulation bans the main input → a strong impracticability claim. The flower shop next to a hospital that closes: is the purpose substantially frustrated, or can it serve other customers? Did the landlord know the hospital would close?

Part 17. Conditions

Topic note: Express Conditions.

A condition is an event that must occur before a duty to perform becomes due. A promise is a commitment whose breach gives damages. Many terms are both. If a condition fails, the other party simply doesn't have to perform. If a promise is broken, there are damages.

Express conditions are strictly enforced. In Oppenheimer v. Oppenheim Appel Dixon, a sublease was conditioned on written notice of the landlord's consent by a deadline. A phone call on the deadline didn't satisfy it, and the jury's finding of "substantial performance" was irrelevant. In Maxton Builders v. Lo Galbo, a cancellation notice that arrived a few days late failed, and the lawyer who caused it was liable for malpractice. Because strict enforcement can cause forfeiture, courts (a) read doubtful language as a promise rather than a condition (§ 227), and (b) excuse conditions through waiver (only non-material conditions, and only by the party they protect), prevention (bad-faith interference), or disproportionate forfeiture (§ 229). In JNA Realty v. Cross Bay Chelsea, a restaurant tenant gave its renewal notice months late through carelessness, after investing heavily. Equity could excuse the condition if there was forfeiture, no prejudice to the landlord, and only "venial inattention."

Constructive conditions are default rules courts supply: each party's substantial performance is a condition of the other's duty. That is where Jacob and Youngs v. Kent comes in (Part 18).

Part 18. Breach and repudiation

Topic notes: Material Breach and Substantial Performance, Anticipatory Repudiation and Adequate Assurance.

Substantial performance. In Jacob and Youngs v. Kent, the builder used pipe of equal quality from makers other than the specified "Reading." It was an innocent breach, and the pipe was sealed in the walls. Kent couldn't withhold the final $3,483.46, because the builder had substantially performed. Damages were the (nominal) difference in value, not the cost of tearing out walls. Cardozo stressed good faith and avoiding forfeiture for "venial faults." Factors: the purpose of the term, the reason for the shortfall (willfulness), the cost to remedy it, and whether damages are adequate.

The breach spectrum. A partial breach gives damages only, and the injured party keeps performing. A material breach lets it suspend performance while the breacher has time to cure. If the breach is not cured, it becomes total, and the injured party may terminate and claim full damages. In Sackett v. Spindler, a stock buyer's bounced check for the $59,200 balance and repeated unkept promises added up to a total breach, so the seller could resell and keep payments to cover its damages. Terminating too soon risks becoming the breacher.

Anticipatory repudiation. A definite and unequivocal statement or act, before performance is due, that a party won't perform is a total breach. The injured party may sue at once. In Truman L. Flatt and Sons v. Schupf, a buyer's letter dropping the zoning application and asking for a lower price was not a repudiation, just an attempt to renegotiate. In any event the buyer retracted it before the sellers relied on it or treated it as final. After a repudiation, the injured party must still mitigate (Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge).

Adequate assurance. Where there are reasonable grounds for insecurity but no clear repudiation, UCC § 2-609 (and Restatement § 251) lets the insecure party demand assurance in writing and suspend performance. Failing to respond within a reasonable time (at most 30 days under the UCC) is a repudiation. In Hornell Brewing v. Spry, a distributor with arrears, a bounced check and no real operation, who then placed an order over its credit limit, ignored Hornell's demand for proof of credit. Hornell could terminate.

Part 19. Expectation damages and their limits

Topic notes: Expectation Damages, Limits on Damages.

The goal. Put the injured party where performance would have put it. The formula: loss in value + other loss − cost avoided − loss avoided (§ 347). Work the course's two examples until they're automatic: the builder ($130,000 − $85,000 − $10,000 = $35,000) and the employee ($75,000 + $1,000 − $45,000 = $31,000).

Contexts.

  • Land: contract price vs. market value at the time of breach. In Crabbys v. Hamilton, a resale within about a year, not a distress sale, was good evidence of market value.
  • Employment: an employer whose employee quits recovers the extra cost of the replacement. An upgrade it couldn't avoid is "imposed upon" it, not a benefit (Handicapped Childrens Education Board v. Lukaszewski).
  • Construction: cost of completion, unless it is wasteful compared with the gain in value. Then use diminution in value (Jacob and Youngs v. Kent).

Limits.

  • Foreseeability. Under Hadley v. Baxendale, general damages arise naturally. Special damages require that the circumstances were communicated when the contract was made. A carrier wasn't told a mill would sit idle without its shaft, so there were no lost profits. In Florafax v. GTE Market Resources, GTE knew its telemarketing work served Florafax's contract partners, so the lost Bellerose profits were foreseeable. Net profits only.
  • Certainty (§ 352). New ventures often can't prove lost profits. Fall back on reliance (Wartzman v. Hightower Productions).
  • Mitigation (§ 350). In Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge, a builder that kept building after the county repudiated got only its costs to the breach plus profit. A wrongfully fired employee must seek comparable work, but the employer must prove it was available (Maness v. Collins). Wages from any job actually taken are deducted.
  • Attorney fees. The American rule: each side pays its own, unless a contract, statute or court rule says otherwise. In Zapata Hermanos v. Hearthside Baking, the CISG doesn't make fees consequential damages, and pre-suit bad faith can't be sanctioned with fees.
  • Emotional distress. Generally not recoverable. In Erlich v. Menezes, a defective dream house didn't qualify. Only where emotional concerns are the essence of the contract (or there is bodily harm).

Part 20. Reliance, restitution and specific relief

Topic notes: Reliance and Restitution Damages, Specific Performance.

Reliance damages put the plaintiff where it would have been if the promise had never been made: out-of-pocket costs. Use them when expectation is too uncertain (Wartzman v. Hightower Productions: a law firm's securities blunder killed a promotional venture, so the investors recovered what they put in) and as the usual promissory estoppel remedy (Walser v. Toyota Motor Sales). The same limits apply: foreseeability, causation, mitigation. [Outside knowledge] A defendant can reduce reliance damages by proving the contract would have lost money.

Restitution for breach. The injured party may instead recover the value of what it conferred. On a losing contract, that can exceed what the contract would have paid. In US ex rel Coastal Steel v. Algernon Blair, a sub who stopped work after the GC refused to pay for crane rental recovered quantum meruit for its work, regardless of whether it would have lost money. Even a breaching party may recover benefits conferred minus the harm it caused (§ 374). In Lancellotti v. Thomas, a buyer who abandoned a luncheonette business could seek back the part of his $25,000 payment that exceeded the sellers' proven damages. Compare Sackett v. Spindler, where the payments were offset by damages. Restitution is unavailable where an express contract covers the issue. In Shaffer v. George Washington University, no express term promised in-person classes, so implied-contract and unjust-enrichment claims for COVID-era tuition went forward.

Specific performance. An equitable remedy, available only where damages are inadequate (land, unique goods, damages too uncertain to prove). It is subject to equitable factors (hardship, delay, benefit received, the plaintiff's reliance) and practical limits (definiteness, supervision). In City Stores v. Ammerman, developers who used a department store's support to get zoning approval for their mall were ordered to give it a lease. The missing terms came from the other anchors' leases, and future profits were "almost incalculable." Personal services are never ordered. In Reier Broadcasting v. Kramer, even a negative injunction against a coach working for a rival station was refused, and damages were the remedy. Under the UCC, goods must be unique "or in other proper circumstances" (§ 2-716).

Part 21. Agreed remedies

Topic note: Liquidated Damages.

Parties may agree in advance on damages. Courts enforce a reasonable forecast of harm that was hard to estimate, and strike a penalty meant to compel performance. The traditional test: (1) damages were uncertain or hard to prove; (2) the parties intended compensation; (3) the amount is a reasonable forecast. Restatement § 356 asks whether the amount is reasonable in light of anticipated or actual loss. The live split is when to judge reasonableness. The majority takes a single look at formation (Barrie School v. Patch: a private school kept a full year's tuition after a late withdrawal, even though it filled the seat). The dissent and the modern trend take a second look at actual harm. A valid clause replaces proof of damages and the duty to mitigate. Doubts are resolved toward finding a penalty, and the challenger bears the burden. There is no slide deck for this topic; the source is Liquidated damages (Week 15).

Part 22. Third parties

Topic note: Third-Party Beneficiaries and Assignment.

Not in the Fall 2026 syllabus. Everything here is [Outside knowledge]. Intended beneficiaries (creditor or donee) can enforce; incidental beneficiaries can't. A beneficiary's rights vest once it relies, sues or assents. Rights are generally assignable and duties delegable unless the change materially affects the other side, the performance is personal, or the contract forbids it. The delegating party stays liable without a novation. Confirm with the professor before studying this for the exam.

Part 23. Writing the essay

  • Organize by issue, in the logical order: applicable law → formation → enforceability (statute of frauds, defenses) → terms (PER, interpretation, implied terms) → performance (conditions, breach, excuse) → remedies.
  • For each issue: state the rule (cite the leading case), apply the facts on both sides, conclude, and move on. Professor Lourie's practice materials reward alternative arguments: if no contract, then promissory estoppel; if no promissory estoppel, then restitution. See Contracts Essay Writing Guide - Lourie.pdf in Practice/.
  • Use the hypos. Each topic note has a "Hypos (slides)" section. The professor tends to change one fact (a price, a date, merchant status, an "as is" clause) to flip the result.
  • Common flips to watch:
    • goods vs. services (UCC vs. common law; § 2-207 vs. mirror image);
    • merchant vs. non-merchant (§ 2-207(2), § 2-201(2), § 2-205);
    • express condition vs. promise (strict compliance vs. substantial performance);
    • repudiation vs. a request to renegotiate (Flatt);
    • "as is" clause present or absent (Lenawee vs. the flooded-lot hypo);
    • a windfall vs. a real loss (BMW vs. Donovan);
    • comparable job available and proven by the employer, or not (Maness).
  • Remedies: always compute numbers when the facts give them, show the formula, and then apply the limits.
