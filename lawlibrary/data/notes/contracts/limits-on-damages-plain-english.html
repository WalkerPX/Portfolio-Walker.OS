The short version

You can't recover every loss a breach causes. Losses must have been foreseeable when the deal was made, provable with reasonable certainty, and not ones you could have reasonably avoided. Some kinds of losses are almost never recoverable.

Written for readers with no legal background. For the law-school version, see Limits on Damages.

What it's about

Even when expectation damages are the goal (see Expectation Damages (Plain English)), courts apply limits:

1. Foreseeability

You can recover only losses that, at the time of contracting, the breaching side had reason to foresee as a probable result:

  • losses that happen in the ordinary course of things, or
  • special losses that arise from circumstances the breaching side was told about or had reason to know.

2. Certainty

Damages must be proved with reasonable certainty. Lost profits for a new, untested business are often too speculative.

3. Mitigation (avoidable losses)

You can't recover losses you could have reasonably avoided after the breach. Don't keep building after the other side cancels, and do look for a comparable job if you're fired.

4. Usually not recoverable

  • Emotional distress: except where serious emotional harm was a particularly likely result (contracts about funerals, for example).
  • Attorney's fees: under the "American rule," each side pays its own unless a contract or statute says otherwise.
  • Punitive damages: not available for breach of contract alone.
  • Contracts may also cap or exclude certain damages.

An everyday example

You send your laptop for repair and the shop loses it for a week. You can recover for the laptop and a rental. You probably can't recover for a $1 million deal you missed, unless you told the shop at the time that the laptop held the deal and every hour mattered.

The cases, in plain words

  • Hadley v. Baxendale: a carrier delayed a mill's broken shaft, but it wasn't told the mill would sit idle, so the mill couldn't recover lost profits. This is the foundation of foreseeability.
  • Florafax v. GTE Market Resources: lost profits were recoverable because they were foreseeable and provable.
  • Wartzman v. Hightower Productions: a flagpole-sitting stunt's profits were too speculative, so the promoters got their wasted investment instead.
  • Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge and Maness v. Collins: the duty to mitigate.
  • Erlich v. Menezes: owners of a leaky "dream house" got repair costs but not emotional distress damages.
  • Zapata Hermanos v. Hearthside Baking: the winning side couldn't recover its attorney's fees.
  • Princess Cruises v. General Electric: a contract term limiting liability cut a $4.58 million verdict to $231,925.

Words you'll hear

  • Consequential damages: losses caused indirectly, such as lost profits from a stalled business.
  • Mitigation: reasonable steps to reduce your losses.
  • American rule: each side pays its own lawyers.
