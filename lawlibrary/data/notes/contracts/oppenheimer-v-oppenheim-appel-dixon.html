Quick reference

Citation: 86 N.Y.2d 685, 636 N.Y.S.2d 734, 660 N.E.2d 415 (1995) · Casebook: Knapp p. 827 · Topic: Express Conditions › Strict compliance One line: An express condition (written notice of the landlord's consent by February 25) must be strictly satisfied. Substantial performance (telephone notice) does not count, so a $1.2 million verdict was overturned.

Overview

The leading modern case on the strict enforcement of express conditions. The substantial performance doctrine of Jacob and Youngs v. Kent does not apply to express conditions.

Parties

  • Plaintiff / respondent: Oppenheimer & Co. A tenant moving out of a suite in One New York Plaza. It wanted to sublease the old space and sued for damages when the subtenant backed out.
  • Defendant / appellant: Oppenheim, Appel, Dixon & Co. An accounting firm already in the building. It agreed to sublease, subject to conditions, then called the deal off.

Facts

  • The parties signed a letter agreement for the sublease, which was to be executed only if two conditions were met:
    1. the landlord's written consent to the sublease (obtained, with timely written notice); and
    2. the defendant's receipt, on or before a stated date (January 30, extended in writing to February 25), of written notice of the landlord's consent to construction work linking the defendant's existing and new spaces (telephone communications).
  • On February 25, the plaintiff's attorney told the defendant's attorney by telephone that the landlord had consented. The defendant's attorney said he would "get back to him."
  • The next day, the defendant declared the deal off: written notice had not been timely delivered, and no extension would be given.

Procedural History

  • Jury: the defendant had not waived the deadline and was not estopped, but the plaintiff had "substantially performed" the conditions. The verdict was $1.2 million.
  • Trial court: judgment n.o.v. for the defendant, because substantial performance is irrelevant to an express condition.
  • Appellate Division: reversed and reinstated the verdict.
  • Court of Appeals: reversed again, reinstating judgment for the defendant.

Issue

Can an express condition precedent (timely written notice) be satisfied by substantial performance?

Holding

No. Express conditions must be literally performed. Substantial performance does not satisfy them. Because the written-notice condition was not met by February 25, the defendant's duty never arose.

Reasoning

  • Express vs. constructive conditions: express conditions are those agreed to by the parties. Constructive conditions are imposed by law to do justice. Substantial performance applies to constructive conditions (as in Jacob & Youngs), to avoid forfeiture.
  • Parties are free to make any event a condition. When they clearly do, courts enforce it as written, honoring freedom of contract even if the result seems harsh.
  • The forfeiture concern does not apply here: the plaintiff did not lose any performance it had rendered, since it simply didn't get the sublease (no reliance or partial performance was lost).
  • The court did not squarely address waiver or estoppel, which the jury found were absent.

Rule

An express condition must be strictly (literally) satisfied. Substantial performance does not apply to express conditions, though doctrines such as waiver, estoppel and excuse to avoid disproportionate forfeiture (Restatement § 229) may still relieve a party.

Why It Matters

  • Note 1: courts require clear language to create an express condition. Restatement § 227: a doubtful term should be read as a promise, not a condition, if a condition would increase the risk of forfeiture. Oppenheimer's language was clear ("conditions," "on or before"), though it could have been stronger (e.g., "on condition that").
  • Note 2: conditions and promises differ, and a term can be both. Three readings of the Oppenheimer consents: (1) a promise to obtain them; (2) a promise to try; (3) no promise, merely conditions.
  • Note 3: Maxton Builders v. Lo Galbo: late written notice failed an express condition, though the other party knew.
  • Note 4: pay-if-paid clauses create express conditions. Pay-when-paid clauses are only timing mechanisms.

Other Key Points

  • Restatement § 224: a condition is "an event, not certain to occur, which must occur, unless its non-occurrence is excused, before performance under a contract becomes due."
  • Related: JNA Realty v. Cross Bay Chelsea (excuse to avoid forfeiture).
